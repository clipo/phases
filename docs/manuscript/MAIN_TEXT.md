# What Are Phases? Geographic Structure, Neutral Copying, and the Search for Social Boundaries in the Lower Mississippi Valley

**Carl P. Lipo, Beau DiNapoli, and Mark E. Madsen**

## Abstract

Culture historians built phases to describe continuous spatial and temporal variation in artifact classes. Yet, researchers often treat phases as social units and communities, a practice that continues today. We ask why phases look like groups of people, and whether the St. Francis basin phases of the Lower Mississippi Valley are more than groupings of nearby sites. 28 time-averaged decorated-ceramic assemblages, clustered on site coordinates alone, recover Phillips's phases for all but two locations. A spatial field model assigns 93 percent of the between-site variance in its latent log-ratio composition to a smooth geographic surface, and the phase boundaries run through the least-occupied ground between settlement clusters. The difference between the basin's two spatial clusters is what neutral copying at matched diversity can produce, though most settings produce less. Variability in rim attributes tabulated separately on 19 sites exceeds the expectations for spatial distance but shows no shared pattern with the decoration. We conclude that the two models built to detect bounded units do not reliably structure the data, plausibly because of the settlement gaps. Thus, phases are real as a geographic pattern, not resolved as bounded units, and not demonstrated as social units.

**Keywords:** cultural transmission, neutral models, isolation by distance, phases, Mississippian archaeology, Parkin phase

## Resumen

Los historiadores culturales construyeron las fases para describir la variación espacial y temporal continua de las clases de artefactos, pero las interpretaron como unidades sociales y las trataron como comunidades, una práctica que continúa hoy. Preguntamos por qué las fases parecen grupos de personas y si las fases de la cuenca del río St. Francis, en el valle inferior del Misisipi, son algo más que agrupaciones de sitios cercanos. En 28 conjuntos de cerámica decorada promediados en el tiempo, el agrupamiento basado solo en las coordenadas de los sitios recupera las fases de Phillips para todos salvo dos, un modelo de campo espacial asigna a una superficie geográfica suave el 93 por ciento de la varianza entre sitios de su composición latente en razones logarítmicas, y los límites de las fases atraviesan el terreno menos ocupado entre las concentraciones de asentamientos. La copia neutral con diversidad equiparada puede producir una diferencia como la observada entre las dos agrupaciones espaciales de la cuenca, aunque la mayoría de sus configuraciones producen menos. Tres clases decoradas comunes varían entre sitios mucho más de lo que producen las configuraciones neutrales evaluadas, con o sin deriva regional. El número de sitios con clases raras cae en su mayoría dentro de las bandas neutrales. Los atributos de los bordes, tabulados por separado en 19 sitios, también exceden las bandas y no muestran un patrón compartido con la decoración. Dos modelos construidos para detectar unidades delimitadas no recuperan de manera fiable las unidades simuladas en estos sitios, posiblemente por los vacíos de asentamiento. Las fases son reales como patrón geográfico, no quedan resueltas como unidades delimitadas y no están demostradas como unidades sociales.

**Palabras clave:** transmisión cultural, modelos neutrales, aislamiento por distancia, fases, arqueología misisipiana, fase Parkin

## Introduction

Phases are the units in which much of the North American archaeological record is organized. Willey and Phillips [-@willey_phillips_1958, 22] defined the phase as "the practicable and intelligible unit of archaeological study" formed by a set of traits distinctive enough to separate it from other units, confined to a locality or region and a brief interval of time. Nothing in that definition is social. Pottery types and the other artifact classes that provide the empirical foundation for a phase vary continuously across space and through time, and the researchers who built phases knew they were dividing that variability into units for description. However, the units are often interpreted as social groups. Willey and Phillips [-@willey_phillips_1958, 48-50] set out the chain of reasoning that equates component with community and phase with society, conceded that "in practice it does not work," and then proposed that archaeologists "may act as if" phases had social reality. In the Lower Mississippi River Valley, the survey phases were built "to elucidate sociopolitical boundaries" [@haley_2014, 41], and Williams, defining the southeast-Missouri phases, wrote that "in most cases, phase probably equals 'society'" [@williams_1954, 25; quoted in @fox_1998, 33]. In this way, a descriptive unit came to be treated as a people.

The step leading to a social interpretation of phases depends on a premise that is rarely stated. The premise holds that assemblages more similar to one another than to their neighbors were made by a bounded set of people who interacted mainly with each other. Based on that premise, a phase defined by a single class of artifacts becomes the basis of a hypothesis about social interaction. It remains a hypothesis until independent evidence from other artifact classes, ancient DNA, linguistics, or documented history tests it. Pairing a phase with a dominant center site strengthens the premise. A grouping of similar deposits that includes a much larger one becomes the basis of inferred polity before independent evidence for its social boundaries has been evaluated [@fox_1998]. This logic follows the expectation that a ranked society leaves a hierarchy of settlement types with its center at the top [@peebles_kus_1977, 431].

Why, then, do phases appear to be groups of people? When culture historians examined the record, they saw groups of nearby deposits whose assemblages resembled one another more than they resembled assemblages elsewhere. That observation matched their expectations, and the phases they drew documented those patterns. Earlier critiques have shown that the groups are not sharply bounded. Fox [-@fox_1998] used cluster analysis and ordination to show that the southeast-Missouri phases fail as both groups and classes, with member assemblages falling into statistical clusters at random rather than by phase. Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003, 180] found "little evidence for the integrity of the traditional late period phases" of the Lower Mississippi Valley, even though "most sites assigned to most phases are located in the general vicinity of each other in ordination space." Lipo [-@lipo_2001, 60] suggested through frequency seriation that "the phases themselves might be simply arbitrary spatial and temporal slices of a single interacting population that is much larger in size." These studies show that the phases do not separate completely. They leave open why the phases appeared to form coherent groups in the first place, and what those groups are.

Similarity alone cannot answer that question, for structural rather than statistical reasons. Phillips [-@phillips_1970] drew the phases tested here by sorting assemblages on their proportions of decorated pottery types and on their proximity. A scheme recovered from the same frequencies and the same map records how the scheme was drawn, not how its units behaved [@willey_phillips_1958, 27-28]. Ordination arranges assemblages by resemblance. It cannot distinguish a bounded learning community from copying that declines gradually with distance, because both produce neighbors that resemble each other.

The Lower Mississippi Valley phases are a well-documented case. Its late pre-contact phases are well defined, long established, and in active use. Regional phases include Kent [@house_1991], Nodena [@morse_d_1990], Parchman [@starr_1984], Tipton, Walls, and Jones Bayou [@smith_1990], and Parkin [@morse_1990]. Figure 1 shows four of them, Nodena, Parkin, Walls, and Kent, as Phillips [-@phillips_1970] drew them. The Parkin phase contains the Parkin site, the largest moated and palisaded town in the St. Francis basin of northeastern Arkansas. Reconstructions of the Hernando de Soto expedition's route identify Parkin as Casqui, the seat of a paramount chief the expedition met in 1541 [@morse_1990, 125-129; @hudson_smith_depratter_1990; @mitchem_2017]. Because of its size, fortification, platform mound, and ethnohistoric identification, Parkin is routinely interpreted as the center of a Mississippian polity. Extending that interpretation from the town to the phase makes a further claim: that the sites assigned to the Parkin phase belonged to a bounded, hierarchically organized society headed by the Parkin site.

We ask three questions of the St. Francis basin phases. First, why do culture historians see phases in these data? Second, is there evidence of social boundaries between the phases beyond the geographic distribution of settlement along the basin's rivers? That evidence is what would support the culture historians' claim. Third, what would it take to detect such boundaries in archaeological deposits like these? The data that form the basis for phases are surface-collected decorated-ceramic counts of Phillips, Ford, and Griffin [-@phillips_ford_griffin_1951], compiled by Lipo [-@lipo_2001]. We analyze them with one spatial model, one neutral copying model as the standard of comparison, and two models built to detect bounded units. These analyses concern how ceramic producers learned and chose decorative elements for pottery. In this study, a bounded learning community means a population in which copying was more common internally and less common across its edge than distance alone would predict.

## Distance-dependent copying and clumped settlement

Cultural transmission theory offers a way to explain patterns in pottery decoration through how people learn. While individuals are free to experiment, much pottery practice is learned from other potters, so the designs they make preserve evidence of those connections. In a neutral model, no decorative variant has an advantage in being copied. New variants arise, and their proportions change by chance as a finite population of potters copies existing designs, a process called neutral drift [@dunnell_1978; @neiman_1995; @bentley_hahn_shennan_2004; @eerkens_lipo_2005; @eerkens_lipo_2007; @fogarty_kandler_creanza_2024]. This conceptual model has three consequences for an archaeological record like this one.

The effects of copying generally decline with distance. People learn from those they live near and see often, so a potter is more likely to copy a neighbor than someone further away. Under neutral drift, this tendency makes nearby assemblages more alike than distant ones, with similarity declining gradually across the landscape. Population geneticists call the pattern isolation by distance [@wright_1943], and it has long been recognized in stylistic frequencies [@dunnell_1970; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_hunt_1997; @neiman_1995; @shennan_crema_kerig_2015]. It arises wherever interaction is mainly local. It also produces between-place differences without any boundary, because small or poorly connected places drift toward idiosyncratic combinations of stylistic features [@lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021].

However, communities rarely settle evenly across a landscape and are often clumped. People did not live in locations that are evenly spaced across the St. Francis basin. Settlements concentrate on particular reaches of the basin's rivers, with less-occupied stretches between them. Any gradual change in style is therefore sampled unevenly, densely in some places and sparsely or not at all in others.

Gradual variation sampled at settlement concentrations can resemble a set of distinct units. Seriation and ordination methods sort assemblages by resemblance, and the culture historians who drew the phases also sorted them by proximity. When styles change gradually across clumped settlements, both criteria group the assemblages of each concentration together and place boundaries in the gaps. Units produced this way appear distinct even when the copying process has no edge [@lipo_madsen_dunnell_hunt_1997; @lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021]. This reflects the difference between typological and population interpretations [@mayr_1959; @dunnell_1980]. Interpreted typologically, a cluster of similar assemblages is a unit with members and an edge. Interpreted as a population, the same cluster is a sampled portion of a larger learning network. Figure 2 illustrates the problem: one set of sampled values is drawn beside a gradient and beside a step at the gap. No assemblage comes from the stretch where a gradient predicts intermediate values and a step does not.

Identifying a bounded learning community therefore requires more than clustering. Mixing erodes differences between populations [@henrich_boyd_1998, 219-220], so restricting copying across an edge should increase differences between communities beyond what distance alone produces. Whether such a restriction is detectable depends on its strength and duration, site location, and the record's resolution.

## Materials and methods

### Data

We use decorated-class counts from the Lower Mississippi Valley Survey collections of Phillips, Ford, and Griffin [-@phillips_ford_griffin_1951], compiled by Lipo [-@lipo_2001]. We focus on decorated classes as candidates for neutral stylistic variation, designs whose frequencies may change through copying and chance without affecting how a pot performs [@dunnell_1978; @neiman_1995]. Decoration of Mississippian vessels in this area tends to be only on the rim, neck, and shoulder areas. As a result, assemblages include many plain body and base sherds. Variation in plain sherds therefore reflects differences in paste and temper that can indicate firing conditions and vessel function [@feathers_2006; @feathers_and_peacock_2008]. Combining plain and decorated wares would also introduce variation in the degree to which the vessels were decorated. Ten decorated classes remain after pooling. Following Lipo [-@lipo_2001], we pool sherds from Barton Incised, Kent Incised, and Mound Place Incised, and we pool the painted types Old Town Red, Nodena Red and White, and Avenue Polychrome. This pooling lets us include smaller sherds that often have only portions of the attributes needed to identify these types, increasing overall sample sizes and ensuring comparability. Each class is a culture-history type, a unit of measurement built by analysts, not a category assumed to have existed in the past.

An assemblage is a member of the phase of the area it lies in, using Phillips' phase areas [-@phillips_1970] as Lipo [-@lipo_2001, Figure 2.6] mapped them (Figure 1). Two assemblages (Cramor Place and Holden Lake) fall outside all defined phase areas and are assigned to the nearest phase. We include only assemblages with at least 75 decorated sherds, leaving the Nodena phase with a single assemblage, which we exclude. These rules leave 28 assemblages with 14,101 decorated sherds (median 251 per assemblage; range 79 to 1,709) across three phases: Parkin (12 assemblages), Kent (11), and Walls (5). Of the 28, 18 are the 1940 survey collections alone, nine combine the 1940 collection with controlled surface collections Lipo made at the same site in 1996 and 1997, and one, Holden Lake, is Lipo's alone [@lipo_2001]. We located each assemblage using the Lower Mississippi Survey site files, and we drew settlement attributes from a regional database [@lipo_dunnell_2007]. Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003] compiled a separate database from largely the same survey files with revised phase assignments. Because its counts overlap ours, we analyze it separately as a second case in the Supplemental Text.

These assemblages are time-averaged surface collections that are built up over years of use and discard. Thus, we treat them as the accumulated product of copying across a population rather than as degraded samples of a single moment [@schiffer_1972; @dunnell_1995; @neiman_1995; @holdaway_wandsnider_2008; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_2015; @perreault_2019]. Time-averaging reduces differences between assemblages and distorts measures of diversity and neutrality in proportion to how long variants remained in use [@madsen_2012; @premo_2014; @miller_atkins_premo_2018]. A neutral model also describes people copying one another, whereas an assemblage counts durable objects, so its expectations apply only approximately [@crema_bortolini_lake_2023].

Radiocarbon determinations compiled by Mainfort [-@mainfort_2001] match just four of the 28 assemblages. Their calibrated medians, each computed by pooling that site's calibrated densities, are AD 1466 at Kent Place, 1483 at Parkin, 1513 at Neeleys Ferry, and 1596 at Clay Hill. Hollywood has two calibrated one-sigma ranges reaching back to AD 1285 [@haley_2014; @house_1993, 23]. On that evidence, the late pre-contact occupation spans roughly the fourteenth through the early seventeenth centuries, and the sequence includes communities affected by European contact and the demographic collapse that followed [@morse_morse_1996; @lipo_2001]. The deposits overlap temporally, and each accumulated over time. Every analysis below therefore treats the record as a time-averaged spatial sample, and the neutral simulations it is compared against are built to the same condition.

### Standard of evidence and analyses

Our standard of evidence for identifying a social unit includes signatures from independent sources of information that converge on the same boundaries. This is an evidential criterion, not a derived result. It assumes that the same learners, connected by the same networks, carried the traits being compared. Neither the cited models nor our simulations derive what a boundary must produce across different trait systems. A single signature can be produced by several processes [@crema_kandler_shennan_2016]. 

Deterministic seriations are built from the same frequencies that drew the phases, so their structure cannot test them. Tests of departure from neutral copying are distorted by time-averaging of surface collections at this resolution [@madsen_2012; @premo_2014]. This leaves us with two possible signatures. The first is the spatial pattern of the decorated classes, compared with what distance-dependent copying produces. The second is the rim attributes, the one trait system in these collections that was recorded separately from the decorated types. Table 1 lists the analyses by the question each answers.

The analyses that Table 1 marks as posterior use Bayesian methods [@gelman_etal_2013; @otarola_castillo_torquato_2018]. A Bayesian analysis begins with a prior distribution, which states the plausible values of each unknown quantity before the data are considered. It combines the prior with the data to produce a posterior distribution, which states how plausible each value is given both. Three properties of the record and of our questions make this approach suitable. First, the record is small and uneven. The 28 assemblages range from 79 to 1,709 decorated sherds, and some classes are rare. The proportions calculated from the counts are therefore uncertain, and more uncertain at some sites than at others [@robertson_1999]. A posterior carries that uncertainty into every quantity calculated from it, whereas a calculation from the raw proportions treats a class's share of 79 sherds as being as well known as its share of 1,709. Second, our questions concern magnitudes: how much of the variation between sites follows location, and how different two sites a given distance apart are expected to be. A posterior answers them with a range of supported values and does not reduce them to accepting or rejecting a null hypothesis [@wasserstein_lazar_2016]. Third, 28 sites may not be enough to resolve some of these quantities. When they are not, the posterior remains wide or close to its prior, which directly shows the limit of the record. Because results can depend on the priors, we state each one. For the three models in Table 1 (A0, A1, and A3), we also refit under alternative priors and test whether each model recovers known values from simulated records [@gelman_etal_2020].

### The spatial field model

Our first analysis describes how pottery composition varies with geography without first dividing the sites into groups. To do this, we fit a Bayesian spatial field model, A0, to the counts of the ten decorated classes in each of the 28 assemblages. In the model, each assemblage is a multinomial sample, the distribution that describes sherds drawn at random from a site where the ten classes occur in fixed but unknown proportions. We do not estimate those proportions independently for each site. Each is built from two parts. The first is a baseline for the class, which is the same at every site and reflects how common the class is across the basin. The second is a site-specific adjustment. Addition occurs on the log-odds scale, which is the logarithm of a class's proportion divided by the reference class's proportion, and the softmax function converts the ten log-odds into positive proportions that sum to one. One class serves as the reference, with its log-odds fixed at zero, and the model estimates the other nine classes relative to it.

Geography enters the model through site-specific adjustments. For each of the nine non-reference classes, the adjustments across the 28 sites are modeled as a Gaussian process: a smooth surface is placed over the map, with nearby sites taking similar values and distant sites taking nearly independent values. An exponential covariance sets how quickly that similarity declines. The correlation between two sites falls as exp(-d/rho), where d is the straight-line distance between them, and rho is the range, a distance in kilometers that governs how far the resemblance extends. A second parameter, the field scale sigma, sets how large the geographic adjustments can be. Each site also receives an adjustment with no spatial pattern, independent from site to site. Its scale, tau, sets how much a site can differ from its neighbors for reasons unrelated to location. All nine class fields share one sigma, one rho, and one tau. Comparing sigma with tau is the purpose of the model. A record organized by location places most of its between-site variation in the surface, whose scale is sigma. A record in which each site has its own character regardless of its neighbors places it in the site-level term, whose scale is tau.

We gave sigma and tau the same half-Normal(0, 2) prior, so that neither source of variation is favored before the data are seen. A half-Normal(0, 2) distribution is a Normal distribution with mean zero and standard deviation 2, restricted to positive values. It places half its probability below 1.35 and 95 percent below 3.9 on the log-odds scale. For the range rho, we used a prior proportional to 1/rho over 0.5 to 300 km. This prior gives equal probability to each multiplicative distance interval within that window, so it expresses no preference between a range of a few kilometers and one longer than the basin. On the class baselines, we placed a Normal(0, 10) prior, which on the log-odds scale is wide enough to let a rare class be very rare. A narrower prior on the baselines can shift part of a rare class's rarity into the field's basin-wide level. We therefore refit the model under two alternatives, a Normal(0, 2) prior and a Normal prior centered on each class's log-odds in the pooled counts of the whole basin, and report all three. We wrote the model in Stan and fitted it by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo [@carpenter_etal_2017]. The posterior of a model of this kind has no formula that can be evaluated directly. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo is a sampler, an algorithm that instead generates a long sequence of parameter values, called draws, whose distribution approximates the posterior. Each independent run of the sampler from its own random starting values is a chain. By default, we ran four chains of 3,000 iterations each. The first 1,500 iterations of each chain are warmup, during which the sampler adjusts its settings, and they are discarded. That leaves 6,000 draws.

We focus on two quantities from the model. The first is the contrast-based spatial share. A class's field has a basin-wide average level that the data cannot separate from the class baseline. We remove that level and take the variance of what remains, the part of the field that makes sites differ from one another. The share is that remaining variance as a fraction of itself plus the site-level variance tau squared, on the model's latent log-ratio scale. A share near one means nearly all the between-site variance the model finds on that scale lies in the smooth surface, and a share near zero means sites differ in ways location does not describe. The share partitions expected latent variance in log-ratios to the reference class, not variance explained in the observed compositions. The second quantity is the standard deviation of the contrast between two sites a stated distance apart, on the log-odds scale. It states how different, under the field alone, two sites that far apart are expected to be in a class's log-odds. We evaluate it at the median spacing between nearest neighbors, the median distance between site pairs, and the largest distance between any two sites in the sample.

### The neutral copying model

To state what distance-dependent copying predicts, we use the finite-population network model of Lipo, DiNapoli, Madsen, and Hunt [-@lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021] on the real site map. Each of the 28 sites has a finite population of learners who copy decorated classes mostly within their site and, otherwise, from other sites with a probability that falls with straight-line distance. New variants appear at a constant rate in proportion to each class's share of the regional pool. Each simulated assemblage accumulates over a span with a random start and a random length, independent of location and phase. Span lengths are sized by the simulator's measured decorrelation length, and each simulated assemblage is subsampled to the real assemblage's sherd count. The model places no restriction on copying between any two sites.

The model has four parameters. The number of learners at each site sets how fast chance alone changes class frequencies, since a small population drifts faster than a large one. Innovation is the probability that a learner's class in a given step is drawn from the regional pool rather than copied from another learner. Mixing is the probability that a copy comes from another site rather than from the learner's own. Interaction length is the distance over which the probability of copying from a particular other site declines. The form of that decline with distance is the kernel.

We do not estimate these parameters from the record, and we do not tune the simulated assemblages to the between-site statistics the comparisons test, except as stated below. A model tuned to those statistics would reproduce the record by construction. Instead, we screen a grid of combinations of kernel shape, interaction length, population size, innovation rate, and mixing rate, and keep those whose simulated assemblages match three diversity summaries, each within 10 percent of the record's value. Two are local: the mean Gini-Simpson diversity, the probability that two sherds drawn from the same assemblage belong to different classes, and the mean number of classes present in an assemblage. The third is regional and consists of the Gini-Simpson diversity of the 28 pooled assemblages. Pooled diversity compared with local diversity carries information about dispersion between sites, so these summaries are not free of between-site information, although none of them is a statistic the later comparisons test.

An operating point is one combination of parameter values at which the model is run for the comparisons. From the matched combinations we retain two operating points in two stages. The screen, at six seeds per combination, supplies the candidates. We retain matched combinations whose median two-cluster cultural F_ST (defined in the next section) lies within 50 percent of the observed value. Each candidate is then rerun with 50 fresh seeds and scored by the sum of squared relative errors over the three diversity summaries and the F_ST median. The primary point has the smallest such loss among candidates that still match at 50 seeds. The secondary has the smallest loss among those with a different kernel family and a different population size. The between-cluster F_ST therefore entered the choice of operating point, as a filter and as a term in the loss. The comparison of the record's two-cluster F_ST against the operating points in the Results is interpreted with that in mind. The Supplemental Text gives the same comparison across every diversity-matched combination of the screen without that selection. The per-class, drift, marker, and rim statistics were not selection criteria, and their results remain conditional on the selected operating points.

Some predictions depend on a parameter the diversity match leaves open, for example, the window of time over which the assemblages' accumulation spans are spread or the amplitude of regional drift, both described in the next section. Such a parameter is not set to a single value. It is swept over a grid, and the record's position relative to the model is reported at every point on that grid, so readers can see whether a conclusion holds across the range or only at one setting.

For each statistic, we generate 50 simulated records at an operating point and compute the statistic on each. The 50 values show the range the statistic takes under neutral copying alone at that setting. We summarize them by their median and by the neutral band, the interval from their 10th to their 90th percentile, which contains the middle 80 percent of the simulated values. An observed value inside the band is one that neutral copying commonly produces at that operating point. About one simulated record in ten exceeds the band's upper limit, and about one in ten falls below its lower limit. Some statistics are therefore expected to fall outside the band under neutral copying alone. The marker analysis described below uses the neutral records' own count outside their band, near 10 of 50, as the baseline against which a detectable departure is measured. We classify the record as inside the band, outside it, or unavailable. It is unavailable when too few of the simulated records yield a finite value of the statistic for a band to be formed. Only an observed value above the band is interpreted as more variation than neutral copying produces. Restricted copying, if it occurred, would increase differences between sites, and a record that varies less than the model does not indicate it. Every such interpretation is conditional on the generator and its operating point. A different generator or setting could predict differently, which is why the swept parameters are reported at every point.

### Statistics compared with the neutral model

Cultural F_ST [@wright_1951; @bell_richerson_mcelreath_2009] measures the share of decorated diversity, by the Gini-Simpson index, that lies between groups rather than within them, in Nei's [-@nei_1973] form. We report it between the two spatial clusters the site layout supports, with a neutral band in each of 30 cells. The cells cross the two operating points with three sweeps. The first is occupation spread, where we vary the window over which span endpoints are drawn while keeping individual span lengths unchanged. The second is occupation spread with collection sizes assigned in rank order of span length. The third is regional drift. For each decorated class, we compute a site-weighted moment estimate of F: the between-site variance of the class's share, with binomial sampling variance removed, divided by its mean share times one minus that share. For the three common classes (Parkin Punctated, the pooled Barton, Kent, and Mound Place Incised, and the pooled painted types), the statistic is F. For the seven rare classes, it is the number of the 28 sites where the class is present, because F is poorly determined for a class present at few sites. In the model, neutral copying implies one population-level F shared by every class. Estimated F in finite samples still differs among classes, so we compare each class with its own simulated band.

Change over time is a second source of differences between sites that does not involve boundaries. Assemblages that accumulated at different times sampled a regional pool of designs that changed over the interval. Regional drift, the rise and fall of class frequencies across the whole region through copying and chance, is the documented form of that change in these data. As described so far, the simulator holds the regional pool from which innovations are drawn fixed. To let that pool drift, after each copying step we redraw it from a Dirichlet distribution centered on its current composition. A Dirichlet distribution is a probability distribution over sets of proportions that sum to one, so each draw is a completely new set of class shares that differs from the current set by a random amount. Each class's expected share is unchanged by the redraw, and the size of the random step matches the mean and variance of one generation of a Wright-Fisher population, the standard model of change by chance alone. The occupation window is the stretch of simulated time from which the assemblage spans are drawn. We set that window to $W = 8 L_R$, eight times the number of simulation steps over which the regional composition decorrelates. We quantify drift by its accumulated effect. The amplitude $F_W$ is the regional F accumulated over the window. It measures how far the pool at the end of the window has drifted from the pool at its start, on the same scale as the between-site F reported for the classes. It is 0 when the pool is fixed, and we run it at 0, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, and 0.3, the last larger than any between-site F the record shows.

To measure what size of departure the comparison can detect, we add a marker to simulated records: a known change in one class in one of the two spatial clusters the site layout supports. The marker multiplies the class odds by 1.5, 2, or 3 at every site in the cluster before that site's sherds are drawn. A class with a share of 20 percent at a site thus becomes 27 percent at a multiplier of 1.5, 33 percent at 2, and 43 percent at 3. Everything else in a marker record is identical to the neutral record it was made from. For each marker and each drift amplitude, we count how many of the 50 marker records have the class's statistic outside the neutral band for that class and amplitude. We report that count beside the number of neutral records that fall outside their own band, which is near 10 of 50 by construction because the band spans the 10th to the 90th percentile. A marker the comparison can detect raises the count well above that baseline, and one it cannot detect leaves the count near it.

### Composition by collection

Nine of the 28 rows combine the 1940 survey collection with Lipo's 1996 to 1997 collections at the same site. To ask whether merging collections produces the per-class pattern, we decompose each row into its parts and recompute the per-class statistics on the 1940 material alone, matched on the 27 sites whose 1940 part has at least 75 sherds. At each mixed site, we report the log odds ratio of each class between the two collections. Its 90 percent interval is computed under independent multinomial sampling of each part and a Dirichlet(1/2) prior, and the medians under Dirichlet(0.1) and Dirichlet(1) are reported as prior sensitivities.

### Rim attributes

The rim attributes serve two purposes. They are a second line of evidence, a trait system recorded separately from the decorated types, against which the pattern in the decorated classes can be compared. They also change the scale of measurement. A decorated class is a type, defined by a combination of attributes, and its frequency records only whether sherds meet the whole definition [@dunnell_1971]. An attribute such as interior beveling or lip form is a single choice a potter makes during manufacture. It is therefore closer in scale to the cultural trait, the unit that one potter learns from another and can modify, than a type is. A type records several such units as a package and measures transmission at that coarser scale [@obrien_etal_2010]. Attributes of the same artifacts can have different transmission histories [@bettinger_eerkens_1999; @eerkens_lipo_2005]. Ethnographic work also shows that different steps in making a pot spread through different social networks, and that some steps follow the networks in which potters learned more closely than decoration does [@gosselain_2000]. Frequencies of single attributes may therefore register differences in learning between communities, and so boundaries, that type frequencies average out. Whether they do so in this record is an empirical question.

Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003_rims, Table 1] published counts of six rim attributes on 7,195 rims from 55 late-period assemblages, tabulated separately from the decorated types and by one observer, including the interior beveling and exterior notching used here. Lip form and paste are not in that paper. They come from unpublished data that Mainfort shared with us, which gives them as percentages that we converted to counts using the published rim totals. Nineteen of our 28 assemblages meet this threshold, with at least 25 rims, for a total of 2,065 rims. One of the 19 falls below Mainfort's own minimum of 45. We use three attributes that we interpret as not tabulated as types in 1951: interior beveling, exterior notching, and lip form. We treat paste separately, because Bell Plain and Neeley's Ferry Plain are type-level distinctions of the 1951 typology and are not independent of how the phases were drawn. The paste statistic is Bell's share of all counted rims. Of the 2,065 rims, 641 are Bell and 874 Neeley's Ferry, and the remaining 550 (0 to 81 percent by site)  are in neither named paste. What those rims are remains unclear.

For each attribute, we compute the same moment F on the 19 sites against the same neutral model, with all 28 populations transmitting and the 19 rim sites observed, plus the three common decorated classes on the same 19 sites. We report each attribute's rank correlation with latitude and longitude and its pairwise differences with distance as posterior summaries. Each site's frequency of an attribute is given a Beta(1/2, 1/2) prior. A Beta distribution describes a single proportion between zero and one, and this one has the same effect as adding half a rim with the attribute and half a rim without it to each site's count. It keeps a site where the attribute was never observed, or always observed, from being treated as exactly 0 or 100 percent. The summaries are computed over 4,000 draws from the resulting posteriors. We repeat them under a Beta(1, 1) prior, which adds one rim of each kind, as a sensitivity check. We also report the rank correlation between rim and decoration differences across the 171 site pairs after linear adjustment for distance. Phillips [-@phillips_1970] did not define the phases on decorated types alone, so we describe the rims as a second trait system recorded by a second observer and do not call them independent of the phase definitions.

### Models for bounded units

The spatial field model describes composition as a smooth surface. Two further models ask whether the record is better described by that surface plus discrete units, each unit a set of sites that share a shift in composition beyond what their locations predict. Both keep everything in A0 and add one feature, so that A0 is recovered exactly when the added feature is set to zero.

The latent-unit model, A1, assigns each site to one of three units, the number of phases in the basin, without being told which site belongs to which. Each unit shifts the log-odds of every class at its member sites. The three units' shifts sum to zero within each class, so the units move composition relative to one another without changing the basin-wide baseline. A single scale, $\sigma_g$, governs the size of those shifts and has a half-Normal(0, 2) prior. Membership is unknown. A Dirichlet(1) prior on the frequencies of the three units is symmetric in their labels and uniform over those frequencies, which is not the same as uniform over divisions of the sites. The model sums over every possible assignment of sites to units rather than committing to one, so the posterior for $\sigma_g$ reflects every way of dividing the sites into three units, weighted by that prior and the data.

A3, the barrier model, fixes the division in advance by drawing an edge through the site map, and asks whether sites on the same side of the edge share a shift in composition beyond what the smooth surface predicts. Its barrier parameter $\omega$ is the standard deviation, in log-odds, of that shared shift, with a half-Normal(0, 2) prior, and $\omega = 0$ is A0 exactly. We placed the edge in two ways. The first is the two-cluster division of the sites by k-means on their coordinates, the same two spatial clusters used for the cultural F_ST, which separates eleven of the twelve Parkin-area sites from the other seventeen. The second is a straight line across the basin, chosen from 30 candidate lines as the one that best agrees with Phillips's Parkin-versus-rest labels, so this placement uses the phase labels. The same line also best matches the k-means division.

Each model was tested on simulated records with known parameter values before it could be fitted to the record. We simulated records from each model's own generator at the real 28 site locations and fitted the model to each simulated record. We set the true value of $\sigma_g$ to 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 for A1 and $\omega$ to 0, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 for A3. For A3, we repeated the fits with the field's range left free and with it pinned at 6 and 12 km, under both class-intercept priors. For A1, the fourteen recovery fits were run under the Normal(0, 2) prior, and three of them were repeated under Normal(0, 10). A model that recovers the true value returns a posterior centered near it that moves as the true value moves. One whose posterior stays near the prior's median of 1.35, whatever the true value, has learned little about that parameter from the data. We report posterior medians against true values, sampler diagnostics for every fit, and the share of fits that pass those diagnostics. A model that does not recover a known value on records from its own generator cannot be interpreted on the record, where the true value is unknown, so we report no estimate from it.

### Reporting conventions

Three kinds of numbers appear in the Results. Posterior quantities state what the record implies about a quantity once the uncertainty in its counts is carried through. Each is reported as its median with a 90 percent equal-tailed interval, the range from the 5th to the 95th percentile of the posterior draws, unless stated otherwise. Neutral comparisons place an observed statistic against the distribution of the same statistic over records simulated under neutral copying. The cultural $F_{ST}$ and the per-class F are computed directly from the counts, in the same way on the record and on each simulated record. They are reported as point values beside the neutral band defined above, not as a posterior interval. Descriptive statistics summarize the record or a set of divisions and carry no inferential claim.

Posterior intervals on proportions apply only where an analysis states one. For a decorated composition, each assemblage's proportions have a Dirichlet posterior, combining a multinomial likelihood for its counts with a Dirichlet(1/2) prior that adds half a sherd to every class. For a rim attribute, each site's frequency has a Beta posterior under a Beta(1/2, 1/2) prior. The statistic is recomputed on repeated draws from those posteriors, and its reported median and interval summarize that set of values.

Every model fitted in Stan is checked for whether the sampler explored the posterior before any of its output is used. The checks use five diagnostics [@vehtari_etal_2021]. R-hat compares variation between chains with variation within them. It equals 1 when the chains have converged to the same distribution, and it rises above 1 when they have not, in which case their draws cannot be combined as a single posterior sample. The effective sample size is the number of independent draws that would estimate a quantity as precisely as the chains do. It is smaller than the number of draws because successive draws in a chain resemble one another. The bulk effective sample size applies to the center of a posterior, and so to a median. The tail effective sample size applies to its extremes, and so to the limits of an interval. A divergent transition is an iteration at which the sampler's numerical approximation of its path failed. Divergent transitions indicate a region of the posterior that the sampler could not explore accurately, so the draws may underrepresent that region. The energy Bayesian fraction of missing information measures how freely the sampler moves between the dense center of the posterior and its sparse outer regions, and a low value means the outer regions were explored slowly. Tree depth is the length of the path the sampler follows in one iteration, which has a set maximum. Saturation is the share of iterations that reached that maximum, and it indicates inefficient sampling, not incorrect draws.

A fit passes when, for every parameter checked, R-hat is at most 1.01 and both the bulk and tail effective sample sizes are at least 400, no transition diverged, and the energy Bayesian fraction of missing information is at least 0.3. We report tree-depth saturation but do not fail a fit on it. A fit that fails at the default settings is rerun with longer chains and stricter sampler settings before it is judged. In the latent-unit model, the labels of the units are arbitrary and can differ from one chain to another, so parameters tied to a label are not checked. We require instead that the co-membership probabilities, the posterior probabilities that two sites belong to the same unit, agree across chains. A fit that fails is reported as failing, and its posterior is not used as an estimate. In the recovery tests, failed fits are shown with their draws marked as failed so that the denominator is visible. The Supplemental Text defines each diagnostic and includes prior and posterior predictive checks, prior sensitivity, and recovery of known values under each prior.

## Results

### Why did culture historians see phases in these data?

The phases are recoverable from the sites' spatial distribution alone. Ward linkage on the 28 assemblages' coordinates, scored on the 26 assemblages within a mapped phase area, agrees with Phillips's assignments at an adjusted Rand index of 0.740. The two assemblages it places with another phase, Big Eddy and Castile Landing, are the two in the narrow southern extension of the Parkin area, which it groups with Kent (Figure 3A). Divisions by phase size, built by allocating sites to random centers on the map, agree at a median of 0.485, a reference that preserves both group sizes and geographic allocation. Composition alone, under a chi-square transform that needs no treatment of zero counts, agrees at 0.358 and misplaces six of the 26. Adding composition to the coordinates does not raise the agreement above the map alone under Ward linkage. A k-means clustering on the coordinates reaches 0.480 and rises to 0.740 only when composition enters at a quarter of the coordinates' weight (Supplemental Text). A scheme divided by the map alone would be recovered from coordinates and would not improve as type frequency data were included, and these assemblages show that. This result describes how the scheme was drawn by researchers who sorted these collections by proximity and resemblance. It does not show how the scheme's units behaved.

Composition itself is organized by location. The spatial field model divides the differences between sites into a part that changes smoothly across the map and a part specific to each site (Methods). The share that lies in the smooth surface has a posterior median of 0.93, with a 90 percent interval from 0.78 to 1.00, under the Normal(0, 10) intercept prior. Under the two alternative priors, it is 0.93 (0.79 to 1.00) and 0.94 (0.78 to 1.00) (Figure 4). Nearly all of the between-site variation the model finds therefore follows location. This share is measured on the model's log-odds scale and is not a proportion of the variance in the observed frequencies (Methods).

The model also states how different two sites are expected to be at a given distance, and that difference increases with distance. We express it as the standard deviation of the difference between two sites in a class's log-odds, in logits. It is 0.89 logits (0.70 to 1.10) at the median nearest-neighbor spacing of 4.3 km, 2.37 (1.91 to 2.85) at the median pair distance of 36.6 km, and 3.12 (2.51 to 3.79) at the largest pair distance of 79.1 km. Exponentiating each median converts it to a ratio. A difference of one standard deviation means a class's odds relative to the reference class differ by about a factor of 2.4 between neighboring sites, about 11 at the median distance, and about 23 across the full extent of the sample. All three fits pass every diagnostic (largest R-hat 1.006, smallest bulk effective sample size 608, no divergent transitions, no iterations at maximum treedepth, smallest E-BFMI 0.67).

Two descriptive summaries that do not use the model show the same organization. Correspondence analysis arranges the assemblages along axes so assemblages with similar compositions lie close together, and its first axis captures the largest share of the differences among them. That axis tracks latitude (rank correlation +0.77; latitude and longitude together account for 63 percent of its variance). The two earliest dated assemblages share one end of the axis with the latest, so on the evidence of the four dated assemblages the axis is not an ordering by date. Phillips's areas also differ in pooled composition. Between the Parkin and Kent areas, the log-odds of Parkin Punctated in the pooled counts are 0.47 higher in Parkin. The pooled log-odds of Barton, Kent, and Mound Place Incised, and of the painted types, are 0.12 and 0.90 lower in Parkin.

Two parameters describe the smooth surface. Its scale, sigma, sets how large the differences across the map can be, and its range, rho, is the distance over which sites resemble one another (Methods). The 28 sites cannot determine these two values separately, because different pairs of values fit the record about equally well. All 28 sites lie within 79 km of one another. At those distances, a field with a large scale and a long range produces nearly the same contrasts between sites as a field with a smaller scale and a shorter range. These data constrain how different two sites are expected to be, not sigma and rho separately, which is why we report the share and the contrast. The share also does not by itself distinguish a smooth surface from social units aligned with geography. If units existed and their members lived near one another, each unit's shared composition would appear at 28 sampled sites as one region of a smooth surface, and the surface would account for it. A high share explains why phases appear to be groups. It leaves open whether anything beyond geography structures the record, which the models for bounded units address.

The phase boundaries cross the least-occupied ground. We reconstructed boundaries by assigning each point on the map to its nearest analyzed assemblage and merging territories by phase. We then measured the density of late pre-contact settlement along them, using the 53 sites in the regional compilation that lie more than a kilometer from any analyzed assemblage. At a 5 km bandwidth, density along the phase boundaries is 0.56 sites per 100 km², compared with 1.01 at the assemblages themselves. Of 300 divisions with phases centered randomly, 94 percent cross denser settlement than the phase boundaries do, as do 100 percent of 300 compact divisions (Figure 3B). At a 3 km bandwidth, the shares are 98 and 100 percent, and at 8 km they fall to 89 and 86 percent (Supplemental Text). Settlement concentrates along the larger rivers, and the boundaries lie in the stretches between the concentrations.

These three results answer the first question. Culture historians saw phases in these data because pottery composition is organized by location, settlement is clumped along the rivers, and sorting assemblages by proximity and resemblance places boundaries in the gaps. None of this restricts who copied whom.

### Is there evidence of social boundaries beyond geography?

The archaeological record of the St. Francis basin does not allow us to determine the neutral model's four parameters. Many combinations of population size, innovation rate, mixing rate, and interaction length produce simulated records with the same diversity as the 28 time-averaged assemblages. Of the combinations we screened, 498 match all three diversity summaries (Methods), and the record gives no basis for choosing among them. We therefore carry two of them, the operating points, through every comparison. Each has a loss, the measure of mismatch with the record defined in Methods, for which a smaller value is a closer match. The primary operating point has a loss of 0.108. Its kernel is Gaussian, a curve along which the probability of copying from another site declines slowly at short distances and steeply beyond them, with an interaction length of 6 km. Each site has 2,000 learners; the innovation rate is 0.002, and the mixing rate is 0.4. The secondary point has a loss of 0.148. Its kernel is exponential, declining most steeply at short distances and more gradually beyond them, with an interaction length of 6 km. It has 120 learners per site, an innovation rate of 0.024, and the same mixing rate of 0.4. The two points thus differ in kernel shape, in population size by a factor of about 17, and in innovation rate by a factor of 12, while matching the same diversity summaries. A result that holds at both does not depend on those three choices. It can still depend on the interaction length and the mixing rate, which are the same at both points.

The difference between the two spatial clusters is small. The between-cluster cultural F_ST is 0.0062, meaning that about 0.6% of the decorated diversity lies between the clusters and the rest lies within them. Neutral copying can produce a difference this large, but only under some combinations of settings. 

We compared the observed value with the neutral band in 30 cells. The neutral band is the interval containing the middle 80 percent of values simulated under neutral copying at one setting (Methods). A cell is one operating point at one setting of occupation spread or regional drift (Methods). The observed value lies inside the band in 17 of the 30 cells, and in 7 of the 11 cells at the primary point without regional drift. It lies above the band mainly at the widest occupation spreads and the smaller drift amplitudes (Supplemental Text). At these two operating points, neutral copying therefore reaches the observed difference under narrower occupation spreads and larger drift amplitudes. These counts overstate how readily neutral copying produces the observed difference. We chose the two operating points in part because their F_ST is close to the observed value (Methods), and that choice favors combinations with unusually high F_ST. Ranked by six-seed median F_ST, the two points are 25th and 11th among the 498 diversity-matched combinations.

The 498 combinations, taken without that selection, give a different result. Each was run with six seeds. The observed value lies above the combination's six-seed 90th percentile in 436 of them (88 percent) and above all six simulated values in 400. It lies at or below the combination's median in only 8 (Supplemental Text). All 8 have a Gaussian kernel, five with an interaction length of 6 km and three with 12 km. At the scale of the two clusters, the record is therefore consistent with neutral copying, in the sense that some neutral settings reproduce the observed difference. Most neutral settings produce a smaller difference than the record shows. Because both statements hold, this comparison neither shows nor excludes a boundary.

The three common decorated classes vary between sites far more than neutral copying alone predicts (Table 2). Their site-weighted moment F values are 0.165 for Parkin Punctated, 0.117 for the pooled Barton, Kent, and Mound Place Incised, and 0.199 for the painted types, against neutral medians of 0.007 to 0.018 at every occupation-spread setting and both operating points. The seven rare classes, by contrast, are present at about as many sites as neutral copying predicts. At the primary operating point without drift, five of the seven lie inside the neutral band, Fortune Noded lies below it and Hull Engraved above it (Table 2), and the exceptions change with the cell. The rare classes' site counts are a weak discriminator, and the Supplemental Text reports them cell by cell. No tested setting of the simulator's unidentified parameters covers both the common classes' F and the rare classes' presence. The simulated common classes already estimate a higher F than the simulated rare classes, so the ordering of common above rare is not itself a departure. The departure is how far the common classes' F is above their simulated bands.

The three remaining alternatives were measured in two exploratory extensions of this comparison. The first crossed amplitudes up to 0.9 with three accumulation-span limits, three occupation windows, and seven arrangements of occupation date by location, giving 504 combinations per operating point with 50 simulated records each. Amplitudes of 0.7 and 0.9 bring all three common-class F values inside their bands at 5 of 504 combinations at the primary point and 45 of 496 evaluable combinations at the secondary. In those combinations, at most one of the seven rare classes has its observed site count inside its band, because the simulated records hold the rare classes at fewer sites than the record does. Conversely, no common-class F lies inside its band in any combination that covers at least five of the seven rare classes. No span limit or window covers both together. Occupation timing does matter for the spatial pattern. When dates are independent of location, the latitude trend of the painted types lies inside its band in none of 72 combinations at either point. When dates are ordered strictly by latitude, it does so in 91 of 144 combinations at the primary point and 82 of 144 at the secondary. Partial ordering gives 13 and 1 of 144. Only four of the 28 assemblages are dated, so the record cannot show whether such an ordering existed. These counts are of dependent combinations from one set of simulations, and each states only that a single summary of the record lies inside its own band. The second extension simulated the third alternative, new types entering the regional pool during the occupation. It modelled the regional pool as a finite population in which a draw is occasionally a new type, at five origination intensities from none to high, crossed with amplitudes of 0.1 to 0.9 (300 combinations per operating point). Classes were compared by rank of abundance, because a simulated new type has no counterpart among the named classes. Without origination, no combination places both the three common-class F values and at least five of the seven rare classes inside their bands. With origination, 36 of 300 combinations do so at the primary point and 35 of 300 at the secondary, all at amplitudes of 0.5 or more. Those combinations produce many more types than the record holds. Their median simulated richness runs from 18.5 to 333.5 types at the primary point and from 26 to 302 at the secondary, against ten classes in the record. In every combination whose richness band contains ten, at most four of the seven rare classes are covered. The richness comparison is weak evidence, because the record's classes are analyst-defined and some combine several types. The covering combinations also require that six to eight of the ten commonest types, including one or two of the three commonest, originated during the occupation (medians per combination). A dated sequence of type appearances could test that requirement, and the present record cannot. A neutral account therefore remains possible under three joint conditions: large regional turnover, origination of most types within the occupation, and, for the spatial pattern, dates ordered by latitude. None of the three can be tested with these data.

The comparison can detect a moderate departure. Doubling the odds of one common class in one of the two spatial clusters placed that class outside the neutral band in 23 to 45 of 50 marker records at amplitudes up to 0.1. Tripling it did so in 37 to 50 of 50. The neutral cells' own count outside their band is 10 of 50. A 1.5-fold change placed 7 to 26 of 50 outside at amplitudes up to 0.1 and 5 to 21 at 0.3. It moved the median of the marker records outside the band only for the pooled incised classes in the larger cluster at the two smallest amplitudes. Walls Engraved, the one rare class given a marker, never moved its occupancy median outside the band at any multiplier (Figure 5). These counts are conditional on the generator and the amplitude. They show what size of effect the neutral comparison detects in this sample, not what produced the record.

The departure is not an artifact of merged collections. On the 1940 survey material alone, matched on the 27 sites with at least 75 sherds in the 1940 part, the common classes' F values are 0.151, 0.087, and 0.195. For the current counts on the same 27 sites, they are 0.145, 0.076, and 0.195 (Table 2). Collection source matters at the level of a site's two collections and for the presence of rare classes. At the six sites with large additions, the log odds ratio of Parkin Punctated between Lipo's collection and the 1940 collection ranges from -1.03 at Cramor Place to +0.39 at Beck. The 90 percent interval excludes zero at four of the six sites for that class, at five of six for the pooled incised classes, and at one of six for the painted types. Wallace Incised is absent from every 1940 collection among the 28 and occurs only in Lipo's additions and at Holden Lake, so its presence pattern partly reflects which sites were revisited. A positive correlation between the pooled incised classes' share and collection size (Spearman +0.26 over the 28 rows) falls to zero in the 1940 material. Two correlations persist in the 1940 material. The share of Parkin Punctated rises with collection size (+0.42 over the 28 rows, +0.49 on the 1940 parts of the 27 matched sites) and the share of the painted types falls (-0.57 and -0.51). No neutral generator we ran reproduces them. Differences in site function may explain this, but these data cannot test that possibility.

Rim attributes recorded on 19 of the sites also exceed the neutral bands. Interior beveling (F 0.105), exterior notching (0.069), and Bell's share of all counted rims (0.266; 0.221 if restricted to rims of the two named pastes, for which no band was computed) lie above every neutral band on the 19 sites. They exceed the band's upper limit by about 0.04, 0.04, and 0.21 or more in every cell (the smallest excess is 0.0395, for notching at the secondary operating point; Figure 6A). Lip form (0.031) exceeds the widest band by 0.0001, and a reconstruction of Mainfort's lip percentages to within one rim contains that edge, so the lip result is not resolved by the record. The three common decorated classes at the same 19 sites also lie above their bands (0.107, 0.044, and 0.208).

The rims and the decoration show no shared pattern. Their directions of change across the basin are mixed. Beveling and notching fall with latitude (posterior medians of the rank correlation: -0.58, 90 percent interval -0.68 to -0.47, and -0.39, -0.53 to -0.25) and rise with longitude (+0.30 and +0.51). Parkin Punctated rises with latitude (+0.65, 0.58 to 0.72), the painted types fall with it strongly (-0.74, -0.78 to -0.70), and the pooled incised classes are weakly positive (+0.16, 0.02 to 0.29; Figure 6B). Beveling and notching thus change in the same direction as the painted types and opposite to Parkin Punctated. After linear adjustment for rank-based distance, pairwise differences in each rim attribute and pairwise total-variation differences in decoration correlate weakly. The medians lie between -0.07 and +0.14, and the intervals for beveling and notching against the current counts include zero (Figure 6C).

The rims are a second line of evidence for structure, recorded by one observer, and their relation to the phases as drawn is indirect. How far the rims Mainfort tabulated overlap physically with the 1940 survey collections is unknown. These three attributes were not tabulated as types in 1951 [-@phillips_ford_griffin_1951], although the collections Phillips sorted likely included them. Mainfort's [-@mainfort_2003_rims, 37] own account makes interior beveling spatial as well as temporal, so we cannot conclude a simple chronological inference from it.

These results answer the second question in two parts. At the scale of the two spatial clusters, the decorated frequencies differ by an amount neutral copying can produce, though most of its settings produce less. Below that scale, the three common classes and the rim attributes exceed the neutral bands, and the rare classes' site counts mostly fall within them, with exceptions that vary by setting. The convergence criterion, applied to the decorated classes and the rim attributes against the same neutral model, finds no shared pattern. This describes the record under the criterion's stated assumption. It does not test what a boundary would produce.

### What would it take to detect a boundary?

Neither model built to detect bounded units reliably recovered units of known size. The latent-unit model A1 was fitted to records simulated from its own generator at the real 28 sites with the between-unit scale set to 0.5 logits. Its posterior medians for that scale were 0.27 to 0.48, below the true value. On a record simulated with no units at all, where the true value is zero, the model returned 0.52 (0.05 to 1.39), an estimate as large as those from the records that contained units. An analyst given these posteriors alone could not distinguish a record with units from one without. The model's sampler also failed to explore the posterior. Twelve of the 14 recovery fits failed the diagnostics: eleven on R-hat or effective sample size of the co-membership probabilities (the model's estimates of whether two sites belong to the same unit), and one on divergent transitions. Even the fit to the no-unit record failed on R-hat and bulk effective sample size (Figure 7A). Failed fits are reported as failed validation attempts, not as estimates. The failures are not due to the intercept prior. Under the corrected Normal(0, 10) prior, the posteriors are essentially unchanged (0.58 against 0.52 on the no-unit record; 0.26 and 0.27 against 0.27 and 0.27 at a true value of 0.5).

The barrier model A3 fails differently, with the same consequence. Its sampler passes every check, but its posterior moves only weakly and unreliably with the true barrier. The mean of the six posterior medians is 0.80 at a true barrier of zero and 1.41 at a true barrier of 1.0. With the range-free and Normal(0, 10) intercept prior, all 39 fits pass the diagnostics. At a true barrier of zero, the model returned medians of 0.48 to 1.21 over six records, and at a true barrier of 1.0 it returned 0.70 to 2.28 (Figure 7B). These two ranges overlap, so a posterior median from this model does not say whether the barrier in the record it was fitted to was absent or large. The range for a true barrier of 1.0 includes the prior median of 1.35, where the posterior is located when the data say little about the parameter. The range for a true barrier of zero lies below the prior median but reaches within 0.14 of it. Pinning the range at 6 or 12 km removes one parameter the data do not identify. For a true barrier of zero, the posteriors move to 0.69 to 1.58 at 6 km and 1.03 to 1.51 at 12 km under the same prior. Under the Normal(0, 2) prior, it moves them to 2.7 to 3.5 over both pinned ranges. The true values still do not separate. All six prior-by-range combinations are tabulated in the Supplemental Text.

Site geometry is a plausible explanation. These 28 assemblages are located in clusters separated by gaps of occupied but uncollected or sparsely occupied ground. We observe a composition that changes gradually across the basin, sampled only at the clusters, largely as a difference between the clusters. A composition that steps at the edge of a bounded unit located at the same gap produces a similar difference (Figure 2). We have not isolated geometry as the cause. A1's failures are largely failures of its sampler, whereas A3 samples without diagnostic failures and recovers weakly, and a recovery result is not a statement that the two histories have equal likelihood. On these 28 sites, the two models that respect distance decay did not reliably distinguish a step from a gradient. That is a measured statement about these two models and this sample, not a theorem about every model.

## Discussion

### Three senses in which the phases could be real

The question of whether the phases are real covers three claims, and these data answer them differently (Table 3). As a geographic pattern in pottery composition, the phases are real. Location organizes composition, the map alone recovers the scheme, and the boundaries lie where settlement is sparsest. This is the pattern the culture historians noticed, and their expectation that it would be there was correct. As bounded units with edges, the phases are not resolved in either direction by the two models we tested on this sample. Those models do not reliably recover known edges at these sites, so these analyses neither show nor exclude a step at the gaps. As social units, which is what the culture historians meant and what this study set out to test, the phases are not demonstrated. Our standard for that claim is that independent signatures converge, and the two signatures that could be assessed on this record do not.

**Table 3.** The three senses in which the phases could be real, and what the record shows for each.

| Sense | Measured basis | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| A geographic pattern in pottery composition | Contrast-based share of latent log-ratio variance 0.93 to 0.94 across three intercept priors (Figure 4); coordinates alone recover the phases for 24 of 26 assemblages; boundaries in the least-occupied ground | Yes |
| Bounded units with edges | A1 and A3 do not reliably recover known structure on their own generators at these 28 sites; settlement gaps are a plausible reason, not a demonstrated cause | Not resolved by these two models on this sample, in either direction |
| Social units, under the convergence criterion | Seriation not independent of the frequencies; spatial signature not separable from distance; between-cluster F_ST 0.0062 inside the neutral band in 17 of 30 cells at operating points chosen in part on that statistic, and above the six-seed 90th percentile of 436 of 498 diversity-matched cells without that selection; rims and decoration show no shared pattern | Not demonstrated |

Phases are then best understood as what their makers said they were, divisions of continuous variability drawn at the gaps in a clumped settlement pattern. McNutt [-@mcnutt_1996, 250] wrote of the southern phases that "the distinctions that one tries so desperately to make between the phases involved are little more than comments on local elaborations—ceramic drift, if you will—of the basic pottery complexes possessed by peoples involved in this expansion." McNutt, Franklin, and Henry [-@mcnutt_franklin_henry_2012, 247] describe the Walls phase as "a developmental lineage" spanning roughly two centuries, so that a phase's members need not even be contemporaneous. Our results give that interpretation a measured basis and also mark its limit. Mainfort's [-@mainfort_2003, 180] observation that phase members lie "in the general vicinity of each other in ordination space" without forming clusters is what a geographic pattern sampled at settlement concentrations produces. Proximity on the ground accounts for proximity in the ordination.

### What the record shows beyond geography

The common decorated classes vary between sites more than neutral copying produces at any setting we tried, with or without regional drift over time. The rare classes' site counts mostly fall within their bands, with exceptions that vary by setting. The measured departure is the level of the common classes' F above their simulated bands, and it persists when the later collections are removed. Its cause is not established, and we have deliberately left three candidate causes unordered. The first is differences in how collections were made or classified. The 1940-only comparison makes this less likely but does not eliminate it, because several field parties made the 1940 collections over a season. The second is directed change over time that is not drift. Sites occupied at different times during a span in which the common types rose and fell would differ in those types without any boundary between them, and these collections lack the temporal information needed to test this option. The third is spatial structure in the sense the culture historians meant. Only the third would indicate a social boundary, and decoration alone does not separate it from the other two. One neutral form remains untested: a process with type origination, in which types enter the regional pool at different times. Such a process would produce unequal per-class F under neutrality and could change both rare-class occupancy and the per-class pattern.

Rim attributes do not resolve this. Beveling, notching, and paste depart from neutral copying on the same 19 sites by margins larger than the lip form's and comparable to the common decorated classes'. The attribute scale could register differences in learning more readily than the type scale (Methods). Beveling and notching nonetheless depart from neutral copying by margins comparable to those of the common classes, so the rims do not show the larger departures that greater boundary sensitivity would suggest. That comparison does not test sensitivity. The rim sample is smaller (19 sites vs. 28), and we have not measured what size departure a boundary would produce at either scale. Beveling and notching decrease with latitude, as do the painted types, whereas Parkin Punctated increases with it. We do not interpret this as evidence for or against a boundary, because neither a joint spatial model nor a recovery experiment establishes what a boundary or independent transmission would produce across these trait systems. The paste statistic also includes 550 rims of unestablished paste (Methods). The rims are one observer's tabulation of 19 sites, with totals from 27 to 277, and it is unclear whether they were included in the phase definitions. We therefore interpret them as a second line of evidence that suggests structure, not as evidence against the phases.

### Parkin

Parkin was the basin's largest town, with one of its tallest mounds, and its prominence is unquestioned. Decorated frequencies do not, however, establish a boundary in ceramic learning at the edge of the Parkin phase. The phase lies near the northern of the two spatial clusters the site layout supports, and the difference between those clusters is one that distance-dependent copying can produce, though most tested settings produce less. The mound-height distribution and regional evidence on hoe exchange are described in the Supplemental Text, and neither requires restricting the phase to its edge. Other evidence for a Parkin polity addresses political organization and not the ceramic boundary. The region's own history offers a precedent for separating the two. Coosa, the best-documented Mississippian paramountcy, spanned two ceramic traditions and site clusters separable at the phase level, and political affiliation rather than shared decoration united them [@anderson_1996, 250-251]. If the polity the De Soto expedition met was at Parkin, the evidence for it lies in ceremony, fortification, and settlement size, and the decorated pottery does not resolve it.

### Limits

Four limits are findings of this study, not caveats. The first is site geometry. A1 and A3, the two models we built that respect distance decay, did not reliably separate a step from a gradient in this sample, as their recovery tests measured. The clustered geometry is a plausible explanation, not a demonstrated cause, and other models remain untested. The second is chronology. Four of 28 sites have radiocarbon medians. The seriation order is built from these frequencies and tracks latitude, and no analysis here tests change along the sequence. The third is the neutral model. Its operating points match local and pooled diversity and are not identified. Its fixed innovation target makes it a standard of comparison for spatial copying among contemporaneous sites, which is why the drifting target was added and why the per-class departure is reported at every amplitude rather than at one. The fourth is time-averaging, which reduces differences between assemblages [@tomasovych_kidwell_2009; @kidwell_tomasovych_2013; @perreault_2018; @youngblood_miton_morin_2023]. Every simulated record has accumulation spans of random start and length, so the reported bands include their effects under the modeled conditions.

### Requirements for other cases and for further data

Interpreting similarity as evidence of a bounded social unit has two requirements that apply beyond this case. First, the pattern must exceed what distance-dependent interaction on the actual settlement map produces. The comparison must be made at matched diversity, because a neutral model run at the wrong diversity is easily credited with structure it cannot produce. A localized neutral process is likewise easily mistaken for biased copying when diversity is taken at face value [@schneider_martins_deaguiar_2016; @premo_2016; @premo_scholnick_2011]. Second, before a model's boundary-detection results on a record are interpreted, the model must recover a known boundary on records simulated at the same sites. The second requirement changed our conclusions. Had we fitted the latent-unit model to the record without the recovery test, we would have reported a posterior for the between-unit scale that the recovery test shows does not distinguish a record with units from one without. Together with the convergence criterion, these requirements give a way to evaluate social interpretations of artifact variation within evolutionary archaeology [@dunnell_1995; @neiman_1995; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_2015].

The same results identify what to collect next. Assemblages in the gaps would help separate a step from a gradient, because a gradient predicts intermediate compositions there and a step does not. Dated or stratified contexts would let us observe change along the sequence, which these surface collections do not allow. They would also show whether the common classes' extra variation is directed change over time at sites occupied at different times. Trait systems recorded separately from the decorated types, of which the rim attributes are the only available example, would let the convergence criterion be applied. Under the criterion's assumption, we look for a departure shared across trait systems, and the rims and decoration on these 19 sites do not show one. The neutral comparison detects a doubling of a common class's odds in one cluster in roughly half to nine-tenths of marker records. A departure of that size, if it were concentrated at one edge, is therefore within what the method can detect on this sample. The record shows departures of that size and larger. What is missing is the independent evidence that would attribute them to a cause.

## Conclusion

Phases organize the archaeological record of the Lower Mississippi Valley, and their use as social units makes a claim their definition does not: that similar pottery at nearby sites identifies a community and, at Parkin, a political territory. The St. Francis basin phases appear to be groups of people because pottery composition is organized by location, settlement is clumped along the rivers, and sorting assemblages by proximity and resemblance draws boundaries in the gaps. Between the basin's two spatial clusters, decorated frequencies differ by an amount neutral copying can produce, though most settings produce less. Three common decorated classes and the separately recorded rim attributes vary between sites more than the tested neutral settings produce. The two trait systems show no shared pattern, and the cause of that variation is not established. Neither model built to detect bounded units reliably recovered known units at these sites, and the settlement gaps are a plausible reason. The phases are real as a geographic pattern, but the two models tested on this sample did not resolve them as bounded units, and the evidence does not demonstrate them as social units. Phase membership alone is weak evidence for a bounded learning community, and the inference from one phase to one polity is not warranted on its own. Resolving the question requires assemblages from the settlement gaps, dated or stratified contexts, and trait systems recorded independently of the units under test. Those are the data the culture historians' hypothesis has always needed.
