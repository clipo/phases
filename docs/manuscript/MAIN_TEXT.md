# What Are Phases? Geographic Structure, Neutral Copying, and the Search for Social Boundaries in the Lower Mississippi Valley

**Carl P. Lipo, Beau DiNapoli, and Mark E. Madsen**

## Abstract

Culture historians built phases to describe continuous spatial and temporal variation based on assemblages of artifact classes. They also interpreted phases as reflecting social units. We ask why phases look like groups of people, and whether the St. Francis basin phases of the Lower Mississippi Valley are more than groupings of nearby sites. Twenty-eight time-averaged decorated-ceramic assemblages, clustered on site coordinates alone, recover Phillips's phases for all but two locations. A spatial field model assigns 93 percent of the between-site differences in composition, beyond sampling error, to a smooth geographic surface, and the phase boundaries run through the least-occupied ground between settlement clusters. Neutral copying can produce the difference between the basin's two spatial clusters, but most parameter combinations matching the record's diversity produce a smaller one. Three common decorated types, and rim attributes tabulated separately on 19 sites, vary between sites more than neutral copying produces, except in tested settings that fail to match the record's other features, but they share no pattern and support no phase boundary. We conclude that two models built to detect bounded units do not reliably structure the data, plausibly because of the settlement gaps. Thus, phases are real as a geographic pattern, not resolved as bounded units, and not demonstrated as social units.

**Keywords:** cultural transmission, neutral models, isolation by distance, phases, Mississippian archaeology, Parkin phase

## Resumen

Los historiadores culturales construyeron fases para describir la variación continua en el espacio y el tiempo a partir de conjuntos de clases de artefactos. También interpretaron las fases como reflejo de unidades sociales. Preguntamos por qué las fases parecen grupos de personas y si las fases de la cuenca del río St. Francis, en el valle inferior del Misisipi, son más que agrupaciones de sitios cercanos. Veintiocho conjuntos de cerámica decorada, promediados en el tiempo y agrupados únicamente por las coordenadas de los sitios, recuperan las fases de Phillips en todas las localidades, salvo dos. Un modelo de campo espacial asigna a una superficie geográfica suave el 93 por ciento de las diferencias de composición entre sitios que exceden lo atribuible al error de muestreo, y los límites de las fases atraviesan el terreno menos ocupado entre las concentraciones de asentamientos. La copia neutral puede producir la diferencia entre las dos agrupaciones espaciales de la cuenca, pero la mayoría de las combinaciones de parámetros que reproducen la diversidad del registro producen una diferencia menor. Tres tipos decorados comunes y los atributos de los bordes tabulados por separado en 19 sitios varían entre sitios más de lo que produce la copia neutral, salvo en las combinaciones evaluadas que no reproducen las demás características del registro, pero no comparten un patrón ni respaldan un límite entre fases. Concluimos que dos modelos construidos para detectar unidades delimitadas no estructuran los datos de manera fiable, posiblemente debido a los vacíos de asentamiento. Así, las fases son reales como patrón geográfico, no quedan resueltas como unidades delimitadas y no están demostradas como unidades sociales.

**Palabras clave:** transmisión cultural, modelos neutrales, aislamiento por distancia, fases, arqueología misisipiana, fase Parkin

## Introduction

Phases are the spatial and temporal units in which much of the North American archaeological record is organized. Willey and Phillips [-@willey_phillips_1958, 22] defined the phase as "the practicable and intelligible unit of archaeological study" formed by a set of traits distinctive enough to separate it from other units, confined to a locality or region and a brief interval of time. Nothing in that definition is social. Pottery types and other artifact classes vary continuously across space and through time, and the researchers who built phases knew they were dividing that variability into units for description. However, the units are often interpreted as social groups. Willey and Phillips [-@willey_phillips_1958, 48-50] set out the chain of reasoning that equates component with community and phase with society, conceded that "in practice it does not work," and then proposed that archaeologists "may act as if" phases had social reality. In the Lower Mississippi River Valley, the survey phases were built "to elucidate sociopolitical boundaries" [@haley_2014, 41], and Williams, defining the southeast-Missouri phases, wrote that "in most cases, phase probably equals 'society'" [@williams_1954, 25; quoted in @fox_1998, 33].

The step leading to a social interpretation of phases depends on a premise that is rarely stated. The premise holds that assemblages more similar to one another than to their neighbors were made by a bounded set of people who interacted mainly with each other. Based on that premise, a phase defined by a single class of artifacts becomes the basis of a hypothesis about social interaction. It remains a hypothesis until independent evidence from other artifact classes, ancient DNA, linguistics, or documented history tests it. Pairing a phase with a dominant center site strengthens the premise. Phases were built around a type site and then treated as given [@fox_1998, 57-58]. A grouping of similar deposits that includes a much larger one thus becomes the basis of an inferred polity before independent evidence for its social boundaries has been evaluated.

Why, then, do phases appear to be groups of people? When culture historians examined the record, they saw groups of nearby deposits whose assemblages resembled one another more than they resembled assemblages elsewhere. Earlier critiques have shown that the groups are not sharply bounded. Fox [-@fox_1998] showed that the southeast-Missouri phases fail as classes, and used cluster analysis and multidimensional scaling to show that they fail as groups, with member assemblages falling into statistical clusters at random rather than by phase. Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003, 180] found "little evidence for the integrity of the traditional late period phases in the study area," even though "most sites assigned to most phases are located in the general vicinity of each other in ordination space." Lipo [-@lipo_2001, 60] suggested through frequency seriation that "the phases themselves might be simply arbitrary spatial and temporal slices of a single interacting population that is much larger in size." These studies show that the phases do not separate completely. They leave open why the phases appeared to form coherent groups in the first place, and what those groups are.

Similarity alone cannot answer that question, for structural rather than statistical reasons. Phillips [-@phillips_1970] drew the phases tested here by sorting assemblages on their proportions of decorated pottery types and on their proximity. A scheme recovered from the same frequencies and the same map records how the scheme was drawn, not how its units behaved [@willey_phillips_1958, 27-28]. Ordination arranges assemblages by resemblance. It cannot distinguish a bounded learning community from copying that declines gradually with distance, because both produce neighbors that resemble each other.

The Lower Mississippi Valley phases are a well-documented case. Its late pre-contact phases are well defined, long established, and in active use. Figure 1 shows four of them, Nodena, Parkin, Walls, and Kent, as Phillips [-@phillips_1970] drew them. The Parkin phase contains the Parkin site, the largest moated and palisaded town in the St. Francis basin of northeastern Arkansas. Reconstructions of the Hernando de Soto expedition's route identify Parkin as Casqui, the seat of a paramount chief the expedition met in 1541 [@morse_1990, 125-129; @hudson_smith_depratter_1990; @mitchem_2017]. Because of its size, fortification, platform mound, and ethnohistoric identification, Parkin is routinely interpreted as the center of a Mississippian polity. Extending that interpretation from the town to the phase makes a further claim: that the sites assigned to the Parkin phase belonged to a bounded, hierarchically organized society headed by the Parkin site.

We ask three questions of the St. Francis basin phases. First, why do culture historians see phases in these data? Second, is there evidence of social boundaries between the phases beyond the geographic distribution of settlement along the basin's rivers? Third, what would it take to detect such boundaries in archaeological deposits like these? To answer these questions, we explore the data that form the basis for phases in the St. Francis Basin. These are surface-collected decorated-ceramic counts of Phillips, Ford, and Griffin [-@phillips_ford_griffin_1951], compiled by Lipo [-@lipo_2001]. We analyze them with one spatial model, one neutral copying model as the standard of comparison, and two models built to detect bounded units. In this study, a bounded learning community means a population in which copying was more common internally and less common across its edge than distance alone would predict.

## Distance-dependent copying and clumped settlement

Cultural transmission theory offers a way to explain patterns in pottery decoration through how people learn. While individuals are free to experiment, much pottery practice is learned from other potters, so the designs they make preserve evidence of those connections. In a neutral model, no decorative variant has an advantage in being copied. New variants arise, and their proportions change by chance as a finite population of potters copies existing designs, a process called neutral drift [@dunnell_1978; @neiman_1995; @bentley_hahn_shennan_2004; @eerkens_lipo_2005; @eerkens_lipo_2007; @fogarty_kandler_creanza_2024].

The effects of copying generally decline with distance. People learn from those they live near and see often, so a potter is more likely to copy a neighbor than someone further away. Under neutral drift, this tendency makes nearby assemblages more alike than distant ones, with similarity declining gradually across the landscape. Population geneticists call the pattern isolation by distance [@wright_1943], and it has long been recognized in stylistic variation [@dunnell_1970; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_hunt_1997; @neiman_1995; @shennan_crema_kerig_2015]. It arises wherever interaction is mainly local. It also produces between-place differences without any boundary, because small or poorly connected places drift toward idiosyncratic combinations of stylistic features [@lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021].

However, communities rarely settle evenly across a landscape and are often clumped. For example, people did not live in locations that are evenly spaced across the St. Francis basin. Settlements concentrate on particular reaches of the basin's rivers, with less-occupied stretches between them. Any gradual change in style is therefore sampled unevenly, densely in some places and sparsely or not at all in others.

Gradual variation sampled at settlement concentrations can resemble a set of distinct units. Seriation and ordination methods such as correspondence analysis [@smith_neiman_2007] sort assemblages by resemblance, and the culture historians who drew the phases also sorted them by proximity. When styles change gradually across clumped settlements, both criteria group the assemblages of each concentration together and place boundaries in the gaps. Units produced this way appear distinct even when the copying process has no edge [@lipo_madsen_dunnell_hunt_1997; @lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021]. This reflects the difference between typological and population interpretations [@mayr_1959; @dunnell_1980]. Interpreted typologically, a cluster of similar assemblages is a unit with members and a boundary. Interpreted as a population, the same cluster is a sampled portion of a larger learning network. Figure 2 illustrates the problem: one set of sampled values is drawn beside a gradient and beside a step at the gap. No assemblage comes from the stretch where a gradient predicts intermediate values and a step does not.

Identifying a bounded learning community therefore requires more than clustering. Mixing erodes differences between populations [@henrich_boyd_1998, 219-220], so restricting copying across an edge should increase differences between communities beyond what distance alone produces. Whether such a restriction is detectable depends on its strength and duration, site location, and the record's resolution.

## Materials and methods

Each analysis is described here by the question it asks, what it is compared against, and how its result is read. The Supplemental Text gives the full specification of each model, its priors, and its settings.

### Data

We use decorated-class counts from the Lower Mississippi Valley Survey collections of Phillips, Ford, and Griffin [-@phillips_ford_griffin_1951], compiled by Lipo [-@lipo_2001]. We focus on decorated classes as candidates for neutral stylistic variation, designs whose frequencies may change through copying and chance without affecting how a vessel performs [@dunnell_1978; @neiman_1995]. Plain sherds are excluded because their variation reflects differences in paste and temper that can indicate firing conditions and vessel function [@feathers_2006; @feathers_and_peacock_2008]. Following Lipo [-@lipo_2001, Table 7.4], we pool sherds from Barton Incised, Kent Incised, and Mound Place Incised, and we pool the painted types. The painted class is Old Town Red, Nodena Red and White, and Avenue Polychrome, and the rows taken from his table also count Carson Red-on-Buff and Hollywood White Slipped in it (Supplemental Text). This pooling lets us include smaller sherds that often have only portions of the attributes needed to identify these types.  Ten decorated classes remain after pooling. Each class is a culture-historical type, a unit of measurement built by analysts, not a category assumed to have existed in the past.

An assemblage is a member of the phase of the area it lies in. Using Phillips' phase areas [-@phillips_1970], Lipo [-@lipo_2001, Figure 2.6] mapped them as shown in Figure 1. Two assemblages (Cranor Place and Holden Lake) fall outside all defined phase areas and are assigned to the nearest phase. We include only assemblages with at least 75 decorated sherds (Supplemental Text, Table S1), leaving the Nodena phase with a single assemblage, which we exclude. These rules leave 28 assemblages with 14,101 decorated sherds (median 251 per assemblage; range 79 to 1,709) across three phases: Parkin (12 assemblages), Kent (11), and Walls (5). Of the 28, 21 are the 1940 survey collections alone, six combine the 1940 collection with collections Lipo made at the same site in 1996 and 1997 (controlled surface collections, and excavation at Castile Landing), and one, Holden Lake, is Lipo's alone [@lipo_2001]. We located each assemblage using the Lower Mississippi Survey site files, and we drew settlement attributes from a regional database [@lipo_dunnell_2007]. Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003] compiled a separate database, much of it transcribed from the same survey counts. He follows Phillips's phase assignments and adds Smith's Horseshoe Lake phase and the phases of western Tennessee. Because its counts overlap ours, we analyze it separately as a second case in the Supplemental Text (Tables S9 and S10).

These assemblages are time-averaged surface collections that are built up over years of use and discard. Thus, we treat them as the accumulated product of copying across a population rather than as degraded samples of a single moment [@schiffer_1972; @dunnell_1995; @neiman_1995; @holdaway_wandsnider_2008; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_2015; @perreault_2019]. Time-averaging reduces differences between assemblages and distorts measures of diversity and neutrality in proportion to the time over which an assemblage accumulated relative to how long variants remained in use [@madsen_2012; @premo_2014; @miller_atkins_premo_2018]. A neutral model also describes people copying one another, whereas an assemblage counts durable objects, so its expectations apply only approximately [@crema_bortolini_lake_2023].

Radiocarbon determinations compiled by Mainfort [-@mainfort_2001] match just four of the 28 assemblages, with calibrated medians from AD 1466 at Kent Place to 1596 at Clay Hill (Supplemental Text, Figure S1). Hollywood has two calibrated one-sigma ranges reaching back to AD 1285 [@haley_2014]. On that evidence, the late pre-contact occupation spans roughly the fourteenth through the early seventeenth centuries, and the sequence includes communities affected by European contact and the demographic collapse that followed [@morse_morse_1996; @lipo_2001]. The deposits overlap temporally, and each accumulated over time. Every analysis below therefore treats the record as a time-averaged spatial sample, and the neutral simulations it is compared against are built to the same condition.

### Standard of evidence and analyses

Our ideal standard of evidence for identifying a social unit includes signatures from independent sources of information that converge on the same boundaries. This is an evidential criterion, not a derived result. It assumes that the same learners, connected by the same networks, carried the traits being compared. Neither the cited models nor our simulations derive what a boundary must produce across different trait systems. We recognize that a single signature can be produced by several processes [@crema_kandler_shennan_2016]. 

Deterministic seriations are built from the same frequencies that drew the phases, so their structure cannot test them. Tests of departure from neutral copying are distorted by time-averaging of surface collections at this resolution [@madsen_2012; @premo_2014]. This leaves us with two possible signatures. The first is the spatial pattern of the decorated classes, compared with what distance-dependent copying produces. The second is the rim attributes, the one trait system in these collections that was recorded separately from the decorated types.

Our analyses fit a model or state an interval using Bayesian methods [@gelman_etal_2013; @otarola_castillo_torquato_2018]. The record is small and uneven, with assemblages of 79 to 1,709 decorated sherds, so the proportions calculated from the counts are more uncertain at some sites than at others [@robertson_1999]. A Bayesian analysis begins with a prior distribution, which states the plausible values of each unknown quantity before the data are considered. It combines the prior with the data to produce a posterior distribution, which states how plausible each value is given both. The posterior carries the uncertainty in the counts into every quantity it produces. It also answers a question of magnitude with a range of supported values, not by accepting or rejecting a null hypothesis [@wasserstein_lazar_2016]. We report each posterior quantity as its median with a 90 percent equal-tailed interval.

### The spatial field model

Our first analysis describes how pottery composition varies with geography without first dividing the sites into groups. We fit a Bayesian spatial field model to the counts of the ten decorated classes in each of the 28 assemblages. The model treats each assemblage as a random sample of sherds from a site where the ten classes occur in fixed but unknown proportions. It builds those proportions from three parts. The first is a baseline for each class, the same at every site and reflecting how common the class is across the basin. The second is a smooth surface over the map, on which nearby sites take similar values and distant sites take nearly independent values. The third is an adjustment for each site with no spatial pattern, letting a site differ from its neighbors for reasons unrelated to location. A record organized by location places most of its between-site variation in the surface. A record in which each site has its own character regardless of its neighbors falls under site-level adjustment.

We report two quantities. The spatial share is the fraction of the between-site variation the model finds in the smooth surface. We measure it on the model's log-odds scale, which is the logarithm of a class's proportion relative to that of a reference class. The second quantity states how different two sites a given distance apart are expected to be under the surface alone, in standard deviations of a class's log-odds. We fit the model under three priors on the class baselines and report all three. Our model is written in Stan, and we fit it with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo [@carpenter_etal_2017].

### The neutral copying model

To model copying that declines with distance, we use the network model of Lipo, DiNapoli, Madsen, and Hunt [-@lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021], placed on the real site map. The model is a computer simulation. Each of the 28 sites has a fixed number of potters, whom we call learners. At each step, a learner usually copies a decorated class from another learner at the same site. Less often, the learner copies from another site, and nearer sites are more likely sources than distant ones. Occasionally, a learner adopts a class without copying anyone (innovation), choosing it in proportion to how common each class is across the region. Each simulated assemblage builds over time from a random starting point and length, unrelated to the site's location or phase. It is then reduced by random sampling to the number of sherds in the real assemblage. Nothing in the model limits copying between any two sites, so the simulated region contains no boundaries.

The model has five settings: the number of learners at each site, how often a learner innovates (the innovation rate), how often a copy comes from another site (the mixing rate), the distance over which copying between sites declines (the interaction length), and the shape of that decline (the kernel). We do not estimate these parameters from the record. We also do not adjust them until the simulation reproduces the differences between sites that our comparisons test, because a model adjusted in that way would match the record automatically and the comparison would show nothing. Instead, we iterate through a grid of parameter combinations and keep those whose simulated assemblages match the record on three measures of diversity, each within 10 percent of the record's value. The first is the average diversity within an assemblage, measured by the Gini-Simpson index, which is the probability that two sherds drawn from the same assemblage belong to different classes. The second is the average number of classes present in an assemblage. The third is the Gini-Simpson diversity of all 28 assemblages combined.

Many parameter combinations match the record on all three measures, and the record gives no basis for choosing among them. We therefore run every comparison with two of them, which we call operating points. An operating point is one complete set of parameter values: one number of learners, one innovation rate, one mixing rate, one interaction length, and one kernel. The primary operating point is the matched combination whose simulated assemblages come closest to the record on the three diversity measures and on the between-cluster *F*~ST~ defined below. The secondary operating point is the closest such match among combinations with a different kernel and a different number of learners. Because *F*~ST~ was used to choose both points, the comparison of the record's *F*~ST~ with them is interpreted with that in mind. We also report that comparison for every combination that matches the record's diversity, without that selection.

For each statistic, we run the simulation 50 times at each operating point. Each run produces one simulated record of 28 assemblages, and we compute the statistic on each simulated record in the same way as on the archaeological record. We summarize the 50 values by their median and by the neutral band, the range containing the middle 80 percent (from the 10th to the 90th percentile). An observed value inside the band is one that neutral copying commonly produces at that operating point. Because the band excludes the highest tenth of simulated values, about one simulated record in ten lies above it, even though the simulation has no boundary. Only an observed value above the band is read as more variation than neutral copying produces, because restricted copying, if it occurred, would increase differences between sites. Every such reading holds only for this model at that operating point. Two further conditions are not fixed by an operating point: how widely the sites' periods of occupation are spread in time, and how much the regional mix of designs changes by chance over time (regional drift). We vary each over a range of values and report where the record falls relative to the neutral band at every value.

### Statistics compared with the neutral model

Cultural *F*~ST~ [@wright_1951; @bell_richerson_mcelreath_2009] measures the share of decorated diversity, by the Gini-Simpson index, that lies between groups rather than within them, in Nei's [-@nei_1973] form. We report it between the two spatial clusters the site layout supports, a division by k-means on the site coordinates that separates eleven of the twelve Parkin-area sites from the other seventeen. We compare it with the neutral band in each of 30 cells. A cell is one operating point at one setting of occupation spread or regional drift.

For each decorated class, we compute a site-weighted moment estimate of *F*: the between-site variance of the class's share, with binomial sampling variance removed, divided by its mean share times one minus that share. For the three common classes (Parkin Punctated, the pooled Barton, Kent, and Mound Place Incised, and the pooled painted types), the statistic is *F*. For the seven rare classes, it is the number of the 28 sites where the class is present, because *F* is poorly determined for a class present at few sites. We compare each class with its own simulated band. Six of the 28 assemblages combine the 1940 survey collection with Lipo's 1996 to 1997 collections at the same site, so we also recompute these statistics on the 1940 material alone.

Change over time is a second source of differences between sites that does not involve boundaries. Assemblages that accumulated at different times sampled a regional pool of designs that changed over the interval. We therefore also let the simulated regional pool drift through copying and chance. The drift amplitude is the regional *F* accumulated over the simulated occupation, on the same scale as the between-site *F* reported for the classes. It is 0 when the pool is fixed, and we run it at 0, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, and 0.3, the last larger than any between-site *F* the decorated classes show. Two further sets of simulations extend the comparison to amplitudes up to 0.9, to occupation dates ordered by location, and to new types entering the region during the occupation. We designed both after seeing the first results, fixed the rules for reading each before running it, and ran each once.

To measure the magnitude of departure the comparison can identify, we introduce a marker into simulated records. We achieve this by multiplying the odds of one class by 1.5, 2, or 3 at every site within one of the two spatial clusters. We then determine how many of the 50 marker records show the class's statistic outside the neutral band. Finally, we present this count alongside the number of neutral records that fall outside their own band, which is approximately 10 of 50 by design.

### Rim attributes

The rim attributes serve two purposes. They provide a second line of evidence, a trait system recorded separately from the decorated types. They also change the scale of measurement. A decorated class is a type, defined by a combination of attributes [@dunnell_1971]. An attribute such as interior beveling or lip form is a single choice a potter makes during manufacture, and is therefore closer in scale to the unit that one potter learns from another [@obrien_etal_2010]. Attributes of the same artifacts can have different transmission histories [@bettinger_eerkens_1999; @eerkens_lipo_2005], and some steps in making a pot follow the networks in which potters learned more closely than decoration does [@gosselain_2000]. Frequencies of single attributes may therefore register differences in learning between communities that type frequencies average out.

Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003_rims, Table 1] published counts of rim attributes on 7,195 rims from 55 late-period sites, tabulated separately from the decorated types. Lip form comes from unpublished data that Mainfort shared with us. Nineteen of our 28 assemblages have at least 25 rims, for a total of 2,065 rims. We use three attributes that we interpret as not tabulated as types in 1951: interior beveling, exterior notching, and lip form. We treat them as potentially stylistic, because they are choices that do not appear to affect how these vessels perform and that carry no differential cost of manufacture. Mainfort's tabulation also records paste. We do not analyze it, for the same reason that plain sherds are excluded: paste and temper can reflect firing conditions and vessel function (Data). 

For each attribute, we compute the same moment *F* on the 19 sites against the same neutral model, and for the three common decorated classes on the same 19 sites. We report each attribute's rank correlation with latitude and longitude as a posterior summary. This requires a prior for each site's attribute frequency, and we use a Beta(1/2, 1/2) distribution. A Beta distribution describes a single proportion between zero and one. This one has the same effect as adding half a rim with the attribute and half a rim without it to each site's count, so it has little influence on the result. It keeps a site where the attribute was never observed, or always observed, from being treated as exactly 0 or 100 percent. We also report the rank correlation between rim and decoration differences across the 171 site pairs after linear adjustment for distance. Phillips [-@phillips_1970] did not define the phases on decorated types alone, so we describe the rims as a second trait system recorded by a second observer and do not call them independent of the phase definitions.

### Models for bounded units

The spatial field model describes composition as a smooth surface. Two further models ask whether the record is better described by that surface plus discrete units, each unit a set of sites that share a shift in composition beyond what their locations predict (Figure 3). The latent-unit model assigns each site to one of three units, the number of phases in the basin, without being told which site belongs to which. Its between-unit scale sets how far the units differ in composition. The barrier model fixes the division in advance by drawing an edge through the site map, and asks whether sites on the same side of the edge share a shift in composition beyond what the smooth surface predicts. Its barrier parameter is the size of that shared shift. We placed the edge in two ways: at the division between the two spatial clusters, and along the straight line across the basin that best agrees with Phillips's Parkin-versus-rest labels. Each model is the spatial field model exactly when its added parameter is zero.

![**Figure 3.** The three models, drawn on the 28 site locations with north at the top. This is a schematic of what each model assumes, not a display of fitted values. (A) The spatial field model: composition varies as a smooth surface over the map, shown by the shading. (B) The latent-unit model: the same surface, plus three units that each shift composition at their member sites. Marker shape shows one assignment of sites to units, drawn at random. (C) The barrier model: the same surface, plus a shift shared by the sites on one side of a fixed edge, drawn as a step in the shading. Both tested placements of the edge are shown: the two clusters from k-means on the site coordinates (filled and open circles, 11 and 17 sites), and the straight line that best agrees with Phillips's Parkin-versus-rest assignment (dashed, 10 and 18 sites).](../../figures/fig3_model_schematic.png)

Before fitting either model to the archaeological record, we tested whether it could detect a boundary that we knew was there. We used each model to produce simulated records at the 28 site locations, some with units or a barrier of a known size and some with none. We then fitted the model to each simulated record and compared its estimate with the value we had set. A model that cannot return the known value from data it produced itself cannot be interpreted on the archaeological record, where the true value is unknown, so we report no estimate from it. We also check every fitted model against five standard diagnostics of whether the fitting algorithm sampled the posterior reliably [@vehtari_etal_2021]. A fit that fails is reported as failing, and its posterior is not used as an estimate.

## Results

### Why did culture historians see phases in these data?

The phases are recoverable from the sites' spatial distribution alone. Ward linkage on the 28 assemblages' coordinates, scored on the 26 assemblages within a mapped phase area, agrees with Phillips's assignments at an adjusted Rand index of 0.740. The two assemblages it places with another phase, Big Eddy and Castile Landing, are the two in the narrow southern extension of the Parkin area, which it groups with Kent (Figure 4A). Divisions by phase size, built by allocating sites to random centers on the map, agree at a median of 0.485, a reference that preserves both group sizes and geographic allocation. Composition alone agrees at 0.358 and misplaces six of the 26 (Supplemental Text). Adding composition to the coordinates does not raise the agreement above the map alone under Ward linkage. A scheme divided by the map alone would be recovered from coordinates and would not improve as type frequency data were included, and these assemblages show that. This result describes how the scheme was drawn by researchers who sorted these collections by proximity and resemblance. It does not show how the scheme's units behaved.

Composition itself is organized by location. The spatial field model splits differences between sites into a component that varies smoothly across the map and a component specific to each site. The share that lies in the smooth surface has a posterior median of 0.93, with a 90 percent interval from 0.79 to 1.00, under the Normal(0, 10) intercept prior. Under the two alternative priors, it is 0.93 (0.80 to 1.00) and 0.94 (0.79 to 1.00) (Figure 5). Nearly all of the between-site variation the model finds therefore follows location.

The model also states how different two sites are expected to be at a given distance, and that difference increases with distance. It is easiest to read as a ratio. For any decorated class, take its odds at a site, meaning how common it is there relative to a fixed comparison class. Between two neighboring sites, at the median nearest-neighbor spacing of 4.3 km, those odds typically differ by a factor of about 2.4. At the median distance between pairs of sites, 36.6 km, they differ by a factor of about 11, and across the full extent of the sample, 79.1 km, by a factor of about 23. These factors come from the model's own scale, the logarithm of the odds, which is measured in units called logits. On that scale, the standard deviation of the difference between two sites is 0.89 logits (90 percent interval 0.70 to 1.10) at 4.3 km, 2.37 (1.91 to 2.85) at 36.6 km, and 3.12 (2.51 to 3.79) at 79.1 km, and each factor is the exponential of the corresponding median. Sites a few kilometers apart are therefore expected to resemble one another, and sites at opposite ends of the basin are expected to differ greatly, with no boundary between them. All three fits pass every sampler diagnostic (Supplemental Text).

Two descriptive summaries that do not use the model show the same organization. The first axis of a correspondence analysis, which captures the largest share of the differences among the assemblages, tracks latitude (rank correlation +0.77; latitude and longitude together account for 63 percent of its variance). The two earliest dated assemblages share one end of the axis with the latest, so on the evidence of the four dated assemblages the axis is not an ordering by date.

The 28 sites constrain how different two sites are expected to be, not the scale and the range of the smooth surface separately, which is why we report the share and the contrast (Supplemental Text). The share also does not by itself distinguish a smooth surface from social units aligned with geography. If units existed and their members lived near one another, each unit's shared composition would appear at 28 sampled sites as one region of a smooth surface, and the surface would account for it. A high degree of sharing helps explain why phases appear as groups. It leaves open whether anything beyond geography structures the record, which the models for bounded units address.

The phase boundaries cross the areas of the region that are least occupied. We reconstructed boundaries by assigning each point on the map to its nearest analyzed assemblage and merging territories by phase. We then measured the density of late pre-contact settlement along them, using the 53 sites in the regional compilation that lie more than a kilometer from any analyzed assemblage. At a 5 km bandwidth, density along the phase boundaries is 0.56 sites per 100 km², compared with 1.01 at the assemblages themselves. Of 300 divisions with phases centered randomly, 94 percent cross denser settlement than the phase boundaries do, as do 100 percent of 300 compact divisions (Figure 4B). At a 3 km bandwidth, the shares are 98 and 100 percent, and at 8 km they fall to 89 and 86 percent (Supplemental Text). Settlement concentrates along the larger rivers, and the boundaries lie in the stretches between the concentrations (Supplemental Text, Table S3).

These three results answer the first question. Culture historians saw phases in these data because pottery composition is organized by location, settlement is clumped along the rivers, and sorting assemblages by proximity and resemblance places boundaries in the gaps. None of this demonstrates restrictions on who copied whom.

### Is there evidence of social boundaries beyond geography?

The archaeological record of the St. Francis basin does not determine the neutral model's parameters. Of the combinations we ran, 498 match all three diversity summaries, and the record gives no basis for choosing among them. The two operating points we use with every comparison differ in kernel, in population size by a factor of about 17 (2,000 and 120 learners per site), and in innovation rate by a factor of 12 (0.002 and 0.024). A result that holds at both does not depend on those three choices. It can still depend on the interaction length (6 km) and the mixing rate (0.4), which are the same at both points.

The difference between the two spatial clusters is small. The between-cluster cultural *F*~ST~ is 0.0062, meaning that about 0.6 percent of the decorated diversity lies between the clusters and the rest lies within them. Neutral copying can produce a difference this large, but only under some combinations of settings.

We compared the observed value with the neutral band in 30 cells, each cell being one operating point at one setting of occupation spread or regional drift. The observed value falls within the band in 17 of the 30. That count makes neutral copying look more capable of producing the observed difference than it is. We chose the two operating points in part because the *F*~ST~ they produce is close to the observed value, and that choice favors parameter combinations that produce unusually large differences between the clusters. Among the 498 combinations that match the record's diversity, the two points rank 25th and 11th by median *F*~ST~ over six runs each. Overall, the 498 combinations give a different result. In 436 of them (88 percent), the observed value exceeds the 90th percentile of the combination's six runs. In only 8, it is at or below the combination's median (Supplemental Text). Two statements therefore hold at the scale of the two clusters. Some neutral settings reproduce the observed difference, so the record is consistent with neutral copying. Most neutral settings produce a smaller difference than the record shows. Because both hold, this comparison neither shows nor excludes a boundary.

The three common decorated classes vary between sites far more than neutral copying alone predicts (Table 1). The statistic is *F*, which is near zero when a class has about the same share at every site and grows as its share differs from site to site (Methods). It is 0.165 for Parkin Punctated, 0.117 for the pooled Barton, Kent, and Mound Place Incised, and 0.199 for the painted types. Under neutral copying, its median is 0.007 to 0.018 at both operating points and at every occupation-spread setting. The seven rare classes, by contrast, occur at about as many sites as neutral copying predicts. At the primary operating point without drift, five of the seven lie inside the neutral band, Fortune Noded occurs at fewer sites than the band, and Hull Engraved at more (Table 1). Which rare classes fall outside the band changes from cell to cell (Supplemental Text, Table S6). A difference between common and rare classes is not in itself a departure from neutral copying, because the simulated common classes also show a higher *F* than the simulated rare classes. The departure is how far the common classes' *F* lies above their simulated bands.

Change over time could also produce differences between sites. If the regional mix of designs drifted while the sites were occupied, sites occupied at different times would differ without any boundary between them. The drift amplitude measures how far that mix drifts over the simulated occupation (Methods). At the amplitudes first tested, regional drift does not remove the difference. At the largest of them, 0.3, the neutral medians of the common classes' *F* reach only 0.029 to 0.035 at the primary operating point and 0.033 to 0.041 at the secondary, and the upper limits of the neutral bands at the primary point reach 0.052 to 0.111. The record's 0.117 to 0.199 lies above every band (Figure 6). Drift also has a cost. As the regional mix drifts, rare classes disappear from the simulated region, so amplitudes that bring the simulation closer to the common classes lose rare classes that are present in the record.

Three ways remain by which neutral copying could still produce the variation in the common classes. Regional drift could have been larger than the amplitudes tested above. The sites could have been occupied at different times, in an order related to their location. New types could have entered the region during the occupation. We examined all three in two further sets of simulations, each covering several hundred combinations of settings (Supplemental Text, Tables S7 and S8).

Larger drift can reproduce the common classes, but rarely. At amplitudes of 0.7 and 0.9, all three common-class *F* values lie inside their neutral bands in 5 of 504 combinations at the primary operating point, and in 45 of the 496 combinations at the secondary point for which bands could be computed. Those same combinations do not reproduce the rare classes, because the simulated records hold the rare classes at fewer sites than the record does.

Allowing new types to enter the region comes closer. The common classes and at least five of the seven rare classes then lie inside their bands together in 36 of 300 combinations at the primary point and 35 of 300 at the secondary, all at amplitudes of 0.5 or more. Those combinations differ from the record in two other ways. They contain many more types: their median number of types ranges from 18.5 to 333.5 at the primary point and from 26 to 302 at the secondary, compared with ten classes in the record. Most of their commonest types also first appeared during the simulated occupation.

The timing of occupation matters for one feature, the latitude trend of the painted class. That class is mainly Old Town Red, Nodena Red and White, and Avenue Polychrome, and among the 28 assemblages analyzed here its share of the decorated sherds falls toward the north. In the 1940 collections, where we can separate those three types, nine-tenths of their sherds are Old Town Red (751 of 834), and Nodena Red and White occurs at one site, Hollywood, in the south of the sample (Supplemental Text). The trend is therefore mainly that of Old Town Red. The sample does not include the Nodena phase area north of the basin, so this trend describes the sampled sites and not the valley as a whole. No combination in which occupation dates are independent of location reproduces that trend. When dates are ordered strictly by latitude, 91 of 144 combinations reproduce it at the primary point and 82 of 144 at the secondary. Only four of the 28 assemblages are dated, so the record cannot show whether latitude-ordered occupation occurred.

No tested combination reproduces all of these features together: the variation of the common classes, the site counts of at least five rare classes, the number of types, and the spatial pattern. The record measures neither the amplitude of drift nor the dates at which types first appeared, so it cannot show whether the conditions of the closest combinations held. In summary, copying with no boundary of any kind does not produce the variation of the three common classes at drift amplitudes up to 0.3. At larger amplitudes, it does so only in combinations that fail to reproduce other features of the record. The extra variation of the common classes therefore remains unexplained by the neutral settings we tested. That is not evidence for a boundary between phases. A departure from neutral copying shows that something other than distance-dependent copying acted on those classes. It does not show what.

We also asked whether this comparison would detect a moderate difference if one were present. To test this, we added a known change, the marker described in Methods, to simulated records. Doubling the odds of one common class in one of the two spatial clusters placed that class outside the neutral band in 23 to 45 of 50 marker records, at drift amplitudes up to 0.1. Tripling the odds did so in 37 to 50 of 50. For comparison, 10 of 50 neutral records fall outside their own band (Figure 6). Smaller changes, and a change in a rare class, were detected less often or not at all (Supplemental Text). The comparison can therefore detect a moderate departure in a common class.

The departure is not an artifact of merged collections. In the 1940 survey material alone, the common classes' *F* values at the 27 matched sites are 0.151, 0.087, and 0.195. For the current counts on the same 27 sites, they are 0.145, 0.076, and 0.195 (Table 1). Collection source does matter for a site's two collections and for the presence of rare classes. Wallace Incised, for example, is absent from every 1940 collection among the 28, so its presence pattern partly reflects which sites were revisited (Supplemental Text). Two correlations between a class's share and collection size also persist in the 1940 material, and no neutral generator we ran reproduces them (Supplemental Text).

Rim attributes recorded on 19 of the sites also exceed the neutral bands. Interior beveling (*F* 0.105) and exterior notching (0.069) lie above every neutral band on the 19 sites. They exceed the band's upper limit in every cell, including cells computed without the one site with fewer than 45 rims. The smallest excesses are 0.0512 for beveling and 0.0395 for notching, both at the secondary operating point (Figure 7A; Supplemental Text). Lip form (0.031) exceeds the widest band by 0.0001, and a reconstruction of Mainfort's lip percentages to within one rim contains that edge, so the lip result is not resolved by the record. The three common decorated classes at the same 19 sites also lie above their bands (0.107, 0.044, and 0.208).

The rims and the decoration, however, show no shared pattern. Their directions of change across the basin are mixed. Beveling and notching fall with latitude (posterior medians of the rank correlation: -0.58, 90 percent interval -0.68 to -0.47, and -0.39, -0.53 to -0.25) and rise with longitude (+0.30 and +0.51). Parkin Punctated rises with latitude (+0.65, 0.58 to 0.72), the painted types fall with it strongly (-0.74, -0.78 to -0.70), and the pooled incised classes are weakly positive (+0.16, 0.02 to 0.29; Figure 7B). Beveling and notching thus change in the same direction as the painted types and opposite to Parkin Punctated. After linear adjustment for rank-based distance, pairwise differences in each rim attribute and pairwise total-variation differences in decoration correlate weakly. The medians lie between -0.07 and +0.14, and the intervals for beveling and notching against the current counts include zero (Figure 7C).

The rims are a second line of evidence for structure, and their relation to the phases as drawn is indirect. How far the rims Mainfort tabulated overlap physically with the 1940 survey collections is unknown. Phillips, Ford, and Griffin [-@phillips_ford_griffin_1951] did not tabulate these three attributes by site, although their type descriptions mention rim beveling and the sherds Phillips sorted almost certainly included them. Mainfort's [-@mainfort_2003_rims, 37] own account makes interior beveling spatial as well as temporal, so we cannot conclude a simple chronological inference from it.

These results answer our second question: whether the data show evidence of social boundaries. The record contains no evidence of social boundaries between the phases beyond the geographic distribution of settlement. Two observations support that answer. First, the two spatial clusters do not differ in decoration by more than neutral copying can produce, although most of its settings produce less. Second, the variation that does exceed neutral copying does not mark a common boundary. Under the convergence criterion, a boundary should produce a departure that the decorated classes and the rim attributes share, and the two show no shared pattern. This answer has a limit. It rests on the criterion's assumption that the same learners, connected by the same networks, carried both sets of traits. The record therefore shows no evidence of social boundaries at least with these ceramic artifact classes, though it does not show that none existed.

### What would it take to detect a boundary?

A model built to detect bounded units is useful only if it reports a boundary when one is present and none when one is absent. We tested both models for this before interpreting either on the record. For each model, we simulated records at the 28 site locations in which the size of the difference between units was known, including records with no units. We then fitted the model to each simulated record and compared its estimate with the value we had set. Neither model passed.

The barrier model's sampler passed every diagnostic check, but its estimates on records simulated with no barrier overlapped its estimates on records simulated with a barrier of 1.0 (Figure 8B). An estimate from the record could therefore come from either kind of history. The latent-unit model failed for a further reason. Its sampler failed the diagnostic checks in 12 of its 14 recovery fits (Figure 8A), so we could not rely on its estimates even when they lay near the true value. We therefore report no estimate from either model. The Supplemental Text provides estimates from each recovery fit, sampler diagnostics, and the barrier model's results under other priors and range settings (Table S5).

For the barrier model, the site arrangement is the plausible limit. The 28 assemblages are located in clusters separated by gaps of occupied but uncollected or sparsely occupied ground. A composition that changes gradually across the basin, sampled only at the clusters, is largely observed as differences between clusters. A composition that steps at the edge of a bounded unit located in the same gap produces a similar difference (Figure 2). We have not tested recovery on a sample that includes sites in the gaps, so this explanation is plausible but not demonstrated. A failed recovery also does not mean the two histories are equally likely.

These results answer the third question in two parts. With this sample, neither model reliably detects a boundary of the sizes we simulated, so the absence of evidence reported above does not rule one out. Detecting a boundary would take three kinds of data this record lacks. Assemblages from the settlement gaps are the first, because a gradient predicts intermediate compositions there and a step does not. Dated or stratified contexts are the second, because they would show change along the sequence, which surface collections do not. The third is trait systems recorded separately from the decorated types, where a shared departure from neutral copying could be sought. Any model applied to such data would also need to recover a known boundary on records simulated at the new site locations before it is fitted to the record.

## Discussion

### Three senses in which the phases could be real

The question of whether the phases are real covers three claims, and these data answer them differently (Table 2). As a geographic pattern in pottery composition, the phases are real. Location organizes composition, the map alone recovers the scheme, and the boundaries lie where settlement is sparsest. This is the pattern the culture historians noticed, and their expectation that it would be there was correct. As bounded units with edges, the phases are not resolved in either direction. The two models we tested do not reliably recover known edges at these sites, so these analyses neither show nor exclude a step at the gaps. As social units, which is what the culture historians meant and what this study set out to test, the phases are not demonstrated. Our standard for that claim is that independent signatures converge, and the two signatures that could be assessed on this record do not.

**Table 2.** The three senses in which the phases could be real, and what the record shows for each.

| Sense | Measured basis | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| A geographic pattern in pottery composition | Spatial share 0.93 to 0.94 across three priors (Figure 5); coordinates alone recover the phases for 24 of 26 assemblages; boundaries in the least-occupied ground | Yes |
| Bounded units with edges | The latent-unit and barrier models do not reliably recover known structure at these 28 sites | Not resolved by these two models on this sample, in either direction |
| Social units, under the convergence criterion | Between-cluster *F*~ST~ of 0.0062 is within what neutral copying can produce, though above most diversity-matched combinations; rims and decoration show no shared pattern | Not demonstrated |

Phases are then best understood as what their makers said they were, divisions of continuous variability drawn at the gaps in a clumped settlement pattern. McNutt [-@mcnutt_1996, 250] wrote of the southern phases that "the distinctions that one tries so desperately to make between the phases involved are little more than comments on local elaborations—ceramic drift, if you will—of the basic pottery complexes possessed by peoples involved in this expansion." McNutt, Franklin, and Henry [-@mcnutt_franklin_henry_2012, 247] describe the Walls phase as "a developmental lineage" spanning roughly two centuries, so that a phase's members need not even be contemporaneous. Our results give that interpretation a measured basis and also mark its limit. Mainfort's [-@mainfort_2003, 180] observation that phase members lie "in the general vicinity of each other in ordination space" without forming clusters reflects a geographic pattern sampled at settlement concentrations. Proximity on the ground accounts for proximity in the ordination.

### What the record shows beyond geography

The individual types carry most of what the record shows beyond geography. The three common classes vary between sites far more than neutral copying among contemporaneous sites produces, the seven rare classes mostly do not, and the departure is directional: within the sampled sites, Parkin Punctated increases with latitude, and the painted types decrease. These are departures of single types along a gradient, not a shared change at a phase edge, so they do not make the phases bounded units. The cause is not established. Four candidates are possible. The first is differences in how collections were made or classified. The 1940-only comparison makes this less likely but does not eliminate it, because several field parties made the 1940 collections. The second is directed change over time that is not drift, at sites occupied at different times, which these collections lack the temporal information to test. The third is neutral copying under conditions the record does not measure, namely large regional drift with new types entering during the occupation. The fourth is spatial structure in the sense the culture historians meant. Only this one would indicate a social boundary, though decoration alone does not separate it from the other three.

Rim attributes do not resolve this. The attribute scale could register differences in learning more readily than the type scale. If it did, the rims might show larger departures than the types. However, they do not. Beveling and notching depart from neutral copying by margins comparable to those of the common classes. That comparison does not test sensitivity, because the rim sample is smaller and we have not measured what size of departure a boundary would produce at either scale. The rims' directions of change match some decorated classes and oppose others. We therefore interpret the rims as a second line of evidence that suggests structure, not as evidence for or against a boundary.

### Parkin

Parkin was the basin's largest town, with one of its tallest mounds, and its prominence is unquestioned. Decorated frequencies do not, however, establish a boundary in ceramic learning at the edge of the Parkin phase. The phase lies near the northern of the two spatial clusters the site layout supports, and the difference between those clusters is one that distance-dependent copying can produce, though most tested settings produce less. The mound-height distribution and regional evidence on hoe exchange are described in the Supplemental Text (Figure S2), and neither requires restricting the phase to its edge. Other evidence for a Parkin polity addresses political organization and not the ceramic boundary. The region's own history offers a precedent for separating the two. Coosa, a well-documented Mississippian paramount chiefdom, spanned two ceramic traditions and site clusters separable at the phase level, and political ties rather than shared material culture united them [@anderson_1996, 250-251]. If the polity the De Soto expedition met was at Parkin, the evidence for it lies in ceremony, fortification, and settlement size.

### Limits

Four limits of this study are findings, not caveats. The first is site geometry. The latent-unit and barrier models did not reliably separate steps from gradients in this sample. The clustered geometry is a plausible explanation, not a demonstrated cause, and other models remain untested. The second is chronology. Only four of 28 sites have radiocarbon medians. The seriation order is built from these frequencies and tracks latitude, and no analysis here tests change along the sequence. The third is the neutral model, whose parameters the record does not determine. A result that holds at both operating points can still depend on the interaction length and the mixing rate, which the two share. The fourth is time-averaging, which reduces differences between assemblages (Data). Every simulated record has accumulation spans of random start and length, so the reported bands include their effects under the modeled conditions.

### Requirements for other cases and for further data

Interpreting similarity as evidence of a bounded social unit has two requirements that apply beyond this case. First, the pattern must exceed what distance-dependent interaction on the actual settlement map produces. The comparison must be made at matched diversity, because a neutral model run at the wrong diversity is easily credited with structure it cannot produce. A localized neutral process is likewise easily mistaken for biased copying when diversity is taken at face value [@schneider_martins_deaguiar_2016; @premo_2016; @premo_scholnick_2011]. Second, before a model's boundary-detection results on a record are interpreted, the model must recover a known boundary on records simulated at the same sites. The second requirement changed our conclusions. Had we fitted the latent-unit model to the record without the recovery test, we would have reported a posterior for the between-unit scale that the recovery test shows does not distinguish a record with units from one without. Together with the convergence criterion, these requirements give a way to evaluate social interpretations of artifact variation within evolutionary archaeology [@dunnell_1995; @neiman_1995; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_2015].

Dated contexts would do more than show change along the sequence. They would show whether the common classes' extra variation is directed change over time at sites occupied at different times. The same dates would test two properties of the neutral combinations that came closest to the record: occupation ordered by latitude, and the first appearance of most of the commonest types during the occupation. The record shows departures of a size the neutral comparison can detect on this sample. What is missing is independent evidence to attribute them to a cause.

## Conclusion

Phases continue to organize the archaeological record of the Lower Mississippi Valley, and their use as social units makes a claim their definition does not: that similar pottery at nearby sites identifies a community and, at Parkin, a political territory. The St. Francis basin phases appear to be groups of people because pottery composition is organized by location, settlement is clumped along the rivers, and sorting assemblages by proximity and resemblance draws boundaries in the gaps. Between the basin's two spatial clusters, decorated frequencies differ by an amount neutral copying can produce, though most parameter combinations matching the record's diversity produce less. The three common decorated classes vary between sites more than neutral copying produces, except in tested combinations that fail to match the record's other features. Two of the three separately recorded rim attributes, interior beveling and exterior notching, also exceed every neutral band computed for them. The two trait systems show no shared pattern, and the cause of that variation is not established. The phases are real as a geographic pattern, but the two models tested on this sample did not resolve them as bounded units, and the evidence does not demonstrate them as social units. Phase membership alone is weak evidence for a bounded learning community, and the inference from one phase to one polity is not warranted on its own. Resolving the question requires assemblages from the settlement gaps, dated or stratified contexts, and trait systems recorded independently of the units under test. Those are the data the culture historians' hypothesis has always needed.
