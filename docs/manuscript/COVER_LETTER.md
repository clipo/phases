We submit for consideration in *American Antiquity* our manuscript, "Explaining the Spatial Structure of Phases: An Example from the Lower Mississippi River Valley."

The phase is one of archaeology's foundational units, and across much of North America it is read as a bounded, interacting society. Earlier work, including our own, showed that the Late Mississippian phases of the lower Mississippi Valley do not separate cleanly. None of it explained what the phases are. That is what this paper does, by asking two questions: what explains the phases, and does the Parkin phase, long read as the polity of Casqui that the De Soto expedition met in 1541, show any evidence in its decorated-type frequencies that sets it apart as one?

The answer to the first rests on simple cultural transmission theory. Potters copy near neighbors more than distant ones, so neutral drift makes nearby assemblages alike whether or not groups exist; settlement is clumped; and a smooth gradient sampled at clumps and sorted by resemblance and proximity looks like a set of groups. A phase has the qualities of a group only if its frequencies show what distance-limited copying cannot produce. Testing this on the St. Francis basin with a copying model calibrated to the assemblages' own diversity, we find that the site map alone recovers the phases, arbitrary lines separate the pottery better than the phase lines do, and pottery simulated with no groups sorts into the phases as readily as the real pottery. The pottery does vary over shorter distances than the copying model produces, and we report that honestly, but that variation does not follow the phase lines. The answer to the second question is that nothing in the pottery sets the Parkin phase apart: it differs from the rest of the basin no more than copying without groups produces and no more than arbitrary divisions of the map. A single line gives these tests little power, so we say that the frequencies do not require a polity rather than that they exclude one.

The paper joins a methodological contribution to a substantive case in lower Mississippi Valley archaeology, and we believe it suits the readership of *American Antiquity*. The manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and has not been published previously. All authors have approved the submission and declare no conflict of interest.

All data and code needed to reproduce the analyses and figures are openly available at https://github.com/clipo/phases. The repository regenerates every result and figure from the raw data through a single script, and each analysis step is documented, so the work is ready for the journal's reproducibility review.

We thank you for considering our work and look forward to the reviewers' comments.
