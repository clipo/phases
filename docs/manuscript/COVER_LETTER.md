We submit for consideration in *American Antiquity* our manuscript, "Explaining the Spatial Structure of Phases: An Example from the Lower Mississippi River Valley."

The phase is one of archaeology's foundational units, and across much of North America it is often read as a bounded, interacting society. Earlier work, including our own, showed that the Late Mississippian phases of the lower Mississippi Valley do not separate cleanly. None of it explained what produces their apparent organization. This paper offers and tests such an explanation by asking two questions: what explains the phases, and does the Parkin phase, long read as the polity of Casqui that the De Soto expedition met in 1541, show any evidence in its decorated-type frequencies that sets it apart?

The answer to the first rests on simple cultural transmission theory. Potters copy near neighbors more than distant ones, so neutral drift makes nearby assemblages alike whether or not groups exist; settlement is clumped; and a smooth gradient sampled at clumps and sorted by resemblance and proximity looks like a set of groups. A phase marks a restriction on the copying of decorated pottery only if its frequencies show what distance-limited copying does not produce. Testing this on the St. Francis basin with a copying model calibrated to the assemblages' own diversity, we find that the site map alone recovers the phases, alternative spatial divisions of the same sites capture as much or more of the ceramic differentiation, and pottery simulated with no groups sorts into the phases as readily as the real pottery. The inherited phases therefore do not uniquely capture the ceramic differentiation, and spatially structured copying is a plausible account of their apparent organization. We then apply the same tests to the phases Mainfort redrew in 2003 from an ordination of the pottery, as a contrast: lines drawn from the pottery follow the pottery, whereas the inherited lines follow the map. The pottery does vary over shorter distances than the calibrated model produces, which we report as incompletely explained, and that variation does not follow the phase lines. On the second question, the Parkin phase differs from the rest of the basin no more than arbitrary divisions of the map. A single line gives these tests little power, and political boundaries need not follow ceramic traditions, so we say that the frequencies do not require a polity rather than that they exclude one.

The paper joins a methodological contribution to a substantive case in lower Mississippi Valley archaeology, and we believe it suits the readership of *American Antiquity*. The manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and has not been published previously. All authors have approved the submission and declare no conflict of interest.

All data and code needed to reproduce the analyses and figures are openly available at https://github.com/clipo/phases. The repository regenerates every result and figure from the raw data through a single script, and each analysis step is documented, so the work is ready for the journal's reproducibility review.

We thank you for considering our work and look forward to the reviewers' comments.
