We submit for consideration in *American Antiquity* our manuscript, "Explaining the Spatial Structure of Phases: An Example from the Lower Mississippi River Valley."

The phase is one of archaeology's foundational units, and across much of North America it is read as a bounded, interacting society. That reading rests on a silent premise, that assemblages more similar to their near neighbors than to distant ones record a social group. The premise is rarely tested, and it has a strong alternative. Neutral cultural transmission on a spatially structured population produces the same clustering with no bounded groups at all.

Our paper offers an operational criterion for distinguishing a genuine bounded interaction group from spatially structured drift, and demonstrates it on the late pre-contact decorated-ceramic record of the lower Mississippi Valley. We compare drift and bounded-groups processes generatively on the real geography of the assemblages, and we calibrate the comparison to the record's small samples and time-averaging with a signal-recovery experiment. That calibration is the transferable contribution. Before any similarity structure is interpreted as social, a record must be shown able to distinguish groups from drift at all.

Applied to the St. Francis basin and the Parkin phase, often interpreted as the seat of the Casqui paramountcy the De Soto expedition encountered in 1541, the record shows no closure of interaction into bounded groups. Drift on the basin's geography reproduces the observed structure, Parkin is a transmission bridge rather than a capital, and the named phases are largely indistinguishable from spatially structured drift at the scale at which they are drawn. The settlement and exchange records, and an independent ordination of the same assemblages, point the same way.

The paper joins a methodological contribution to a substantive case in central Mississippi Valley archaeology, and we believe it suits the readership of *American Antiquity*. The manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and has not been published previously. All authors have approved the submission and declare no conflict of interest.

All data and code needed to reproduce the analyses and figures are openly available at https://github.com/clipo/phases. The repository regenerates every result and figure from the raw data through a single script, and each analysis step is documented, so the work is ready for the journal's reproducibility review.

We thank you for considering our work and look forward to the reviewers' comments.
