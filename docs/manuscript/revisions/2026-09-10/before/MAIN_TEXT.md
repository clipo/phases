# Are the phases real? Testing bounded interaction against spatially structured drift in Mississippi Valley decorated ceramics

**Carl P. Lipo, Robert J. DiNapoli, and Mark E. Madsen**

## Abstract

Phases organize assemblages by similarity, space, and time, but interpreting them as societies requires additional evidence. Neutral transmission on a spatial network can also generate patterned similarity. We examine decorated ceramics from the St. Francis basin and the wider Mississippi Valley using deterministic frequency seriation, variance partitioning, and simulations matched to observed class counts and assemblage sizes. An injection experiment identifies sensitivity to increasing divergence but does not establish an absence of bounded groups. A fixed-class copying model compares distance-structured interaction with boundary restriction, under accumulation windows and alternative chronological orientations. The revised baseline underpredicts differentiation, and agreement depends on model and observation assumptions. The analyses distinguish a model's ability to generate clustering from its ability to reproduce a particular archaeological contrast. Ceramic similarity alone neither establishes a polity nor demonstrates its absence. Interpreting phases socially requires explicit observation models, matched alternatives, and uncertainty about how interaction shapes the record.

**Keywords:** cultural transmission, neutral models, frequency seriation, isolation by distance, Mississippian archaeology, Parkin phase

## Resumen

Las fases arqueológicas organizan conjuntos según su similitud, distribución espacial y cronología, pero su interpretación como sociedades requiere un argumento adicional. La transmisión neutral en una red espacial también puede producir similitudes estructuradas. Examinamos cerámicas decoradas de la cuenca del St. Francis y del valle del Misisipi mediante seriación de frecuencias, partición de la variación y simulaciones ajustadas al número de clases y al tamaño de los conjuntos observados. Un experimento de inyección evalúa la sensibilidad a una divergencia creciente, sin demostrar la ausencia de grupos delimitados. Un modelo de copia con clases fijas compara la interacción dependiente de la distancia con distintos grados de restricción entre grupos, ventanas de acumulación explícitas y orientaciones cronológicas alternativas. El modelo de referencia revisado subestima la diferenciación, y la concordancia depende de los supuestos del modelo y de observación. La similitud cerámica, por sí sola, no establece la existencia de una entidad política ni demuestra su ausencia. Interpretar socialmente las fases exige modelos explícitos de observación, alternativas comparables e incertidumbre sobre la relación entre interacción y registro arqueológico.

## Introduction

The late pre-contact lower Mississippi Valley is conventionally divided into phases such as Parkin, Nodena, Kent, Walls, Tipton, Jones Bayou, and Parchman. These units organize variation in ceramic assemblages within geographic and chronological limits [@phillips_ford_griffin_1951; @willey_phillips_1958; @mainfort_2003]. Their further interpretation as bounded societies is an empirical proposition. Similarity among neighboring assemblages may reflect restricted interaction within a community, but it may also arise when copying declines continuously with distance. Distinguishing these explanations requires more than recognizing a cluster.

Parkin makes the distinction consequential. Its platform mound, fortification, and identification with Casqui in accounts of the De Soto expedition have supported its interpretation as a political center [@morse_1990; @mitchem_2001; @hudson_smith_depratter_1990]. Those sources remain relevant evidence about political organization. The narrower question addressed here is whether the decorated ceramics independently require a bounded interaction group corresponding to the Parkin phase. A test of that proposition cannot, by itself, determine whether Parkin was a capital or whether authority operated through institutions that ceramic decoration fails to register.

HydroRIVERS provides the mapped river network [@lehner_grill_2013]. Parkin is starred.](../../figures/fig1_phases.png){width=3.7in}

The distinction between classification and social explanation has a substantial regional history. Fox [-@fox_1998] questioned the statistical coherence of southeast-Missouri phases; Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003] found overlapping rather than discrete groups in an ordination of late-period decorated assemblages. Lipo [-@lipo_2001] used frequency seriation to examine interaction structure without identifying every co-seriable subset with a society. These studies show why culture-historical units require evaluation, but a clustering result alone does not identify the process responsible for the overlap or separation.

Cultural-transmission models provide explicit alternatives. Unbiased copying in finite populations produces drift, and geographically restricted exchange can preserve local differences without an imposed social boundary [@neiman_1995; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_hunt_1997; @lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021]. Frequency-dependent copying or reduced transmission across a boundary can also differentiate populations [@henrich_boyd_1998; @crema_kandler_shennan_2016]. Because different mechanisms can generate related frequency patterns, identifying a process requires comparing predictions under an observation model appropriate to the deposits [@kandler_shennan_2015; @crema_bortolini_lake_2023].

We examine the St. Francis basin, the wider lower-valley assemblage set, and the southeast-Missouri assemblages of Williams [-@williams_1954]. We first measure the empirical structure and assess sensitivity to an imposed trajectory of increasing divergence. We then compare a finite-class spatial-copying model with versions that restrict copying across specified partitions. These experiments address different questions: whether a statistic responds to a prescribed signal, whether drift produces apparent groups, and whether a given model reproduces the observed degree of differentiation. Keeping those questions separate prevents a successful illustration of clustering from becoming an unwarranted historical conclusion.

## Phases, interaction, and measurement

A phase can be a useful descriptive unit without corresponding to a bounded society. In the space-time-cultural definition of Willey and Phillips [-@willey_phillips_1958], a phase identifies a limited distribution of distinctive traits. A social interpretation adds claims about how individuals interacted and how institutions organized those interactions. Those claims must be tested using evidence that does not merely restate the traits used to define the phase.

Population thinking directs attention to variation and transmission rather than treating the classified type as the historical entity [@mayr_1959; @dunnell_1980]. We represent interaction as copying among locations, potentially intensified within a subset but not necessarily discontinuous at a territorial edge. In this representation, boundaries vary in strength. The distance-only model is one endpoint of a family in which between-group copying becomes progressively less likely. A strong boundary and an arbitrarily weak one cannot be assumed equally distinguishable from drift.

Decorated ceramics are useful because their class frequencies contain information about transmission, but they are counts of accumulated objects rather than observations of learners [@neiman_1995; @crema_bortolini_lake_2023]. Classes are archaeological measurement categories, not necessarily individual innovations. A newly made decorative variant may fall within an existing class. Our revised spatial model therefore uses a fixed repertoire matching the measured columns and allows recurrent innovation within it. This is an explicit modeling choice, not a claim that the historical repertoire never changed. Its adequacy is assessed against the data and through sensitivity analysis.

Time-averaging is part of the observation process. Surface collections combine production over uncertain spans, and their frequency distributions need not match a living population at a single moment [@madsen_2012; @miller_atkins_premo_2018; @holdaway_wandsnider_2008]. Modeling accumulation allows that distinction to be explored. It does not establish the actual duration of a deposit, nor does a specified simulation window prove that a negative finding reflects the absence of a historical process.

We consider four candidate measurements (Table 1). They share a frequency substrate and are not assumed statistically independent. Agreement among them may be informative within a specified model comparison, but it is not a universal criterion identifying bounded groups. A boundary need not produce conformity, and neutral spatial drift need not preserve perfectly unimodal class histories.

**Table 1.** Candidate measurements and the inferential limits evaluated here.

| Measurement | Operational statistic | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Neutrality departure | Mean absolute deviation of the homozygosity/richness parameter ratio from one | Finite class aggregation, sample size, pooling, and time-averaging can affect the ratio |
| Seriation fragmentation | Maximal deterministic co-seriable groups; within-bin group count per assemblage | Depends on continuity threshold, sampling error, and the history represented by a subset |
| Between-group differentiation | Gini-Simpson cultural $F_{ST}$ | Can arise through drift; depends on the partition and observation model |
| Spatial boundary excess | Within-minus-between similarity at comparable distance | Finite spatial drift can generate excess; labels and distance matching matter |

## Materials and methods

### Assemblages and chronology

The underlying Phillips-Ford-Griffin compilation contains 266 surface assemblages [@phillips_ford_griffin_1951; @lipo_2001]. The curated decorated-class dataset contains 55 georeferenced assemblages and ten classes. Our St. Francis basin subset comprises 29 assemblages within the drainage corridor used in the preceding regional analyses. The operational corridor includes assemblages within 20 km of the St. Francis, Tyronza, and L'Anguille system. It is a geographic sampling frame, not a claim that its edge was a social boundary. The basin assemblages contain 50–4,798 decorated sherds. Spatial comparisons elsewhere in the paper use all 55 curated assemblages or the Mississippian-class subset of Williams's southeast-Missouri survey [@williams_1954].

The wider-valley Parkin comparison uses the eight assemblages assigned to the Parkin phase against 47 assigned to other phases after Mainfort. This named partition differs from both the basin sampling frame and the clusters detected from ceramic similarity. The southeast-Missouri extension retains assemblages with at least 30 sherds in classes other than the designated Woodland classes, producing 39 assemblages from 24 coordinate locations and 42 classes. This retained repertoire includes plain as well as decorated Mississippian classes, so it is an auxiliary regional comparison rather than a replication on identical decorated categories. Multiple assemblages from a site share coordinates but remain separate observation nodes in this exploratory model. That treatment is an additional limitation on the independence of the regional comparison.

We order the basin assemblages on the first correspondence-analysis axis of decorated-class frequencies (Figure 2). Radiocarbon dates compiled by Mainfort [-@mainfort_2001], calibrated against IntCal20 [@reimer_etal_2020], provide a limited external check. Five proveniences can be matched to the curated sequence; their pooled median calibrated ages correlate with the axis at Spearman $\rho=+0.70$. This small sample supports a possible temporal interpretation without establishing that the axis is exclusively chronological. Geography and composition can also contribute to its ordering [@mainfort_2003; @smith_neiman_2007].

![**Figure 2.** Correspondence analysis of the 29 basin assemblages. Color indicates rank on CA1 in the radiocarbon-supported orientation; Parkin is starred. Distances in this plot summarize composition and are not direct measures of calendar time or political affiliation.](../../figures/fig3_ca_ordination.png){width=5in}

The recovery experiment conditions on six ordinal bins of that axis. Its signed trend reverses if the axis is reversed. The spatial simulations compare both orientations and a contemporaneous observation scenario. The latter samples all sites at the terminal recorded slice while retaining the same accumulation-window length. Neither the orientation sensitivity nor the sparse radiocarbon anchor resolves uncertainty in the actual separation of occupations.

### Measuring differentiation and seriation

For a partition into groups, cultural $F_{ST}$ is $(H_T-H_S)/H_T$, where $H_T$ is Gini-Simpson diversity in the pooled counts and $H_S$ is mean within-group diversity weighted by group sherd totals [@bell_richerson_mcelreath_2009]. After rarefaction to a common count per assemblage, those totals are proportional to assemblage number. Otherwise they weight the observed sherd contribution. The estimate describes differentiation in these measured classes; its magnitude is not a universal scale of political integration.

We distinguish three partitions. Spatial clusters are obtained by $k$-means on coordinates, selecting the greatest mean silhouette among the candidate values. The recovery uses $k=2$ through 6, selecting three basin clusters. The spatial simulations use $k=2$ through 4 for each regional set. Ceramic communities are detected by greedy modularity on the complete Brainerd-Robinson similarity graph. The named Parkin partition is fixed by phase assignment. Each statistic is compared with simulations using the same partition or community-detection operation as the observations. When community detection returns one group, its between-group $F_{ST}$ is zero and the run remains in the distribution.

Deterministic frequency seriation searches for orderings in which every class is unimodal and adjacent assemblages share at least one class. We also require that no adjacent per-class frequency jump exceed 0.10. The algorithm enumerates maximal co-seriable groups by growing valid orders and reports any search truncation [@lipo_madsen_dunnell_2015]. It implements a deterministic criterion; the bootstrap significance layer that distinguishes sampling error from frequency reversals is not implemented. For the whole basin we report group sizes and overlapping membership. Within each recovery bin we compute the number of maximal groups divided by the number of assemblages. This is an order-search statistic, distinct from the fixed-order violation proxy used in an earlier idealized experiment reported in the supplement.

The remaining diagnostics are the mean absolute neutrality departure within spatial clusters and distance-controlled boundary excess. The former compares homozygosity- and richness-based estimates of the neutral transmission parameter [@neiman_1995; @ewens_1972]. The latter summarizes within-minus-between Brainerd-Robinson similarity at comparable geographic distances. Their equations and limitations are given in the Supplemental Material. Neither is assumed to uniquely identify a boundary.

### Injection sensitivity

Assemblage size covaries with basin CA position, making raw trends potentially sensitive to sample size. We therefore draw 50 sherds without replacement from each observed and synthetic assemblage. An assemblage already containing 50 sherds is unchanged. We repeat the empirical draw 400 times and report the mean rank trend and its resampling percentiles. These percentiles condition on the observed assemblages, partition, and order; they do not include uncertainty about deposits, chronology, or model choice.

The injection experiment preserves the 29 assemblages, their totals, spatial assignments, and six bins. It starts with a shared Zipf class-frequency profile. A parameter $s$ controls how strongly cluster-specific profiles diverge and concentrate, with the imposed effect increasing along the bins. At $s=0$, all groups have the same fixed profile and differ only through sampling. This baseline is not an evolving spatial-drift model. At larger $s$, the experiment tests sensitivity to a prescribed increasing-divergence trajectory, not to every form of bounded interaction.

We simulate 120 test datasets at each strength from 0 to 1 in increments of 0.1. A separate set of 400 null draws establishes a one-sided 95th-percentile threshold for the $F_{ST}$ rank trend. The test datasets estimate the achieved false-positive rate and power. We repeat the experiment after averaging production over three successive bins as an assumed time-averaging scenario. The first grid point reaching 80% power is a property of this design. We do not invert that point into an empirical upper bound on group strength.

### Spatial copying and restricted interaction

The central spatial comparison uses finite-population neutral copying on the observed coordinates [@lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021]. Each observation node contains 120 learners. Every generation, a learner copies locally with probability 0.98 and from another node with probability 0.02. External source weights decay exponentially with distance. With probability $\mu$, a learner instead draws uniformly from the fixed set of archaeological classes, including the possibility of drawing its current class. The model is unbiased with respect to class frequency beyond ordinary copying; it does not impose a conformist exponent.

For the basin and wider valley, distance is the shortest path along the mapped river network plus access distances between sites and their snapped nodes. The network construction and its limitations are described in the supplement. The southeast-Missouri extension uses geographic distance. Thus numerical comparisons between regions also involve different transport approximations and should not be read as a controlled experiment on geographic extent alone.

Boundary models use the same update and observation process. Before normalizing a node's external source probabilities, we multiply weights crossing a specified group boundary by $\ell$. The distance-only model has $\ell=1$; smaller values restrict copying across the partition. We compare $\ell=1,0.5,0.1,$ and $0.03$. The basin and southeast-Missouri boundaries follow their spatial clusters. The wider-valley boundary follows Parkin versus other named phases. These are specified alternatives, not estimates of the true boundary locations. The multiplier is not the fraction of all copying events crossing a boundary, since row normalization and local copying also determine that fraction.

The baseline uses interaction length 24 km and $\mu=0.012$. Class frequencies begin uniformly, evolve for 1,200 burn-in generations, and are recorded at 90 points over a further 1,800 generations. Each assemblage pools eight successive recorded slices and is sampled to its observed sherd total. To avoid shorter windows at the start, the earliest ordinal rank maps to the end of the first complete eight-slice window. We run 500 baseline realizations for each region and observe each field contemporaneously, in CA order, and in reversed CA order.

A matched boundary grid crosses the four restriction multipliers with two interaction lengths, 12 and 24 km, and three innovation rates, 0.006, 0.012, and 0.024, using 30 realizations per cell in each region. Mixing remains 0.02; the grid is not an exhaustive nuisance-parameter analysis. Additional basin and wider-valley experiments examine 40 classes, a longer burn-in, and monomorphic initialization with and without burn-in. These controls assess dependence on repertoire and startup assumptions.

We report simulation percentiles and upper-tail Monte Carlo probabilities $(1+e)/(R+1)$, where $e$ is the number of simulated statistics at least as large as observed and $R$ is the number of replicates. The add-one correction prevents zero estimated probabilities. Model-data agreement within an envelope is not a posterior probability that a historical process occurred. With only 30 replicates, grid intervals are coarse sensitivity summaries; baseline tails use 500 replicates.

### Parkin and external evidence

For each wider-valley drift realization we measure $F_{ST}$ for the named Parkin partition. We also summarize how frequently each assemblage joins Parkin's detected ceramic community. These probabilities describe variation across simulated histories, not uncertainty about actual historical phase membership. To examine influence, we remove each Parkin-phase assemblage in turn from the observed and simulated count matrices and recompute its matched contrast. Removal changes the observation set, while the simulated copying network remains the full network. This differs from simulating a past landscape on which that location never existed.

Settlement and exchange evidence provide context rather than an automatic adjudication. We examine recorded mound heights within the basin because Parkin's mound area is under-recorded in the regional table. For lithic exchange we draw on Cobb's [-@cobb_2000] regional analysis of chert-hoe production and distribution. A graded mound distribution or decentralized production need not exclude political authority, and neither is assumed to independently measure a ceramic interaction boundary.

## Results

### Sensitivity and the empirical trajectory

After without-replacement rarefaction, the mean basin $F_{ST}$ rank trend is +0.02, compared with +0.60 before size control. The 2.5th–97.5th percentiles across rarefactions are -0.30 to +0.60. The difference demonstrates substantial dependence of the raw trajectory on the sampling procedure. It does not identify which historical process generated the counts. Reversing the bins reverses the signed trend.

In the injection experiment, $F_{ST}$ responds most consistently to the imposed increasing divergence (Figure 3). The first grid point exceeding 80% power is 0.6 without averaging and 0.7 under the three-bin averaging scenario. Estimated false-positive rates on independent null test draws are 0.04 and 0.07. Neutrality departure is non-monotonic, boundary excess is weak, and the normalized IDSS group count does not show the hypothesized positive response. The experiment therefore does not validate a four-signature rising-trend criterion on this record.

Seriation is the maximal deterministic IDSS group count per assemblage within each bin. Right: power of the $F_{ST}$ trend test under unaveraged and three-bin-averaged profiles. Thresholds are estimated from separate null draws. The resampled empirical trajectories also fail to show a common increasing pattern (Figure 4). That finding is descriptive. A stable boundary need not produce an increasing trend, and the sensitivity of these statistics depends on how differentiation is generated. The absence of four-way increase is consequently not evidence that every form of bounded interaction is absent.

### Model-data comparisons

The basin spatial-partition $F_{ST}$ is 0.0179. Under the revised time-transgressive baseline, the median is 0.0061 (95% simulation interval 0.0023–0.0145), with upper-tail Monte Carlo $p=0.004$. The corresponding southeast-Missouri observed value is 0.0337, compared with 0.0067 (95% simulation interval 0.0049–0.0093) and $p=0.002$. Thus the baseline underpredicts these observed contrasts; it does not reproduce the within-region record merely because it generates some clustering. These comparisons are conditional on the stated population, repertoire, distance, and accumulation assumptions.

![**Figure 5.** Observed partition $F_{ST}$ against matched finite-class simulations at interaction length 24 km and innovation 0.012. The copying multiplier ranges from 1 (distance-only drift) to 0.03 (strong restriction). Points and bars summarize simulation medians and 2.5th–97.5th percentiles, with 30 realizations per setting. Basin and southeast-Missouri values use spatial partitions; the wider-valley value uses Parkin versus other phases. Full parameter-grid results are reported in the supplement.](../../figures/revision_boundary_comparison.png){width=6.5in}

For the basin, 3 of 6 distance-only cells and 6 of 18 restricted-copying cells bracket the observation. For the named Parkin partition, 2 of 6 distance-only cells and 6 of 18 restricted-copying cells bracket the observation. For the southeast-Missouri subset, 0 of 6 distance-only cells and 2 of 18 restricted-copying cells bracket the observation. These counts summarize a finite sensitivity grid; they are not posterior model weights. Changing restriction and nuisance parameters on both sides of the comparison prevents one selected strong-boundary setting from standing in for the entire family of group models.

These comparisons separate two statements often conflated in interpreting a neutral model. Drift can generate differences and algorithmically detected communities, but that fact does not guarantee an adequate prediction of the observed magnitude. Conversely, a model that underpredicts differentiation does not uniquely identify a social boundary: population size, interaction weights, innovation, occupation histories, and class aggregation remain possible sources of discrepancy.

### The named Parkin partition

The named Parkin-versus-others $F_{ST}$ is 0.0121. The time-transgressive drift median is 0.0042 (95% simulation interval 0.0014–0.0113), with upper-tail $p=0.022$. The same observed contrast has $p=0.060$ under contemporaneous, time-averaged sampling and $p=0.016$ under reversed CA order. The contrast is therefore sensitive to how occupation time is represented, and an observation in a model's upper tail should be reported as a discrepancy rather than dismissed because some simulated values exceed it. After actual removal of each Parkin-phase assemblage, 5 of 8 observed contrasts remain above their matched 97.5th simulation percentiles. These influence checks hold the latent copying network fixed and address sensitivity to the observed sample.

### Detected communities and seriation structure

In the wider valley, detected community counts have median 2 under contemporaneous sampling (central 95% range 2–2) and 2 under time-transgressive sampling (2–3). These counts are smaller than the seven named phases. However, a modularity partition and a culture-historical classification are different operations, and the difference in counts is not evidence that the named units are unreal. Observed community $F_{ST}$ is 0.0413, compared with a time-transgressive median of 0.0070 (95% simulation interval 0.0040–0.0132). The number of apparent groups can therefore resemble an archaeological grouping problem even when their differentiation is poorly reproduced.

The deterministic seriation of the 29 original basin assemblages, before rarefaction, contains 35 maximal groups at continuity 0.10, with maximum size four. Parkin joins four groups and ranks eighth by membership count. The descriptive topology therefore places Parkin in overlapping solutions rather than within a single exclusive seriation (Figure 8). This is compatible with its interpretation as a bridge in ceramic interaction [@lipo_2001; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_2015]. Because the deterministic solver omits sampling-significance intervals, it does not establish that every inferred fragment corresponds to a distinct past learning community. Nor does an overlapping seriation exclude a political center that connected several communities.

![**Figure 8.** Deterministic basin seriation at continuity 0.10. Left: maximal group sizes. Right: assemblages with the greatest number of group memberships, highlighting Parkin. Membership in overlapping orderings is a property of the ceramic measurement and is not itself a classification of political institutions.](../../figures/fig6_idss_structure.png){width=6in}

Parkin's recorded platform mound height is 23 feet, tied for the greatest recorded maximum among 55 basin sites with height information. The tallest-to-second ratio is 1.0, and the distribution is graded (Figure 9). These measurements establish prominence without a gap in this particular attribute. They are not a direct measure of tribute, administrative tiers, or redistributive control. Cobb's [-@cobb_2000] account of decentralized chert-hoe production and down-the-line exchange provides additional regional context, but is not a basin-internal test of the political organization of the Parkin phase.

## Discussion

The phase question requires specifying both what a proposed boundary means and what evidence could register it. Our results do not support an automatic translation from ceramic similarity to society. They also do not justify reversing that translation and concluding that weak or overlapping ceramic patterns prove the absence of a polity. The relevant inference is conditional: which observed contrasts are reproduced by specified transmission and observation models, and which remain unexplained?

The revised fixed-class baseline produces less differentiation than observed in the regional partitions, and the Parkin comparison varies with the assumed sampling chronology. That finding changes the evidential balance from a general claim of drift-consistency to a problem of model adequacy. Restricted copying is one explanation to examine, but discrepancy also remains sensitive to nuisance parameters and the measurement process. The matched grid tests specified boundary strengths without showing that the social interpretation of any named phase is uniquely identified.

This distinction qualifies the argument from multiple signatures. The candidate statistics are theoretically motivated but are not sufficient state variables for a unique reconstruction of transmission history. Four measurements of a common frequency matrix can respond to sampling and shared model assumptions. An idealized generator experiment can establish behavior within its constructed scenarios without proving that all realistic alternatives have been excluded. Here, replacing the ordered-row proxy with the actual IDSS group statistic changes the measurement being evaluated and prevents claims about seriation power from resting on a different algorithm.

The same caution applies to dynamic inference. A generative model makes its transition assumptions explicit, but does not automatically achieve empirical identification or dynamic sufficiency in the stronger sense discussed by Lewontin [-@lewontin_1974]. Earlier pooled-population ABC analyses are retained as exploratory material in the supplement. They do not reproduce the spatial observation process used here, and their reported calibration includes substantial undercoverage at one tested bias value. We therefore do not use their narrow intervals to rule out conformist transmission or to turn the spatial comparison into an independent demonstration of neutrality.

Observation assumptions materially affect a drift comparison. A simulation with a different number of classes changes the measured repertoire; one sampled during relaxation from a homogeneous starting state can generate an ordinal gradient without sustained class introduction. Retaining full accumulation windows, matching measured class counts, checking burn-in, and reporting reversed orderings make those assumptions inspectable. They do not make the chosen model uniquely correct. In particular, uniform recurrent innovation within archaeological classes may poorly represent the processes by which those classes were created, recognized, or aggregated. A mismatch should motivate explicit alternatives rather than adjustment solely to recover a preferred result.

There are also limits to the geographic model. Modern mapped watercourses are an approximation to late pre-contact transport routes. Snapping sites to a repaired network does not reconstruct every navigable connection, and the fixed node population does not encode differences in settlement size. The boundary grid varies restriction and two nuisance parameters while holding mixing and population size fixed. More flexible alternatives may fit the data differently. Their evaluation should use the same class mapping and deposition model on both sides of the comparison.

For Parkin, the ceramic evidence should be brought into conversation with ethnohistory, settlement, and exchange rather than asked to replace them. Interpretations involving a paramount center, peer competition, or other arrangements draw on evidence beyond decorated frequencies [@morse_1990; @rees_political_culture; @king_freer_1995]. An interaction bridge could participate in any of several political arrangements. The present analysis evaluates one route from phase membership to social inference, not every independent argument about Casqui.

The transferable contribution is therefore a procedure for making such arguments testable: define the contrast, implement alternative mechanisms on comparable terms, model how their output becomes archaeological counts, and report the resulting limitations alongside the fit. Sensitivity to an injected trajectory and adequacy of a spatial null answer complementary questions. Neither should be substituted for the other.

## Conclusion

Phases remain useful descriptions of variation, but their social interpretation requires additional evidence. The basin's rarefied ceramic trajectory does not show a clear rise in between-group differentiation, and the candidate signatures do not jointly recover the prescribed increasing-divergence signal. Those results limit the trajectory test; they do not establish the absence of bounded interaction. The revised spatial baseline underpredicts important observed contrasts, while the matched boundary and observation scenarios show that model assumptions matter to their interpretation. A drift process can generate apparent groups without adequately explaining the particular groups or magnitudes observed here. The question is consequently not whether clustering looks social, but whether a specified model of social restriction explains observations better than credible alternatives under a defensible observation process. For the Mississippi Valley, that comparison leaves the political interpretation of Parkin open while clarifying what decorated ceramics can contribute to it.
