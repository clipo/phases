## Full specification of the four signatures

Departure from neutral transmission follows Neiman's [-@neiman_1995] application of the infinite-alleles model. We estimated the transmission parameter $\theta$ in two ways from each assemblage. The homozygosity estimator uses the unbiased sample homozygosity $F = \sum_i n_i(n_i-1)/[N(N-1)]$, where $n_i$ is the count of class $i$ and $N$ the assemblage size, and inverts the neutral relation $F = 1/(1+\theta)$ to give $\theta_F = (1-F)/F$. The richness estimator solves the Ewens [-@ewens_1972] sampling expectation for the number of classes $k$,

$$\mathbb{E}[k \mid \theta, N] = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \frac{\theta}{\theta + i},$$

for the value $\theta_E$ that reproduces the observed $k$. Under neutral drift, the two estimators agree. Conformist copying concentrates frequency on common classes, raising $F$ and lowering $\theta_F$ relative to $\theta_E$, whereas anti-conformist novelty bias does the reverse. The signature is the ratio $\theta_F/\theta_E$, which both detects a departure from neutrality and signs it. Because pooling assemblages from divergent groups cancels out the effect, we computed ratios within spatial clusters and averaged the results across clusters.

Seriation-coherence breakdown follows the deterministic frequency-seriation method of Lipo, Madsen, and Dunnell [-@lipo_madsen_dunnell_2015; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_hunt_1997; @dunnell_1970; @graves_cachola-abad_1996; @marquardt_1978; @cowgill_1972; @obrien_lyman_1999; @lyman_obrien_2006]. A set of assemblages is co-seriable if some ordering renders every decorated class's frequency curve single-peaked and without gaps, the battleship-shaped distribution of frequency seriation. We enumerated the complete set of maximal co-seriable groups, the subsets for which a valid ordering exists, and which are maximal under inclusion. A single group indicates one interacting community, whereas many small, mutually non-co-seriable groups indicate a fragmented one. We report the number and maximum size of these groups and, for each assemblage, its group-membership count, which identifies the bridge assemblages that join otherwise incompatible orderings. The number of maximal groups is the continuous signature. Checking every possible ordering becomes computationally intractable beyond about fourteen assemblages. IDSS instead builds solutions agglomeratively, starting from the valid three-assemblage seriations and extending each by adding assemblages that keep every class unimodal and continuous, until none can be added. An ordering is broken when a decorated class is not unimodal along it, or when a class's relative frequency jumps between adjacent assemblages by more than a fixed continuity threshold, which we set to 0.10. The threshold sets how much adjacent frequency change we treat as real rather than sampling noise. A looser threshold admits more orderings and a stricter one fewer, and the fragmented, overlapping topology reported in the main text is stable across a range of thresholds.

In the record-matched recovery experiment and the empirical trajectory, that statistic is the number of maximal co-seriable groups per assemblage within each ordinal bin, computed by the same deterministic algorithm at a continuity threshold of 0.10. The idealized validation of Figure S1 instead uses a simpler proxy, the number of fall-to-rise reversals in each class's frequency column along the supplied order. An earlier implementation of that proxy accepted a single valley as unimodal, and the corrected score treats any fall followed by a rise as a violation. Neither implementation includes the bootstrap significance layer of the published method.

The variance partition is a cultural analog of Wright's $F_{ST}$ computed on Gini-Simpson diversity $H = 1 - \sum_i p_i^2$,

$$F_{ST} = \frac{H_T - H_S}{H_T},$$

where $H_T$ is the diversity of the pooled assemblages and $H_S$ the size-weighted mean within-cluster diversity [@bell_richerson_mcelreath_2009]. It is zero when clusters share a repertoire and increases as they hold distinct ones.

Spatial assortativity asks whether ceramic similarity follows social rather than geographic distance. We measured Brainerd-Robinson similarity $S_{ij} = 200 - \sum_k |100\,p_{ik} - 100\,p_{jk}|$ between every pair of assemblages. Because a Mantel correlation cannot separate a sharp boundary from a smooth distance decay, we summarized assortativity as a distance-controlled boundary excess. Within each pairwise-distance bin, the mean within-cluster similarity minus the mean between-cluster similarity is averaged over bins. Under pure isolation by distance, similarity depends only on distance, and the excess is near zero. A sharp social boundary makes within-cluster pairs much more similar than between-cluster pairs at matched distance, yielding a large positive excess. Spatial clusters were obtained by $k$-means on site coordinates with multiple restarts. The number of clusters was chosen by the mean silhouette.

We combined the four signatures by standardizing each across the ordinal sequence and averaging them, so that no single signature would dominate, and summarized the trajectory as the ordinal slope of the combined score and each component, with rank correlations and resampling intervals. All size-control draws take 50 sherds from each assemblage without replacement (multivariate hypergeometric sampling). An assemblage with exactly 50 sherds remains unchanged. The three signatures the recovery experiment shows to be unreliable are averaged over 400 such draws. The cultural $F_{ST}$ trend, the one signature the record can resolve, is quoted from 4,000 draws with a Monte Carlo interval on the mean, because a single draw of that statistic has a standard deviation of about 0.25 and a few-hundred-draw mean is not stable in sign. All of these condition on the observed assemblages, spatial partition, and ordering rather than on uncertainty about deposits, chronology, or model choice.

## The generative validation model

The validation rests on a generative model in which decorated variants are copied under neutral transmission [@neiman_1995; @bentley_hahn_shennan_2004], with three mechanism-faithful controls, the between-group divergence of class profiles, the strength of within-group conformity (a frequency-dependent copying bias, after Henrich [-@henrich_2001]), and a spatial rule placing group profiles on the landscape. Four generators instantiate the four idealized processes of Table 1, where drift is a single process that the main-text table shows under two regimes, smooth and stochastic, of which the smooth, deterministic regime is the one generated here. The bounded-groups generator raises divergence and conformity together across spatially bounded groups. Environmental patchiness holds a fixed between-group difference under neutral within-group copying with an environment-keyed layout. Aggregated conformity raises within-group conformity in a single undivided pool with no between-group divergence. Drift with distance-limited interaction produces a smooth spatial gradient under neutral copying. Each generator emits a sequence of group-by-class slices along an ordinal axis. We ran the four-signature procedure on each generator, blind to its source, and recorded which registered as convergent, repeating this across 500 random seeds. On the bounded-groups simulations we also computed the correlation matrix of the four signatures, to confirm that their joint movement is informative rather than an artifact of redundancy (Figure S1). This idealized test uses large, clean assemblages (12 groups, several hundred sherds each) and establishes that it discriminates in principle. The record-matched, size-controlled recovery in the main text (Figure 4) is what establishes which signatures survive at the resolution of the actual data.

## Bayesian estimates of the $F_{ST}$ trend

The main text reports the trend in cultural $F_{ST}$ along the seriation sequence as a rank correlation on rarefied assemblages. We also estimate it a second way that does not discard data. The Balding-Nichols model is fitted to each seriation bin, giving a posterior for that bin's $F_{ST}$ whose width reflects how many sherds the bin contains, and the trend is then estimated as a slope over those posteriors. The prior on $F_{ST}$ is Beta(1,10). A flat prior on that parameter is not flat on the observable. At this design it expects a median $F_{ST}$ near 0.27 and places 47 percent of its prior predictive mass above 0.30, against an observed 0.018, whereas Beta(1,10) expects a median of 0.027 with 1 percent above 0.30. Results under the flat prior are reported alongside. Because the model treats sampling generatively rather than equalizing it by subsampling, the sample-size confound is absorbed rather than removed, and the reported uncertainty is a credible interval rather than a resampling spread. The two are different quantities, a rank correlation on rarefied assemblages and a slope in $F_{ST}$ units per standardized bin. The per-bin posteriors themselves are in Table S1.

## The spatial field model

The between-cluster cultural $F_{ST}$ reported in the main text requires a division of the basin into spatial clusters. The field model removes that requirement. It treats the decorated-class composition of each assemblage as a draw from a multinomial whose class probabilities, on the logit scale, are the sum of a class mean, a spatially correlated latent field over the assemblage coordinates, and an assemblage-specific term with no spatial structure. The field has a Matérn-family covariance with amplitude $\sigma$ and length scale $\rho$, and the assemblage-specific term has standard deviation $\tau$. In the variant we report, the assemblage-specific term enters the covariance as a nugget rather than as a second latent field, which halves the number of latent parameters and removes a flat direction along which the two terms trade off. The quantity the question turns on is the spatial share, $\sigma^2 / (\sigma^2 + \tau^2)$, the fraction of assemblage-level compositional variance carried by the spatial field. Bounded interaction groups leave site-specific character that geography does not explain, and in this model that character can only enter through $\tau$. The length scale carries an inverse-gamma prior that places its mass inside the range the basin's geometry can resolve. The models are written in Stan and fitted by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (rstan 2.32). The model files and fitting scripts are in the repository (`stan/spatial/`, `analyses/02_spatial/`).

Fitted to the 28 basin assemblages and ten decorated classes, the spatial share is 0.996 with a 95 percent credible interval of 0.973 to 1.000 under the marginalized nugget, and 0.997, 0.975 to 1.000, under the additive form. The length scale is not identified. Its posterior sits at or above the basin's 128 km extent under both forms, and the sampler saturates its tree depth without divergences, which is the signature of a ridge in the posterior rather than of a step-size problem. We therefore report the length scale as unresolved rather than as an estimate, and interpret the fit as showing that no decay in composition is detectable across the St. Francis drainage. Recovery tests on simulated fields with known length scales show that the design resolves scales only well below the basin's extent (`analyses/02_spatial/01_recovery.R`, `analyses/48_length_scale_recovery.py`).

The model reproduces the quantities it was not fitted to. Under the marginalized form, the posterior predictive interval for the between-cluster cultural $F_{ST}$ at three clusters is 0.0127 to 0.0190 (median 0.0157) against an observed 0.0156 (Bayesian predictive p = 0.53). For the distance decay of similarity it is -0.363 to -0.272 (median -0.322) against -0.341 (p = 0.78), for the mean within-assemblage Gini-Simpson diversity 0.551 to 0.577 against 0.563 (p = 0.57), and for its spread across assemblages 0.096 to 0.122 against 0.112 (p = 0.34). This check is run on the fit it checks; an earlier version of the pipeline ran it before the fit and so compared the previous data set's replicates with the current observations. A field with no groups therefore reproduces the observed differentiation, the distance decay, and the diversity structure. The field model describes the pattern rather than a mechanism, so it does not compete with the calibrated drift comparison of the main text. It establishes that whatever produced the differentiation left it spatially continuous.

### Sensitivity of cultural $F_{ST}$ to the partition

The between-cluster $F_{ST}$ depends on how finely the basin is divided. Fitted with the Balding-Nichols model at two through six spatial clusters, its posterior median moves monotonically from 0.0147 (95 percent credible interval 0.0128 to 0.0167) at two clusters, through 0.0178 (0.0157 to 0.0200) at the three the silhouette criterion selects, to 0.0347 (0.0319 to 0.0376) at six, and the intervals at the two ends do not overlap. It does not depend on which particular division is used at a given number of clusters. Twenty k-means seeds at three clusters return the same partition, and the division we use sits at the 51st percentile of 150 alternative contiguous divisions of the basin at the same grain, whose median of posterior medians is 0.0178. A quantity that responds to the grain of an imposed division and not to which division is imposed is measuring the division. That is why the main text reports the between-cluster $F_{ST}$ alongside the partition-free field model rather than on its own.

## The minimum sample size, and why it is 75

The cultural $F_{ST}$ used throughout is the Gini-Simpson plug-in, and it is biased upward by sampling. Two assemblages drawn from the same population of pots differ because each is a finite sample, and the spurious between-assemblage variance scales roughly as one over the sample size. To find the count at which that artifact stops being able to pass for the differentiation the basin shows, we drew pairs of multinomial samples of size $n$ from the basin's pooled class profile, 4,000 pairs at each size, and computed $F_{ST}$ between them. The two share one profile by construction, so all of the $F_{ST}$ between them is sampling (`analyses/82_minimum_sample_size.py`). The observed between-cluster $F_{ST}$ at five spatial clusters is the reference.

At 75 sherds the median artifact is about a seventh of the observed differentiation and its 95th percentile first falls below the observed value. Above 75 the bias shrinks slowly, by about three percentage points of the observed value between 75 and 100, while assemblages are lost quickly: 100 would remove five more. The analytic expectation for two equal samples from one profile is close to $1/n$. Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003] kept sites with 800 or more sherds in all; decorated sherds are 13 percent of his table, so his rule corresponds to roughly 100 decorated sherds. The drift null is drawn at the observed sherd totals and carries the same bias, so the calibrated comparison is not distorted by it; the minimum protects the per-cluster decomposition of the residual and any reading of a single small cluster, where a term computed on a few dozen sherds is mostly the artifact.

## Robustness checks

Neiman's [-@neiman_1995] sample-size diagnostic for the neutrality estimators supports the Discussion. Across the 28 basin assemblages, we computed the homozygosity-based and richness-based estimates of the transmission parameter and their signed difference. We then correlated each with assemblage size and with seriation position, following Neiman's protocol for separating a transmission signature from a sample-size artifact. Several further checks support the Results. A forking-paths sensitivity analysis recomputes the convergence verdict across twenty-seven combinations of the basin's spatial cutoff, bin count, and cluster number. The frequency increment test [@feder_kryazhimskiy_plotkin_2014] is applied to each decorated class's binned frequency trajectory. Neiman's [-@neiman_1995] squared-Euclidean interassemblage distance is computed with its diversity-distance relationship and divergence trajectory. We evaluate the alternative basin definition, the stricter watershed rule, in full. Clustering-tendency statistics (Hopkins, silhouette, and the gap statistic) [@hopkins_skellam_1954; @rousseeuw_1987; @tibshirani_walther_hastie_2001] on the correspondence-analysis ordination favor a single group, with a maximum silhouette of only 0.47, consistent with a near-continuum rather than sharply bounded ceramic groups. All are provided in the supplementary code. Figure S1 shows the Neiman interassemblage-distance result.

## The $F_{ST}$-led reduction is a property of the record, not the generator

The recovery experiment imposes group closure as between-cluster divergence, which is what cultural $F_{ST}$ measures, so one objection is that the experiment only certifies an $F_{ST}$ test recovering an $F_{ST}$-shaped pattern. We tested this by imposing closure through two further pathways, each matched to a different signature, and asking which signature recovers each. A within-group conformity pathway raises conformity in a single undivided pool with no between-group divergence, the pathway that the neutral departure is meant to detect. A non-spatial social pathway assembles assemblages into social groups scrambled relative to the spatial clusters, the pathway seriation coherence is meant to detect. The result is one-sided in two ways. First, the conformity pathway is recovered by no signature (the neutral departure, which should detect it, inverts rather than rises), and the non-spatial social pathway is not recovered by seriation either. Hence, the neutral and seriation signatures fail to register imposed closure even through the pathway each is designed for. Second, $F_{ST}$ is not a clean spatial-divergence test but a near-omnibus one, responding to group structure, however it is organized. It recovers the spatial-divergence pathway strongly (mean trend rising from -0.40 at the null to +0.83 at strong imposed closure) and also recovers the non-spatial social pathway (from -0.29 to +0.54), because both ultimately raise between-group variance somewhere the partition can see it. The four signatures are therefore not four independent pathways, even in this diagnostic. The only one that registers imposed closure at this resolution is $F_{ST}$, and it does so however the underlying divergence is organized. The reduction to $F_{ST}$ is a property of the record's resolution, not an artifact of a generator whose imposed closure is collinear with $F_{ST}$. That resolution is 29 time-averaged assemblages subsampled to a common count over ten aggregated classes. A series of subsampling-depth runs (the weak signatures climb modestly with more sherds but remain well below $F_{ST}$) and a series of cluster-count runs (the spatial signature stays weak through k = 5) confirm that the limitation is the record's size and time-averaging rather than a single tuning choice. The consequence, stated in the Discussion, is that the negative bounds only cover closure expressed as between-group divergence. A closure carried purely by conformity or by a non-spatially organized social structure is below detection here at any strength.

## Positive control: the basin against calibrated drift and bounded groups

To test what process the basin's structure matches, rather than only which process it fails to show, we simulated neutral cultural drift and a bounded-groups process on the real coordinate layout of the 28 assemblages, using the model and calibration described in the next section. The calibration is the first result. Under uniform innovation, in which the ten classes are exchangeable, one of the 297 parameter combinations (120 learners, innovation 0.0005, mixing 0.05) falls within the 10 percent tolerance on the six calibration draws, and on 500 baseline realizations it does not: its within-assemblage Gini-Simpson diversity is 0.68 and its pooled diversity 0.76, against 0.56 and 0.59 observed, so the uniform model runs too diverse. Under pooled-profile innovation, 51 combinations match, spanning population sizes of 120, 2,000 and 10,000 learners and low rates of innovation and mixing (Table S2). None of the 51 reaches the observed between-cluster $F_{ST}$ at the median. The highest median among them is 0.0045 against 0.0062 observed, a result we return to with the full grid below.

At the diversity-matched parameter combination most favorable to drift (120 learners, innovation 0.024, mixing 0.01, interaction length 24 river-km), the observed between-cluster $F_{ST}$ of 0.0062 at the two spatial clusters the silhouette selects lies inside the drift interval of 0.0002 to 0.0099 (median 0.0018). Eight percent of simulated datasets reach it under the seriation order, 7 percent reversed, and 17 percent contemporaneous. Calibrated drift returns one ceramic community in 236 of 500 runs; where it returns two or more, the between-community $F_{ST}$ has a median of 0.019 (0.010 to 0.036) against an observed 0.039, which 2 percent of runs reach, and modularity a median of 0.001 against an observed 0.045. The distance decay of similarity is steeper in the data than drift produces (observed Mantel r = -0.21; drift interval -0.14 to +0.13). The distance-controlled boundary excess of the spatial clusters is positive in the data (+14.0, within-cluster pairs more similar than between-cluster pairs at matched distance), outside both the drift interval (-9.5 to +7.6) and the bounded-groups interval (-12.2 to +7.5). Reducing copying across the spatial clusters by factors of 0.5, 0.1, and 0.03 leaves the median $F_{ST}$ between 0.0013 and 0.0024 at both interaction lengths. The observation is reached only through the upper tails, in 0 to 23 percent of 30 realizations per cell (Table S3). Innovation drawn from the regional pooled profile is a region-wide source of new variants that no boundary interrupts, which mutes the boundary model in this family. A boundary model whose innovation respected the partition would build the tested boundary into its innovation target, and we did not run one. The comparison therefore shows that the calibrated drift model underpredicts the basin's differentiation and that the bounded-groups model as built here accounts for little of the difference (Figure S2).

## The spatial drift model and its calibration

The drift-versus-groups comparison (Figures S2 and S4) uses one finite-population network model of decorated-class transmission, run on the real geography of the 28 St. Francis basin assemblages. Each assemblage location is a node holding a population of learners. Each generation, a learner copies the decorated class of another learner, drawn from its own node with probability $1-m$ and from another node with probability $m$, the other node chosen with a weight that decays exponentially with along-waterway distance at length scale $L$. With probability $\mu$ the learner instead adopts a class by innovation. The model tracks class frequencies rather than individual learners. The next-generation frequencies at each node are a multinomial draw of $N$ learners from the copying-plus-innovation probabilities, which is distributionally equivalent to independent learner-by-learner copying. The repertoire is fixed at the ten measured decorated classes. Bounded groups are imposed by multiplying every copying weight that crosses a specified partition by a factor $\ell$ before normalization. A value of $\ell = 1$ is distance-only drift, and $\ell = 0.5$, 0.1, and 0.03 impose progressively stronger boundaries on the basin's spatial clusters. Each run discards 1,200 burn-in generations and then records 90 evenly spaced slices over 1,800 further generations.

Innovation requires a choice about what an archaeological class is, and we run the model under two versions of that choice. Under uniform innovation, the standard K-allele model, an innovating learner adopts any of the measured classes with equal probability, so the classes are treated as exchangeable variants. Under pooled-profile innovation, an innovating learner adopts each class in proportion to that class's share of the region's pooled counts, which represents the fact that the culture-history types aggregate unequal numbers of underlying decorative variants, so that innovation lands in a broad type more often than in a narrow one. The pooled profile carries no information about any partition, so it does not build the tested boundary into the null. Neither version introduces frequency-dependent bias.

Because the between-group statistics depend on population size, mixing, and innovation jointly, the model must be calibrated to the record before its between-group predictions mean anything. We calibrate each model on three within-assemblage summaries that do not involve the tested partition. These are mean within-assemblage Gini-Simpson diversity, mean number of classes present at the observed sherd totals, and Gini-Simpson diversity of the pooled counts. A grid of node population size (120, 2,000, and 10,000 learners), innovation rate (0.0002 to 0.2), and mixing rate (0.001 to 0.4) is simulated at six seeds per combination with the same time-transgressive sampling applied to the data, and a combination is diversity-matched when all three simulated summaries fall within 10 percent of the observed values. The baseline combination for the drift comparison is the diversity-matched parameter combination with the highest median partition $F_{ST}$, so the comparison is the one most favorable to drift. When no combination is matched, we use the minimum-misfit combination and identify it as such. The partition $F_{ST}$ is recorded for every combination but never enters the matching criterion. The baseline is then run for 500 realizations, the boundary factors for 30 realizations each at two interaction lengths (12 and 24 km), and seven sensitivity cases (burn-in doubled, monomorphic initialization with and without burn-in, and the calibrated innovation and mixing rates halved and doubled) for 50 realizations each. The fraction of simulated datasets reaching the observed value is reported as $(e+1)/(R+1)$, where $e$ is the number of realizations at or above the observed statistic and $R$ the number of realizations. A cache of every replicate is keyed to a fingerprint of the configuration, the arrays supplied to it, the numerical source, and package versions, and is rebuilt when any of them changes.

Interaction in this riverine settlement system travels along watercourses, so the copying kernel uses river-network distance rather than straight-line distance. We build a graph from the mapped hydrography (the LMVHydrology centerlines clipped to the basin with a 25 km margin), repair its topology by joining vertices within 250 m of one another, keep the largest connected component, and snap each assemblage to the nearest network node, adding the snap distance as an access cost at both ends. The distance between two assemblages is the shortest along-water path between their snapped nodes. All 28 assemblages connect on the resulting network and the mean access distance from an assemblage to a mapped channel is 2.75 km. Detour is summarized two ways, because the summaries differ: the mean of the per-pair ratios of along-water to straight-line distance is 3.7 and the median is 2.9. The per-pair ratio is right-skewed because a pair that is close in a straight line but must route around a meander carries a very large ratio. Substituting straight-line distance changes the results negligibly (between-group $F_{ST}$ and community counts shift within sampling error), so the conclusions do not depend on the choice. We use river-network distance as the null most favorable to drift, not because the pottery follows it: decorated-class distance between assemblages tracks straight-line separation (rank correlation 0.36) better than along-water distance (0.24), and between assemblages in different phases the along-water metric carries nothing once straight-line distance is held fixed (-0.03). The network is modern hydrography, and the St. Francis, Tyronza and Mississippi have all moved since these sites were occupied; a phase on the St. Francis reaches a phase on the Mississippi only through their confluence far to the south, so along-water distances between phases describe the routing of today's channels. Both metrics are carried through the relaxation sweep below.

The assemblages are not contemporaneous, which is both why they can be seriated and why some carry more of the late classes than others, so the evolving field is sampled time-transgressively rather than at a single instant. Each synthetic assemblage is drawn from the recorded slice that matches its observed seriation position, its rank on the first correspondence-analysis axis of the real data, and is accumulated over a trailing window of eight slices so that, like a real death assemblage, it mixes production across its span. The earliest rank is mapped to the end of the first complete window, so every assemblage receives the same window length. We then sample each synthetic assemblage to the observed sherd count of its real counterpart. The synthetic assemblages are treated exactly as the empirical ones, with spatial clusters by the same $k$-means partition, interaction communities by the same modularity partition, and between-group cultural $F_{ST}$ on each. Because the orientation of the seriation axis is only partly anchored, every field is also observed under the reversed order and under a contemporaneous scheme in which every assemblage is drawn from the terminal window.

## Relaxing four assumptions of the drift model

The calibrated drift model reproduces the assemblages' diversity but not their differentiation. The model makes four simplifying assumptions, and relaxing any of them could account for the difference. Relaxing one does not abandon drift. Each variant is the same finite-population drift model with one of its simplifications removed. Each is relaxed here on the basin set at its calibrated combination. The full series is in `output/findings/other_departures.md`. The standard is the same throughout. Relaxing an assumption accounts for the difference only if it reaches the observed $F_{ST}$ of 0.0062 AND keeps all three diversity summaries inside the calibration's 10 percent tolerance. A model that reaches the differentiation by losing the diversity fit has improved one fit at the expense of the other rather than improving both.

The geography runs vary the distance metric, the interaction length and the *form* of the decay, since an exponential fall-off is an assumption rather than a measurement. A Gaussian decay isolates distant sites about eleven times more than an exponential at the same length and narrows the shortfall to 3.2 but breaks the diversity match; the best diversity-matched setting, a power-law decay at 12 km, reaches 3.1. No metric, length or decay shape moves the median by more than a few ten-thousandths on this set. A power-law decay, with its heavier tail, is worse everywhere.

Group innovation profiles are random partial permutations of the regional profile, with the strength setting the fraction of classes permuted. A permutation holds the multiset of frequencies exactly, so every group keeps the same richness and the same within-profile diversity and differs only in which classes it favors. Using each group's observed profile instead would guarantee the result by construction, and two earlier constructions were discarded for confounding the boundary with something else. A Dirichlet perturbation made profiles sparser as well as different, dropping mean richness from 5.31 to 3.48, and a mixture of the regional profile with a permuted copy flattened it, raising diversity well above the observed value.

The individual realizations are widely spread, so the agreement at strengths 0.2 to 0.22 is between the model's central tendency and the observation rather than between tight distributions. It identifies a mechanism the record is consistent with, not one it demonstrates. The mechanism is neutral drift in which new styles arise locally rather than region-wide, not a process of a different kind.

The transport-geography, accumulation-span and innovation-boundary sweeps were also run on the southeast-Missouri assemblages. That set is not adequate for the test, for reasons of class composition set out in the final section of this text, and the way its behavior under these three exposes the problem is reported there rather than here.

## Dynamic-sufficiency tests in the time domain

The four convergence signatures are measured as states along the seriation sequence, so on their own they are only partially dynamically sufficient. They describe states rather than the transitions between them. Two further tests work on the transformation between states, and a third test, which this record cannot resolve, is reported in the code for completeness. The two we rely on are dynamically and empirically sufficient, because each models change between states while respecting the record's time-averaged resolution.

The first infers how potters were copying by simulation. We run many copying scenarios, ranging from strongly conformist to neutral to novelty-seeking, and keep only the ones that reproduce the real assemblages. From the surviving scenarios, we infer the kind of copying implied, with the record's time-averaging built into each simulation (the approximate-Bayesian-computation approach) [@crema_kandler_shennan_2016; @carrignon_bentley_ruck_2019; @crema_edinborough_kerig_shennan_2014]. We modeled the basin sequence as frequency-dependent copying among the ten decorated classes in a finite population. Each generation, a class's adoption weight is proportional to its frequency raised to the power $1+b$, where $b>0$ is conformity, $b<0$ is anti-conformity, and $b=0$ is neutral copying, with an innovation rate that mixes in a uniform draw from the set of classes. An assemblage pools production over a window of generations, so the time-averaging that distorts the static statistics is built into the generative process rather than corrected after the fact. This is what makes the test both empirically and dynamically sufficient. We placed broad priors on the innovation rate, the bias $b$, the population size, and the time-averaging window, and fit the model by sequential Monte Carlo approximate Bayesian computation (800 particles advanced through six rounds of shrinking tolerance), which advances a population of parameter sets through a sequence of shrinking distance tolerances and corrects the accepted values with a local-linear regression adjustment, matching each simulated six-bin sequence to the observed diversity trajectory and rank-frequency profile [@toni_etal_2009; @beaumont_zhang_balding_2002]. The posterior of the bias is concentrated near neutral, with mean $b = +0.003$ and a 95 percent interval of $-0.035$ to $+0.039$ that includes zero in the primary run, and means of $+0.001$ to $+0.004$ across five independent runs of the sampler (Figure S5A). The posterior standard deviation is 0.017 against a prior of 0.289, so the data strongly constrain the bias rather than leaving it at the prior. Any conformist lean is weak and unresolved. $P(b>0) = 0.62$ in the primary run, and across the five runs it ranges from 0.53 to 0.63, so its direction is not stable to the precision a single run suggests. Refitting the bias separately to the early and late halves of the sequence recovers no shift between them. Taken at face value, the interval excludes a strong basin-wide conformist bias by modeling copying dynamics rather than a single static moment. Two caveats limit that interpretation. We develop both after the calibration results below. Because it fits the sequence as a single pooled population, it reflects aggregate conformity across the basin rather than a spatially localized or bounded-group version, which the spatial signatures and the drift comparison address. We validated the method by simulation-based calibration, fitting 200 datasets drawn from the prior and confirming that the rank of each true value within its posterior is uniform (Kolmogorov-Smirnov D = 0.088, p = 0.086, Figure S5B) [@talts_etal_2018]. The posterior is concordant with an independent rejection-sampling fit of the same model (mean $b = +0.019$, 95 percent interval $-0.062$ to $+0.054$, wider and also including zero). The supplementary code includes the full configuration, diagnostics, and software versions.

The calibration battery also reports conditional coverage of the nominal 95 percent interval at nine fixed values of $b$, with 20 simulated datasets each (Table S4). Coverage is adequate at most values but falls to 0.70 at $b = +0.10$ and 0.80 at $b = -0.10$, values that a prior-averaged rank check cannot diagnose. Two further limitations apply. The battery ran with 300 particles and five rounds, whereas the empirical fit used 800 particles and six rounds, so the calibration does not exactly test the empirical sampler configuration. And the synthetic observations in the battery are drawn from the same pooled-population simulator that is fitted, whereas the archaeological observations are spatially distributed assemblages pooled into six ordination bins. The battery therefore checks numerical inference under the assumed simulator, not whether that simulator represents the archaeological observation process. We accordingly treat the empirical interval as consistent with weak or absent basin-wide conformity, conditional on the pooled model, rather than as a demonstrated exclusion of strong conformity.

The second test asks, in plain terms, whether the assemblages' diversity drifts without direction through time, heads steadily in one direction, or is pulled back toward a stable value, by comparing four standard time-series models (an unbiased random walk, a directional walk, stasis, and a mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process) [@hunt_2006; @hunt_bell_travis_2008]. For the Gini-Simpson diversity trajectory and for the abundant decorated classes, binned along the oriented axis, we fit four time-series models on the same level vector, an unbiased random walk (Brownian motion, the neutral-drift expectation), a general biased random walk (the directional signature a sustained transition would leave), a stasis model (white noise about a constant mean), and an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model (mean reversion toward an optimum). Rather than select among the four, we fit the model that nests them and report the two parameters that distinguish them, since unbiased motion, stasis and mean reversion are regions of one parameter space (unbiased motion as the mean-reversion rate approaches zero, stasis as it grows large). Binned along the radiocarbon-oriented axis, decorated-class diversity runs from 0.666 in the earliest bin through 0.734 to 0.460 in the latest, falling over the later half of the sequence. The mean-reversion rate has a posterior median of 0.78 with a 95 percent credible interval of 0.05 to 11.1, spanning the unbiased and stasis limits and so unresolved. The directional drift leans negative but is not resolved, with a median of -0.062 and an interval of -0.164 to +0.029, with P(drift > 0) = 0.063. On the matrix used in an earlier draft, which counted one class twice, the drift was resolved and negative; on the corrected counts the interval reaches zero, and the decline is a tendency of the sequence rather than an established feature of it. Either way it is not a departure from neutrality. Drift in a finite population loses variants, and the posterior predictive check above, which draws its effective population size and innovation rate from the transmission posterior rather than fixing them, reproduces the observed trajectory in all six bins. What the two results say jointly is that diversity tends to decline, and that a neutral process of the estimated size and innovation rate is sufficient to produce that tendency. Because the sequence has only six points, the reversion rate and the drift remain partly confounded, since relaxation toward a lower optimum can imitate sustained directional loss. Reporting a single winning time-series model would have concealed both the confound and the resolved direction. This result is a concordance rather than a decisive selection (Figure S5C). The diversity trajectory is the trait of interest here, because the correspondence-analysis ordering does not arrange it by construction. Individual-class trajectories more often favor the directional model. That comparison, however, is weak and partly tautological because the seriation axis is designed to maximize monotonic class-frequency turnover, and only three classes are abundant enough across the sequence to fit. The robust conclusion is neutral drift, not a sustained directional transition.

### Further checks of neutrality along the sequence

The main text summarizes three further checks. Their full statement follows.

Three further checks add detail. The first follows each decorated class as it rises and falls through the sequence and asks whether those changes look like the undirected fluctuation of unbiased copying, which is what neutral drift produces, or like the directed change a deliberate marker would show. Rather than testing each class separately, we fit the rescaled increments of all nine testable classes in one hierarchical model with partial pooling, following the frequency increment construction [@feder_kryazhimskiy_plotkin_2014]. The population-level departure from drift has a posterior median of +0.008 with a 95 percent credible interval of -0.035 to +0.059, where unbiased drift implies zero. Individual classes are not resolved. A class simulated with a substantial true departure is recovered at about a third of its size with an interval spanning zero, so forty increments across nine classes can support the aggregate statement but not a per-class one.

The second check asks whether a neutral model reproduces the observed diversity trajectory, in the spirit of the non-equilibrium neutrality test [@kandler_shennan_2013]. Rather than sweeping the effective population size over fixed values, we draw it and the innovation rate from the transmission posterior and compare the observed trajectory with the resulting posterior predictive distribution, the range of trajectories the fitted model expects to see. The observed diversity falls inside the 95 percent predictive interval in all six bins, and it does so under each of the five sampler seeds taken separately. The pooled Bayesian predictive p is 0.71 and ranges from 0.54 to 0.87 across those seeds, so the check establishes that the observed trajectory is unremarkable under a neutral model rather than any particular value of p. A neutral model is adequate for this trajectory. That is concordance, not a demonstration that transmission was neutral. The predictive interval is wide because the effective population size is itself uncertain.

The third check asks whether the assemblages grow more different from one another toward contact, as fragmenting groups would. Neiman's interassemblage distance shows no resolved divergence trend along the sequence. The posterior for the rank correlation between mean within-bin pairwise distance and seriation position has a median of +0.26 with a 95 percent credible interval of -0.41 to +0.71 across the six bins, so the direction of any trend is unresolved rather than shown to be absent. Typical differences between spatial clusters are barely larger than differences within them, a ratio of 1.13. The most diverse assemblages are also the most central, which is the pattern a single shared community produces (Figure S1) [@neiman_1995].

## What recovers the phase scheme, and the zero problem behind it

Assemblages are clustered in a joint feature space: site coordinates in kilometers, centered and scaled so the mean squared pairwise distance is one, concatenated with decorated-class composition scaled the same way and multiplied by the square root of a weight $w$. At $w = 0$ the representation is the site map alone; as $w$ grows it becomes the pottery alone. Each clustering is compared with Mainfort's published phase assignments by the adjusted Rand index. Three composition transforms, three clustering algorithms (k-means over 40 initializations, Ward linkage, average linkage), and nine weights are run; the full grid is in `output/findings/phase_recovery.csv`.

Three of the 28 assemblages carry a phase this study derived by territory, which is a geographic rule. Scoring geography against those labels would be scoring geography against itself, so every value quoted in the main text is computed on the 25 assemblages Mainfort assigned.

Class proportions are compositional data, and a Euclidean distance on raw proportions is not the appropriate metric for them. The centered log-ratio transform is the principled alternative, and it cannot be applied here without a decision that turns out to carry the result. Of the 280 cells in the basin matrix, 105 are zero, and the log-ratio of a zero is undefined. Replacing zeros with a fraction of the smallest positive proportion lets that fraction set how strongly absence is amplified, and the agreement with the phases follows it:

| zero convention | adjusted Rand index |
|---|---|
| pseudocount of 0.5 added to counts | 0.533 |
| floor at 2.0 times the smallest positive proportion | 0.607 |
| floor at 0.5 times the smallest positive proportion | 0.607 |
| floor at 0.1 times the smallest positive proportion | 0.766 |

All four are the same data, the same geography and the same weight ($w = 0.5$); only the convention differs, and it moves the answer by a quarter of the index's range. A log-ratio figure quoted without its convention is therefore not a measurement of the record. We report the chi-square transform in the main text because it requires no such choice, and we use a pseudocount rather than a floor wherever the log-ratio transform appears, since a pseudocount has no comparable free limit. This is also a caution about the phases themselves: a classification sensitive to which types are absent, applied to assemblages that differ ninety-sixfold in sherd count, will read sampling failure as cultural difference.

## Radiocarbon chronology

We calibrated the Mainfort [-@mainfort_2001] radiocarbon compilation against IntCal20 [@reimer_etal_2020]. Forty determinations come from seven St. Francis Basin Parkin-phase sites, nineteen of them from Parkin. The summed probability distribution of the basin dates concentrates the occupation in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries, with a median of AD 1428 (Parkin alone, AD 1483). It declines across the contact interval. Twenty-nine percent of the basin probability mass postdates AD 1541 and 18 percent postdates AD 1600 (Figure S6). This is consistent with occupation continuing through the De Soto entrada, which the chronicles place at Casqui, and declining thereafter, rather than with a sequence running unbroken into the seventeenth century. However, the protohistoric tail is partly a calibration artifact of low-activity determinations near the post-1650 plateau [@surovell_brantingham_2007; @surovell_etal_2009]. To test the assumption that the seriation axis tracks calendar time, we pooled the dates of the five proveniences matching curated assemblages. We correlated their pooled median calibrated ages with CA1 position, giving Spearman's r = +0.70 (n = 5, 27 dates pooled), with four sites in calendar order and one late-dated site, Clay Hill, departing from it. The orientation of the axis toward later time is supported but not certain, and we treat directional statements as provisional throughout. The basin chronology is stable under an alternative compilation. Augmenting the Mainfort determinations with the net-new Mississippian dates in the broader lower-valley radiocarbon database compiled by Jeffrey Alvey (personal communication, 2022), twelve determinations concentrated at Hazel and Upper Nodena, shifts the summed-probability median by only eight years, to AD 1436. The contact-truncation pattern and the CA1-calendar correlation are unchanged (Spearman +0.70). Recent AMS re-dating of the Parkin platform-mound post, interpreted as the base of the cross the De Soto expedition raised at Casqui, independently places it at cal AD 1445 to 1650 [@mitchem_2017], consistent with the terminal occupation inferred here. The individual determinations used in the basin chronology, with lab numbers, ages, calibrated medians, and sources, are listed in the supplemental data file `radiocarbon_dates_used.csv`.

## The southeast-Missouri assemblages, and why we do not analyze them

The main text tests one region. This section reports what happened when we tried to extend the test to a second one, and why that attempt does not appear there. The short answer is that the southeast-Missouri material is not adequate for the test at present, and not because its phases are sounder or weaker than the basin's, but because its classes and its sample sizes measure something other than the stylistic transmission the criterion is built for. We report the attempt because a negative of this kind is useful to the next person who considers the same extension, and because it is the paper's own empirical-sufficiency requirement applied to itself.

### What we tried

Williams [-@williams_1954] divided the southeast-Missouri lowlands into four named Mississippian phases, on a survey independent of the Phillips-Ford-Griffin work, which makes it the obvious second case. We digitized his survey tables and georeferenced the sites through the Lower Mississippi Survey grid, which places his assemblages and the basin's in one coordinate frame (Figure S7). Across the named physiographic regions the cultural $F_{ST}$ is 0.11, most of which is a Woodland-to-Mississippian composition gradient rather than synchronic social structure. Restricting the analysis to assemblages with at least 30 sherds in classes other than the designated Woodland classes, and to those classes, gives 39 assemblages from 24 coordinate locations over 42 classes and collapses the between-cluster $F_{ST}$ to 0.034 across four spatial clusters. Multiple assemblages from one location remain separate observation nodes, which already limits their independence.

Run through the same calibrated comparison as the basin, that set behaves much as the basin does. The assemblages do not separate into bounded clusters on the ordination (Figure S8A and B). Ten combinations in the calibration series match the assemblages' diversity under pooled-profile innovation, and at the matched combination most favorable to drift (120 learners, innovation 0.012, mixing 0.005, great-circle distance) the observed $F_{ST}$ lies above the drift interval of 0.004 to 0.032 (median 0.012), reached by 2.4 percent of simulated datasets, 2.2 percent reversed and 4.2 percent contemporaneous. Uniform innovation fits at no combination and overpredicts the differentiation several-fold at its nearest one (median 0.083). The bounded-groups process at multiplier 0.03 leaves the median at 0.013 and brackets the observation only through its upper tail (Figure S8C). Taken at face value this looks like the basin's result reached a second time, and it is the verdict Fox [-@fox_1998] reached for these same phases by cluster analysis and ordination.

### Why we do not report it as a result

Taking it at face value would be a mistake, for three reasons that only appear once the classes are examined rather than counted (`analyses/66_cmv_class_composition.py`).

**The set is ninety percent plain ware.** Mississippi Plain alone is 81.5 percent of the 8,958 analyzed sherds, Bell Plain a further 7.5 percent, and the four plain classes together 90.1 percent. The basin analysis runs on ten Phillips-Ford-Griffin decorated types and contains no plain ware at all. Plain wares are utility pottery, and the coarse-versus-fine paste distinction between Mississippi Plain and Bell Plain is a manufacturing and functional variable rather than a stylistic one. It is also where most of the differentiation lives. The plain component alone gives a between-cluster $F_{ST}$ of 0.065, and the Mississippi-versus-Bell ratio alone 0.076, both well above the 0.034 reported for the full set. A between-group difference in that variable is the signature Table 1 files under environmental patchiness, which the criterion explicitly cannot separate from bounded interaction on this evidence alone.

**The stylistic part is too small to test.** Only 883 sherds, 9.9 percent of the set, are non-plain, a median of 16 per assemblage spread across 38 classes. Applying the study's own 30-sherd minimum to decorated material leaves 12 of the 39 assemblages. The basin set carries 27,924 decorated sherds, a median of 467 per assemblage across ten classes.

**What decorated material there is, is mostly surface film rather than design, and is chronologically loaded.** Varney Red is 38.7 percent of the non-plain sherds and Old Town Red Filmed a further 17.4 percent. Varney Red is a chronologically diagnostic red-filmed ware that the Woodland exclusion does not catch, so its spatial patterning carries the very gradient that exclusion was meant to remove. Dropping it moves the $F_{ST}$ of the named non-plain types from 0.351 to 0.180. Twenty-nine of the 38 non-plain classes occur in only one of the four spatial clusters while holding 302 sherds between them, so rarity and spatial restriction are confounded and cannot be separated at this sample size.

The non-plain subset does differentiate strongly (0.325), and that is not an artifact of small samples. Resampling every assemblage from the regional pooled profile at its own observed count gives a median of 0.003. But neither is it a clean measurement of style, for the two reasons just given. The honest statement is not that southeast Missouri is differentiated or undifferentiated. It is that the set cannot resolve the question the basin set is being asked.

### How this shows up in the modeling

The composition problem is visible in the model's behavior, which is what first drew our attention to it. Of the four assumptions relaxed above, the innovation boundary closes the difference in the basin gradually and predictably. From a permuted fraction of 0 to 0.3 the median $F_{ST}$ rises from 0.003 to 0.019, reaching the observed 0.018, while the diversity misfit falls from 9.9 to 3.7 percent before returning to 7.7. On the southeast-Missouri set the same change does not behave gradually. Its median barely moves while its upper tail becomes extremely large. At a permuted fraction of 0.11 the 97.5th percentile is 22 times the median, against 5 times in the basin, and pooled diversity $H_T$ is the summary that breaks at every step. Refining the grid to steps of 0.01 across 0.10 to 0.15 confirms that no combination both reaches the observed 0.034 and holds the diversity match. The median climbs only from 0.019 to 0.030 while the misfit rises from 8.3 to 17.0 percent. The cause is the plain ware. With the top two classes holding 89 percent of all sherds, a permutation of a group's innovation profile that catches Mississippi Plain moves the entire assemblage rather than its margins. The quantity being varied was the composition of utility pottery.

### A defect in the source table

Three classes in the digitized Williams tables are each carried twice, because their names differ only in capitalization. They are "Thin Ware" and "Thin ware" (5 and 49 sherds), "Fine Ware" and "Fine ware" (4 and 30), and "Brown Slip" and "Brown slip" (2 and 2). Together they are 92 sherds and nothing above turns on them, but they inflate the class count and should be merged at the source before anyone builds on this table.

### What would make the extension possible

A decorated-only analysis of these assemblages, on named types with the red films treated separately from incised and punctated design, and with sample sizes brought up by returning to the collections rather than to the published tables. Until then the southeast-Missouri phases stand as independent corroboration from Fox's ordination that the phases do not hold as groups, which is a different and weaker claim than the one the main text makes for the St. Francis basin.
