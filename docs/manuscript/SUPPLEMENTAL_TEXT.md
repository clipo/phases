## The four measures in full

**Departure from neutral copying.** Following Neiman [-@neiman_1995], we estimate the transmission parameter $\theta$ two ways for each assemblage. The homozygosity estimator uses the unbiased sample homozygosity $F = \sum_i n_i(n_i-1)/[N(N-1)]$, where $n_i$ is the count of class $i$ and $N$ the assemblage size, and inverts the neutral relation $F = 1/(1+\theta)$ to give $\theta_F = (1-F)/F$. The richness estimator solves the Ewens [-@ewens_1972] expectation for the number of classes $k$,

$$\mathbb{E}[k \mid \theta, N] = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \frac{\theta}{\theta + i},$$

for the $\theta_E$ that reproduces the observed $k$. Under neutral copying the two agree. Conformist copying concentrates sherds on common classes, raising $F$ and lowering $\theta_F$ relative to $\theta_E$, and a preference for novelty does the reverse. The measure is the ratio $\theta_F/\theta_E$, computed within spatial clusters and averaged across them, since pooling assemblages from divergent groups would cancel the effect.

**Seriation structure.** We use the iterative deterministic seriation method of Lipo, Madsen, and Dunnell [-@lipo_madsen_dunnell_2015; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_hunt_1997; @dunnell_1970; @graves_cachola-abad_1996; @marquardt_1978; @cowgill_1972; @obrien_lyman_1999; @lyman_obrien_2006]. A set of assemblages is co-seriable if some ordering makes every decorated class's frequency curve single-peaked and without gaps. We enumerate the maximal co-seriable groups, the largest subsets for which a valid ordering exists, and report their number, their maximum size, and the number of groups each assemblage joins. A single group indicates one interacting community; many small, overlapping groups indicate a field too varied to fit one ordering; many small, separate groups indicate divided learning. Checking every ordering is intractable beyond about fourteen assemblages, so the method builds solutions upward from valid three-assemblage orderings, adding assemblages while every class stays single-peaked and continuous. An ordering breaks when a class is not single-peaked along it or when a class's relative frequency jumps between adjacent assemblages by more than a continuity threshold, which we set at 0.10. The overlapping structure reported in the main text holds across a range of thresholds. In the recovery experiment and the observed trajectory the statistic is the number of maximal groups per assemblage within each period, computed the same way. The idealized validation (Figure S1) uses a simpler proxy, the number of fall-then-rise reversals in each class along the supplied order. Neither version includes the bootstrap significance step of the published method.

**Between- and within-group variance.** Cultural $F_{ST}$ is computed on Gini-Simpson diversity $H = 1 - \sum_i p_i^2$ as

$$F_{ST} = \frac{H_T - H_S}{H_T},$$

where $H_T$ is the diversity of the pooled assemblages and $H_S$ the size-weighted mean diversity within clusters, which is Nei's [-@nei_1973] diversity form of the cultural $F_{ST}$ of Bell, Richerson, and McElreath [-@bell_richerson_mcelreath_2009]. It is zero when clusters share a repertoire and rises as they hold distinct ones.

**Boundary excess.** We measure Brainerd-Robinson similarity $S_{ij} = 200 - \sum_k |100\,p_{ik} - 100\,p_{jk}|$ between every pair of assemblages. A Mantel correlation cannot separate a sharp boundary from a smooth fade with distance, so within each band of pairwise distance we take mean within-cluster similarity minus mean between-cluster similarity and average over bands. Under pure isolation by distance the excess is near zero; a sharp social boundary makes it large and positive. Spatial clusters come from $k$-means on site coordinates with multiple restarts, with the number of clusters chosen by the mean silhouette.

**Trends and subsampling.** Each measure's trend along the sequence is its rank correlation with position. All size control draws 50 sherds from each assemblage without replacement; an assemblage of exactly 50 is unchanged. The three measures the recovery experiment shows to be unusable are averaged over 400 draws. The $F_{ST}$ trend is averaged over 4,000 draws, and we quote the spread of single draws (2.5th to 97.5th percentiles, -0.80 to +0.40) rather than the Monte Carlo error of the mean, because a single draw has a standard deviation of 0.42 and the spread is what says whether the record resolves the trend. All of these condition on the observed assemblages, clusters, and order rather than on uncertainty about deposits, chronology, or model choice.

## The idealized validation model

The validation generator copies decorated variants under neutral transmission [@neiman_1995; @bentley_hahn_shennan_2004] and has three controls: how far group class profiles diverge, how strongly potters conform within groups (a frequency-dependent copying bias after Henrich [-@henrich_2001]), and how group profiles are placed on the landscape. Four settings produce the four processes of Table S1. Bounded groups raise divergence and conformity together across spatially bounded groups. Environmental patchiness holds a fixed difference between groups under neutral copying, keyed to an environmental layout. Conformity in one community raises conformity in a single undivided pool with no divergence. Smooth distance-limited copying produces a gradient under neutral copying. Each setting emits a sequence of group-by-class samples along an order, and the four-measure procedure is run on each without knowledge of its source, across 500 random seeds. On the bounded-group runs we also compute the correlations among the four measures, to confirm that their joint movement is not redundancy. These assemblages are large and clean (12 groups, several hundred sherds each), so the test shows what the measures can do in principle; the record-matched recovery (Figure S2) shows what survives on the real record.

## Can the record detect a boundary?

A process that genuinely divides a population into groups should leave its mark on every aspect of how decoration was copied, whereas a process that only resembles it leaves a mark on some aspects and not others. We therefore began with four measures, each a different consequence of restricted interaction (defined above), and asked which of them this record can use. Only a bounded interaction group drives all four together (Table S1). Environmental patchiness produces spatial structure without departing from neutral copying or breaking the seriation. Conformity within a single community departs from neutral copying without creating any boundaries. Smooth distance-limited copying produces only a fading of similarity with distance. Copying on a finite network is less tidy than that smooth ideal, because chance divergence among places raises $F_{ST}$ and the boundary excess with no social boundary present [@lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021].

On idealized data the four measures behave as designed (Figure S1). When interaction narrows toward closed groups all four rise together, whereas the three other processes each move only some of them. Under closure the four are tightly correlated (mean absolute correlation 0.88), and under the other processes they are nearly independent (0.14 to 0.28), so their joint movement carries information.

A real record is smaller, noisier, and time-averaged, so a measure that works in principle may fail in practice. We built one synthetic assemblage for each real one, at its real sample size, spatial cluster, and position in the sequence, and imposed closure of a known strength *s*, from 0 (no groups) to 1 (sharply separated groups), rising along the sequence. We time-averaged the synthetic assemblages by blending neighboring periods, subsampled them exactly as we did the real ones, and asked which measures recovered the imposed closure [@crema_edinborough_kerig_shennan_2014; @kandler_shennan_2015; @perreault_2019; @holdaway_wandsnider_2008]. Only cultural $F_{ST}$ survives (Figure S2). It rises steadily as imposed closure increases. The departure from neutral copying does not move in one direction as conformity increases, the boundary excess does not respond to the two spatial clusters the site layout supports, and the number of seriation groups does not rise as closure predicts. The section "Why only cultural $F_{ST}$ survives" below shows that this reduction is a property of the record rather than of how closure was imposed.

Treating the simulation series as a likelihood turns the observed trend into a posterior for how much closure the basin could contain, under a uniform prior over *s*. This posterior refers to closure that grows along the sequence; a boundary present from the start would not produce a trend, and the phase-line tests of the main text address that case. If the phases were becoming bounded groups, differences between them should grow as interaction narrows. Along the seriation order they do not (Figure S3), although that order is largely south to north rather than early to late, so this test speaks to change across the basin more than to change through time. The raw trend in cultural $F_{ST}$ along the sequence is strongly negative, a rank correlation of -0.80, but that trend is a sampling artifact: assemblage size also rises along the sequence (Spearman +0.61). Once every assemblage is subsampled to a common 50 sherds, the trend averages -0.048 over 4,000 subsamples, and a single subsample returns anything from -0.80 to +0.40. The record resolves no trend. Two of the six periods hold assemblages from a single spatial cluster and carry no between-cluster variance, so the trend rests on four. Read against the recovery experiment, a trend this small corresponds to closure with a posterior median of 0.15 and a 95 percent credible interval of 0.00 to 0.37, with a probability of 0.001 on closure of 0.5 or more. With the record's time-averaging built into the simulations the posterior widens to a median of 0.22 and an interval of 0.00 to 0.57, and 9 percent of it lies at 0.5 or above. Reversing the order barely changes this: the median becomes 0.17 (0.00 to 0.39), or 0.25 (0.00 to 0.60) with time-averaging. The record is consistent with no closure and with weak closure growing along the sequence, and shows no moderate or strong closure of that kind.

## Bayesian estimates of the $F_{ST}$ trend

The section above reports the $F_{ST}$ trend as a rank correlation on subsampled assemblages. We also estimate it without discarding data. The Balding-Nichols model is fitted to each period, giving a posterior for that period's $F_{ST}$ whose width reflects how many sherds it holds, and the trend is estimated as a slope across those posteriors. The prior on $F_{ST}$ is Beta(1,10). A flat prior on the parameter is not flat on what it predicts: at this design it expects a median $F_{ST}$ of 0.236 and places 44 percent of its predictive mass above 0.30, against an observed 0.0062, whereas Beta(1,10) expects a median of 0.026 with 2 percent above 0.30. Because the model treats sampling as part of the data rather than removing it by subsampling, its uncertainty is a credible interval rather than a subsampling spread, and its trend is a slope in $F_{ST}$ units per standardized period rather than a rank correlation. The per-period posteriors are in Table S2.

## The spatial model with no groups

The between-cluster $F_{ST}$ requires dividing the basin into clusters. The spatial model removes that requirement. It treats each assemblage's decorated-class composition as a multinomial draw whose class probabilities, on the logit scale, are the sum of a class mean, a spatially correlated surface over the assemblage coordinates, and an assemblage-specific term with no spatial structure. The surface has a Matérn-family covariance with amplitude $\sigma$ and length scale $\rho$, and the assemblage-specific term has standard deviation $\tau$. In the version we report, the assemblage-specific term enters the covariance directly rather than as a second latent surface, which halves the number of latent parameters and removes a direction along which the two terms trade off. The quantity of interest is the spatial share, $\sigma^2 / (\sigma^2 + \tau^2)$, the fraction of compositional variance among assemblages carried by location. Bounded groups leave site-specific character that location does not explain, and in this model that character can enter only through $\tau$. The length scale has an inverse-gamma prior placing its mass inside the range the basin's geometry can resolve. The models are written in Stan and fitted by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (rstan 2.32); model files and scripts are in the repository (`stan/spatial/`, `analyses/02_spatial/`).

Fitted to the 28 assemblages and ten classes, the spatial share is 0.996 (95 percent credible interval 0.972 to 1.000) in the reported form and 0.997 (0.975 to 1.000) with the assemblage term as a second surface. The length scale is not identified. Its posterior median is 195 km (95 percent credible interval 96 to 292 km) in the reported form, longer than the basin itself, and the sampler saturates its maximum tree depth on every iteration without divergences, the mark of a ridge in the posterior rather than a step-size problem. Diagnostics for the reported form: R-hat 1.0020, bulk effective sample size 2,211, tail effective sample size 7,241, 0 divergent transitions of 12,000, all 12,000 iterations at the maximum tree depth of 10, E-BFMI 0.688; for the two-surface form, R-hat 1.0157, bulk effective sample size 438, 0 divergences, all iterations at the maximum tree depth, E-BFMI 0.793 (`output/findings/gp_basin_fit.md`). The spatial share, which does not depend on where along the ridge the length scale sits, is well sampled; the length scale is not. We therefore report the length scale as unresolved, and read the fit as showing no detectable fade in composition across the St. Francis drainage beyond what the surface already carries. Recovery tests on simulated surfaces with known length scales show that this design resolves scales only well below the basin's extent (`analyses/02_spatial/01_recovery.R`, `analyses/48_length_scale_recovery.py`).

The model reproduces quantities it was not fitted to. Its predictive interval for the between-cluster $F_{ST}$ at three clusters is 0.0128 to 0.0188 (median 0.0157) against an observed 0.0156 (Bayesian predictive p = 0.525). For the fade of similarity with distance it is -0.367 to -0.274 (median -0.321) against -0.341 (p = 0.78), for mean within-assemblage diversity 0.550 to 0.575 against 0.563 (p = 0.57), and for the spread of that diversity 0.096 to 0.122 against 0.112 (p = 0.295). A surface with no groups therefore reproduces the observed differentiation at three clusters, which the copying model does not, as well as the fade with distance and the diversity structure. The shorter-range variation the copying model misses is smooth enough for a surface with no edges to carry it. It describes the pattern rather than a mechanism, so it complements the copying model rather than competing with it.

### How cultural $F_{ST}$ depends on the division

The between-cluster $F_{ST}$ depends on how many clusters the basin is cut into. Fitted with the Balding-Nichols model, its posterior median moves from 0.0062 (95 percent credible interval 0.0051 to 0.0076) at two clusters, through 0.0155 (0.0133 to 0.0180) at three, to 0.0202 (0.0175 to 0.0232) at five and 0.0320 (0.0283 to 0.0362) at six, and the intervals at the two ends do not overlap. It also depends on where the cuts fall. The $k$-means division at two clusters is stable, since twenty seeds return it, and unremarkable, at the 42nd percentile of 150 alternative contiguous divisions into two (median 0.0067), but those alternatives range from 0.0007 to 0.0160 between their 5th and 95th percentiles. A quantity this sensitive to the cutting cannot be read on its own. This is why the main text reports the spatial model alongside any between-cluster value, and why every between-cluster value is compared against the copying model at the same number of clusters.

## The minimum sample size, and why it is 75

The plug-in $F_{ST}$ is biased upward by sampling. Two assemblages drawn from the same population of pots differ because each is a finite sample, and the spurious variance between them scales roughly as one over the sample size. To find the count at which that artifact stops dominating, we drew pairs of multinomial samples of size $n$ from the basin's pooled class profile, 4,000 pairs at each size, and computed $F_{ST}$ between them. The two share one profile by construction, so all of the $F_{ST}$ between them is sampling (`analyses/82_minimum_sample_size.py`). The reference is the observed between-cluster $F_{ST}$ at five clusters, 0.0206.

|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | 0.0146 | 0.0630 | 71% |
| 50 | 0.0070 | 0.0305 | 34% |
| **75** | 0.0044 | 0.0195 | 22% |
| 100 | 0.0034 | 0.0156 | 16% |
| 150 | 0.0023 | 0.0105 | 11% |
| 200 | 0.0016 | 0.0074 | 8% |
| 300 | 0.0011 | 0.0052 | 5% |
| 500 | 0.0006 | 0.0030 | 3% |
| 1000 | 0.0003 | 0.0015 | 2% |

At 75 sherds the median artifact is about a fifth of the observed five-cluster differentiation and its 95th percentile (0.0195) lies just below it, whereas at 50 sherds the 95th percentile (0.0305) exceeds it by half. The five-cluster value is the reference because it is the finest division analyzed cluster by cluster (analyses 72 and 85), where clusters hold the fewest sherds and a sampling artifact would matter most. Pooling into clusters shrinks the artifact further: redrawing every assemblage from the pooled profile at its own sherd count gives each five-cluster term at most 0.0003 (the section on the finer-scale differences below). Against the two-cluster value of 0.0062 the artifact is proportionally larger at every sample size, which is why the copying model is always sampled at the real sherd counts: it then carries the same artifact as the data. Above 75 the bias shrinks slowly, by about six percentage points between 75 and 100, while assemblages are lost quickly: a minimum of 100 would remove three more basin assemblages (Davis, Irby, and Grant). The analytic expectation for two equal samples from one profile is close to $1/n$. Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003] kept sites with 800 or more sherds in all; decorated sherds are 13 percent of his table, so his rule corresponds to about 100 decorated sherds. The copying model is sampled at the observed sherd counts and carries the same bias, so the model comparison is not distorted by it. The minimum protects readings of single small clusters, where a value computed on a few dozen sherds would be mostly artifact.

## Robustness checks

Several further checks are provided in the supplementary code. Neiman's [-@neiman_1995] sample-size diagnostic correlates the two neutrality estimates, and their difference, with assemblage size and with position in the sequence, to separate a transmission signal from a sample-size artifact. Neiman's squared-Euclidean distance between assemblages is computed with its relation to diversity and its trend along the sequence. Measures of clustering tendency on the correspondence-analysis ordination favor a single group: the Hopkins statistic is 0.71, where 0.5 indicates no tendency, the gap statistic favors one cluster, and the best silhouette is 0.54 [@hopkins_skellam_1954; @rousseeuw_1987; @tibshirani_walther_hastie_2001]. The assemblages form a near-continuum rather than sharply bounded groups.

An earlier sensitivity grid recomputed the raw, unsubsampled trends of the three continuous measures across combinations of a latitude cutoff, the number of periods, and the number of clusters (`output/sensitivity_grid.md`). It predates the phase-based definition of the basin and runs on latitude-defined sets of 38, 36, and 17 assemblages, several of its cells duplicate one another, and its raw trends carry the sample-size artifact the recovery experiment removes, so we do not use it. We report it because two of its cells show all three raw trends rising together, and a reader of the code should know why that does not bear on the result.

## Why only cultural $F_{ST}$ survives

The recovery experiment imposes closure as divergence between clusters, which is what cultural $F_{ST}$ measures, so it might only certify an $F_{ST}$ test finding an $F_{ST}$-shaped pattern. We tested this by imposing closure through two further routes, each matched to a different measure, and asking which measure recovers each (`analyses/22_generator_diagnostic.py`, 60 seeds, the real configuration of 28 assemblages, two clusters, six periods, subsampled to 50). One route raises conformity in a single undivided pool, which the neutral departure is designed to detect. The other groups assemblages into social groups scrambled relative to the spatial clusters, which seriation structure is designed to detect.

Neither route is recovered by the measure designed for it. Under conformity in one pool, the neutral departure falls rather than rises (its mean trend moves by -0.73 between no closure and strong closure) and no other measure responds. Under the scrambled social groups, the seriation measure barely moves (-0.21). $F_{ST}$, by contrast, responds to group structure however it is organized: its mean trend rises from +0.01 to +0.96 under spatial divergence and from +0.07 to +0.77 under the scrambled social groups, because both raise between-group variance somewhere the division can see it. Subsampling more deeply, to 100 or 150 sherds, does not rescue the other measures. The reduction to $F_{ST}$ is therefore a property of the record, 28 time-averaged assemblages over ten aggregated classes, rather than of the way closure was imposed. It also sets a limit stated in the main text: closure carried purely by conformity, or by social groups that do not follow the spatial clusters, would escape detection here at any strength.

## The basin comparison in detail

We simulated neutral copying and a bounded-groups variant on the real layout of the 28 assemblages, using the model and calibration described in the next section. The calibration is itself informative. Under uniform innovation, in which the ten classes are interchangeable, one of the 297 parameter combinations (120 learners, innovation 0.0002, mixing 0.4) falls within the 10 percent tolerance on both the six-run screen and the fifty-run check. It sits at the corner of the grid, at the highest mixing rate swept, and its within-assemblage and pooled diversities of 0.62 and 0.65, against 0.56 and 0.59 observed, are close to the tolerance limit, so we report it without treating it as a robust calibration. Under pooled-profile innovation, 50 combinations match on the screen and 45 on the check, spanning populations of 120, 2,000 and 10,000 learners and low rates of innovation and mixing (Table S3). None of the 45 reaches the observed between-cluster $F_{ST}$ at its median; the highest fifty-run median is 0.0027 against 0.0062 observed, and 19 of the 45 include the observation within their 95 percent ranges.

At the matched combination most favorable to the model (2,000 learners, innovation 0.001, mixing 0.02, interaction length 24 km along the rivers), the observed between-cluster $F_{ST}$ of 0.0062 at two clusters lies inside the simulated range of 0.0002 to 0.0113 (median 0.0023, 500 runs). Sixteen percent of simulated datasets reach it in seriation order, 14 percent in reverse order, and 21 percent when assemblages are treated as contemporaneous. The model returns a single community in 464 of 500 runs sampled through time. Where it returns two or more, the difference between communities has a median of 0.012 (95 percent range 0.007 to 0.024) against an observed 0.039, which none of those 36 runs reaches, and modularity is near zero in every run (95 percent range up to 0.005) against an observed 0.045. The fade of similarity with distance lies inside the model's range (observed rank correlation -0.20; simulated median -0.18, range -0.53 to +0.04). The boundary excess of the spatial clusters is +13.7 in the data, outside the model's range (-6.4 to +8.6) and the bounded-groups range at a copying factor of 0.03 and interaction length 24 km (+0.1 to +12.4), though not at 12 km (-8.5 to +16.8). Weakening copying across the two clusters has a visible effect at this mixing rate only at its strongest: at a copying factor of 0.03 the median $F_{ST}$ is 0.0080 at 24 km and 0.0064 at 12 km, reaching the observation in about half of 30 runs, against medians of 0.0023 and 0.0047 with no boundary; factors of 0.5 and 0.1 give medians of 0.0022 to 0.0071 (Table S4, Figure S5). New variants in this model come from the regional pooled profile, a source no boundary interrupts; the section on the phases as groups below gives the boundary model local innovation as well.

## The neutral copying model and its calibration

The comparison uses one finite-population network model of decorated-class transmission, run on the real geography of the 28 assemblages. Each assemblage location is a node holding a population of learners. Each generation, a learner copies the class of another learner, drawn from its own node with probability $1-m$ and from another node with probability $m$, the other node chosen with a weight that falls off exponentially with distance along the rivers at length scale $L$. With probability $\mu$ the learner instead adopts a class by innovation. The model tracks class frequencies rather than individual learners: next-generation frequencies at each node are a multinomial draw of $N$ learners from the copying-plus-innovation probabilities, which is equivalent to independent learner-by-learner copying. The repertoire is fixed at the ten measured classes. The bounded-groups variant multiplies every copying weight that crosses a specified division by a factor $\ell$ before normalizing; $\ell = 1$ is copying by distance alone, and 0.5, 0.1, and 0.03 impose progressively stronger boundaries on the basin's spatial clusters. Each run discards 1,200 burn-in generations and records 90 evenly spaced samples over 1,800 further generations.

Innovation requires a choice about what an archaeological class is. Under uniform innovation, the standard K-allele model, an innovating learner adopts any class with equal probability, treating the classes as interchangeable. Under pooled-profile innovation, an innovating learner adopts each class in proportion to its share of the region's pooled counts, reflecting that the culture-history types lump unequal numbers of underlying decorative variants. The pooled profile carries no information about any division, so it does not build the tested boundary into the model, and neither version introduces a copying bias.

Between-group predictions depend on population size, mixing, and innovation together, so the model is calibrated before any between-group comparison. The calibration uses three within-assemblage summaries that do not involve the tested division: mean within-assemblage Gini-Simpson diversity, mean number of classes present at the observed sherd counts, and Gini-Simpson diversity of the pooled counts. A grid of population size (120, 2,000, and 10,000 learners), innovation rate (0.0002 to 0.2), and mixing rate (0.001 to 0.4) is screened at six runs per combination, with the same sampling through time applied to the data, and a combination is matched when all three simulated summaries fall within 10 percent of the observed values. Every matched combination is then rerun fifty times with fresh seeds and the match rechecked, and the baseline is the combination, among those still matched, with the highest fifty-run median between-cluster $F_{ST}$, so the comparison is as favorable to the model as the grid allows. The second stage exists because the highest of about fifty six-run medians is mostly sampling noise: in the current series the six-run winner's six-run median (0.0053) is twice its fifty-run median (0.0027), and an independent fifty-seed check with fresh seeds gives 0.0025 (`output/findings/calibration_stability.md`). Even at fifty runs the ranking of the leading combinations is not sharp: the independent check agrees with the six-run ranking at a rank correlation of 0.71, and its first-ranked combination (2,000 learners, innovation 0.001, mixing 0.01) failed the fifty-run diversity confirmation in the calibration itself. We therefore repeat the key comparisons at the six most favorable confirmed combinations, and at that unconfirmed one (Table S6). If no combination survived the check, the matched combination with the highest fifty-run median would be used and flagged. The baseline is then run 500 times, the boundary factors 30 times each at interaction lengths of 12 and 24 km, and seven sensitivity cases (burn-in doubled, a single-class starting state with and without burn-in, and innovation and mixing halved and doubled) 50 times each. In the baseline runs of this analysis, the share of simulated datasets reaching the observed value is reported as $(e+1)/(R+1)$, where $e$ is the number of runs at or above the observed statistic and $R$ the number of runs; the scale sweep, cell-sensitivity, phases-as-groups and power tables report the plain share $e/R$. Every run is cached against a fingerprint of the configuration, inputs, source code, and package versions, and rebuilt when any of them changes.

Copying distance is measured along the rivers because interaction in this settlement system plausibly followed watercourses, and because that choice is the one more favorable to the model. We build a graph from the mapped hydrography (LMVHydrology centerlines clipped to the basin with a 25 km margin), join vertices within 250 m of one another, keep the largest connected piece, and attach each assemblage to its nearest node, adding the attachment distance at both ends. All 28 assemblages connect, and the mean distance from an assemblage to a mapped channel is 2.77 km. Paths along the rivers are longer than straight lines by a mean ratio of 3.7 and a median of 2.9 per pair; the mean is pulled up by pairs that are close in a straight line but must route around a meander. The pottery does not follow the rivers more closely than it follows straight lines. Decorated-class distance between assemblages correlates with straight-line distance at 0.36 and with river distance at 0.24, and once straight-line distance is held fixed, river distance adds nothing (-0.02). Between assemblages in different phases, straight-line distance correlates at 0.32 and river distance at 0.09. The network is modern hydrography, and the St. Francis, Tyronza, and Mississippi have all moved since these sites were occupied. Both measures are carried through the relaxations below. At every interaction length from 6 to 96 km the median under straight-line distance is at or below the median under river distance at the calibrated combination, so river distance is the choice more favorable to the model.

The assemblages are not contemporaneous, so the simulated field is sampled through time rather than at one moment. Each simulated assemblage is drawn from the recorded sample matching its real counterpart's position on the first correspondence-analysis axis and accumulated over a trailing window of eight samples, so that like a real deposit it mixes production across a span. The earliest position maps to the end of the first complete window, so every assemblage gets the same window length. Each simulated assemblage is then subsampled to its real counterpart's sherd count and analyzed exactly as the real ones are, with the same $k$-means clusters, the same community detection, and the same $F_{ST}$. Because the direction of the seriation axis is only partly anchored, every run is also sampled in reverse order and as if contemporaneous, with every assemblage drawn from the final window. Which source an assemblage's counts came from matters little. Within each of the three five-cluster groups holding at least two rows of each kind, we computed the posterior probability that rows carrying the survey's counts alone differ from rows carrying Lipo's compilation more than a random split of that group into subsets of the same sizes, with each assemblage's class proportions drawn from Dirichlet(counts + 1/2) (`analyses/78_source_effect.py`). It is 0.04 and 0.15 in two of them and 0.89 in the third (Big Eddy, Castile Landing, Clay Hill, Cramor Place, and Nickel), where source and place cannot be separated with five assemblages. The rows carrying the survey's counts alone are also the smallest, so source and sample size are confounded throughout.

Because community labels are arbitrary and switch between runs, we summarize the basin's simulated communities by co-membership (Figure S6). For each assemblage we record the share of runs in which it falls in the same community as Parkin. Under the calibrated model that share is 0.94 to 1.00 for every assemblage, because the model returns a single community in most of its runs. An earlier, uncalibrated model had produced a graded field of co-membership falling away from Parkin, which was a product of its excess diversity. Under pooled-profile innovation, the 36 time-sampled runs that return two or more communities give a between-community $F_{ST}$ with a median of 0.012 (95 percent range 0.007 to 0.024), and none of them reaches the observed 0.039. Under uniform innovation, which does not fit the assemblages' diversity robustly, the observed value falls within a wide interval.

## Relaxing four simplifications of the copying model

At two clusters the calibrated model covers the observed differentiation, but its median falls short of it, and from three clusters on the observed value lies outside its range (main text Table 1; Table S5). The model makes four simplifications, any of which could account for that shortfall, and relaxing one does not abandon neutral copying: each variant is the same model with one simplification removed, run on the basin at its calibrated combination. The standard is the same throughout. A variant accounts for the difference only if it reaches the observed $F_{ST}$ of 0.0062 *and* keeps all three diversity summaries within the 10 percent tolerance, because a model that reaches the differentiation by losing the diversity fit has traded one fit for the other. Each table gives the median $F_{ST}$, its 95 percent range, the shortfall (observed divided by the model's median), and whether diversity stays matched. The full series is in `output/findings/other_departures.md`.

## The phases as groups: a model with every advantage

The bounded-groups model above draws new variants from one regional pool, which no boundary interrupts, so it is at a disadvantage against the one account that reproduces the shortfall, local innovation. Here the published phases are the groups, and both properties of a bounded group are given to them together (`analyses/84_phases_as_groups.py`): copying across phase lines is multiplied by a factor of 1 (no copying boundary), 0.5, 0.1, or 0.03, and each phase draws new variants from a partly reordered version of the regional profile, with 0, 10, 20, or 30 percent of the classes reordered. Each of the 16 combinations is run 300 times at the calibrated combination (2,000 learners, innovation 0.001, mixing 0.02), with interaction length 24 km along the rivers, and each run is scored on the between-phase $F_{ST}$, the $F_{ST}$ at the two spatial clusters, the boundary excess at the phase lines, and the three diversity summaries. The observed values are a between-phase $F_{ST}$ of 0.0110 and a boundary excess at the phase lines of +10.0.

We summarize the grid with a posterior over its 16 cells, estimated by rejection approximate Bayesian computation: a uniform prior over cells, accepting the 5 percent of all 4,800 runs closest to the observed values on the six standardized summaries. The prior places three quarters of its mass on some copying boundary, which leans toward the groups hypothesis, and three quarters on some local innovation. The posterior probability of a copying boundary is 0.79, and of local innovation 0.65. Across acceptance rates of 1, 2, 5, and 10 percent, and with the summaries restricted to the between-phase $F_{ST}$ and the boundary excess, the first runs from 0.75 to 0.83 and the second from 0.65 to 0.73. To see whether the design could detect a boundary, we drew 40 runs from each of four cells with a boundary built in (copying factor 0.1 or 0.03, local innovation 0 or 0.2) and passed each through the same posterior with itself excluded. The median posterior probability of a boundary was 0.81 to 0.95, against 0.66 for runs from the no-boundary cell. The observed 0.79 lies just below the with-boundary range and above the no-boundary value: the record leans slightly toward a copying boundary without resolving it, and the copying model neither requires nor excludes one. ## Tests in the time domain

The four measures describe states along the sequence rather than the changes between them. Two further tests model change directly while respecting the record's time-averaging, and three shorter checks follow.

**Copying bias inferred by simulation.** We simulate copying scenarios ranging from strongly conformist through neutral to novelty-seeking, keep those that reproduce the real assemblages, and infer the copying bias from the survivors, with time-averaging built into each simulation [@crema_kandler_shennan_2016; @carrignon_bentley_ruck_2019; @crema_edinborough_kerig_shennan_2014]. The model is frequency-dependent copying among the ten classes in a finite population: a class's adoption weight is proportional to its frequency raised to the power $1+b$, where $b>0$ is conformity, $b<0$ anti-conformity, and $b=0$ neutral copying, with an innovation rate that mixes in a uniform draw from the classes. An assemblage pools production over a window of generations. We placed broad priors on innovation, $b$, population size, and window length, and fitted the model by sequential Monte Carlo approximate Bayesian computation (800 particles, six rounds of shrinking tolerance, local-linear regression adjustment), matching each simulated six-period sequence to the observed diversity trajectory and rank-frequency profile [@toni_etal_2009; @beaumont_zhang_balding_2002]. The posterior of $b$ is concentrated near neutral, with mean +0.003 and a 95 percent interval of -0.035 to +0.039 that includes zero, and means of +0.001 to +0.004 across five independent runs (Figure S8A). Its standard deviation is 0.017 against a prior of 0.289, so the data constrain it strongly. P(b > 0) is 0.62 in the primary run and 0.53 to 0.63 across runs, so even the direction of any lean is unstable. Fitting the two halves of the order separately recovers no shift between them (-0.000 and +0.012). Simulation-based calibration over 200 datasets drawn from the prior gives ranks consistent with uniform (Kolmogorov-Smirnov D = 0.088, p = 0.086; Figure S8B) [@talts_etal_2018], and an independent rejection-sampling fit agrees (mean $b$ = +0.019, 95 percent interval -0.062 to +0.054).

Two caveats apply. The calibration battery also reports coverage of the nominal 95 percent interval at nine fixed values of $b$ (Table S7), and coverage falls to 0.65 at $b = +0.10$ and 0.75 at $b = -0.20$ (`output/abc_smc_validation.md`). The battery used 300 particles and five rounds rather than the empirical fit's 800 and six, and its synthetic data come from the same pooled-population simulator that is fitted, whereas the real observations are spatially distributed assemblages pooled into six periods. The battery therefore checks the numerical inference under the assumed simulator, not whether that simulator describes how the record formed. We treat the interval as consistent with weak or absent basin-wide conformity, conditional on the pooled model, rather than as a demonstrated exclusion of strong conformity. Because the model pools the whole basin, it also says nothing about localized or group-specific conformity, which the spatial measures and the copying model address.

**Tempo and mode.** The second test asks whether the assemblages' diversity wanders without direction, heads steadily one way, or is pulled back toward a stable value, the distinctions among an unbiased random walk, a directional walk, stasis, and mean reversion [@hunt_2006; @hunt_bell_travis_2008]. Rather than choose among the four, we fit the model that contains them and report the two parameters that distinguish them, since unbiased motion, stasis, and mean reversion are regions of one parameter space. Along the seriation order as computed, decorated-class diversity runs from 0.666 at one end through 0.734 to 0.460 at the other, falling over the second half. Because the dates do not establish which end is later, the sign of any directional change is not established either. The mean-reversion rate has a posterior median of 0.77 (95 percent credible interval 0.05 to 10.7), spanning the unbiased and stasis limits and so unresolved. The directional drift along the order as computed leans negative but is not resolved (median -0.061, interval -0.154 to +0.035, P(drift > 0) = 0.071). On an earlier version of the matrix, which counted one class twice, the drift was resolved and negative; on the corrected counts the interval reaches zero. A decline of this kind is in any case what drift in a finite population produces, as the predictive check below shows. With six points the reversion rate and the drift remain partly confounded, since relaxation toward a lower optimum can imitate directional loss, which is why we report both parameters rather than a winning model (Figure S8C). Diagnostics for the fit: R-hat 1.008, minimum effective sample size 980, 18 divergent transitions of 8,000 (0.23 percent), E-BFMI 0.61. The companion fit meant to show that the design recovers a directional series did not converge (R-hat 1.18, minimum effective sample size 16, 1,079 divergent transitions of 8,000), so the design's ability to resolve directional change is not established, and we give the drift estimate no weight (`output/findings/tempo_mode_posterior.md`).

A third test, for the early-warning signs of a critical transition, cannot be resolved at this ordinal resolution. The rolling variance along the sequence shows no clear trend (Kendall tau = +0.09) and the lag-1 autocorrelation falls rather than rises (tau = -0.75), so neither behaves as a critical transition would (`output/tempo_mode_ews.md`). Because the ordering places similar assemblages next to one another, we report this in the code but do not rely on it [@scheffer_2009; @dakos_2012].

### Three further checks of neutrality along the sequence

The first follows each decorated class as it rises and falls through the sequence and asks whether those changes look like the undirected fluctuation of unbiased copying or like the directed change a marker would show. We fit the rescaled frequency increments of all ten classes in one hierarchical model with partial pooling, following the frequency increment construction [@feder_kryazhimskiy_plotkin_2014]. The population-level departure from unbiased copying has a posterior median of +0.008 with a 95 percent credible interval of -0.040 to +0.081, where unbiased copying implies zero, and P(departure > 0) = 0.64. Individual classes are not resolved: a class simulated with a substantial true departure is recovered at about a fifth of its size with an interval spanning zero, so the increments support the aggregate statement but not a per-class one.

The second asks whether a neutral model reproduces the observed diversity trajectory [@kandler_shennan_2013]. We draw population size and innovation rate from the posterior of the copying-bias inference and compare the observed trajectory with the resulting predictive distribution. The observed diversity falls inside the 95 percent predictive interval in all six periods, under the pooled posterior and under each of the five sampler seeds taken separately. The pooled Bayesian predictive p is 0.885, and it ranges from 0.634 to 0.915 across seeds. A neutral model is adequate for this trajectory, which is concordance rather than proof that copying was neutral; the predictive interval is wide because population size is itself uncertain.

The third asks whether the assemblages grow more different from one another toward contact, as fragmenting groups would. Neiman's distance between assemblages shows no resolved trend along the sequence: the posterior median rank correlation between mean within-period distance and position is +0.09, with a 95 percent credible interval of -0.53 to +0.63 over six periods. Distances between the two spatial clusters are only modestly larger than distances within them, a ratio of 1.28, and within-assemblage diversity is weakly and negatively related to an assemblage's mean distance from the others (posterior median -0.14, 95 percent credible interval -0.44 to +0.20), the direction a single shared community produces (Figure S4) [@neiman_1995].

## What recovers the phases, and the problem of zeros

Assemblages are clustered in a combined space: site coordinates in kilometers, centered and scaled so that the mean squared pairwise distance is one, joined to decorated-class composition scaled the same way and multiplied by the square root of a weight $w$. At $w = 0$ the representation is the site map alone; as $w$ grows it becomes the pottery alone. Each clustering is compared with Mainfort's published assignments by the adjusted Rand index. We ran three composition transforms, three clustering methods ($k$-means over 40 starting configurations, Ward linkage, and average linkage), and nine weights; the full grid is in `output/findings/phase_recovery.csv`. Three of the 28 assemblages carry a phase this study assigned by territory, a geographic rule, so every value quoted in the main text is computed on the 25 assemblages Mainfort assigned.

Class proportions are compositional data, and the centered log-ratio transform is the usual choice for them. It cannot be applied here without a decision that carries the result. Of the 280 cells in the basin matrix, 105 are zero, and the log of zero is undefined. Replacing zeros with a fraction of the smallest positive proportion lets that fraction set how strongly absence counts, and agreement with the phases follows it:

| zero convention | adjusted Rand index |
|---|---|
| pseudocount of 0.5 added to counts | 0.533 |
| floor at 2.0 times the smallest positive proportion | 0.607 |
| floor at 0.5 times the smallest positive proportion | 0.607 |
| floor at 0.1 times the smallest positive proportion | 0.766 |

All four use the same data, geography, and weight ($w = 0.5$); only the zero convention differs, and it moves the answer by a quarter of the index's range. We report the chi-square transform in the main text because it needs no such choice, and we use a pseudocount wherever the log-ratio transform appears. The result is also a caution about the phases themselves: a classification sensitive to which types are absent, applied to assemblages whose sherd counts differ more than twentyfold, will read sampling failure as cultural difference.

## Radiocarbon chronology

We calibrated the Mainfort [-@mainfort_2001] radiocarbon compilation against IntCal20 [@reimer_etal_2020]. Forty determinations come from seven sites in and near the St. Francis basin, nineteen of them from Parkin. The summed probability of the basin dates concentrates the occupation in the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries, with a median of AD 1428 (Parkin alone, AD 1483), and declines across the contact interval: 29 percent of the basin probability postdates AD 1541 and 18 percent postdates AD 1600 (Figure S9). This is consistent with occupation continuing through the De Soto entrada and declining afterward, although the shape of the calibration curve affects the shape of any summed distribution [@surovell_brantingham_2007], and the latest tail is partly a product of determinations near the post-1650 plateau. To test whether the seriation axis tracks calendar time, we pooled the dates of the four proveniences matching our assemblages (Kent Place, Parkin, Neeley's Ferry, and Clay Hill; 26 dates) and correlated their median calibrated ages with position on the first correspondence-analysis axis. With pooled calibrated medians the rank correlation is 0.40 in magnitude, with Clay Hill out of order, and it favors orienting the axis the way the analyses do; with one-sigma calibrated midpoints it is zero. Four points under two reasonable conventions that disagree cannot establish which end of the axis is later. House [-@house_1993, 23] reports the Kent and Clay Hill determinations with their contexts. At Kent, one date comes from a house floor (Feature 6, SMU-2102, 600 ± 115 BP) and the other from a post intrusive into it (SMU-2103, 310 ± 90 BP), and an archaeomagnetic date on another feature (Feature 1) falls at AD 1345 to 1380. Pooling the intrusive post with the floor makes the Kent Place median (AD 1466) later than the floor itself. Correcting it would not orient the axis either, because Kent Place, then among the earliest dated sites, and Clay Hill, the latest, sit at the same end of the axis. Hollywood, one of the analyzed assemblages but not in the radiocarbon file, has two calibrated dates reported by Haley [-@haley_2014] from Johnson and colleagues' excavations (Johnson et al. 2000:48-49, as cited there): central intercepts of AD 1310, 1355, and 1385 (one-sigma AD 1285 to 1420) for the earliest features, and AD 1405 (1300 to 1435) for the top of Mound A. The uncalibrated ages are not reported, so we have not recalibrated them against IntCal20 or pooled them with the other anchors. Hollywood too lies at the same end of the axis as Kent Place and Clay Hill (ranks 6, 4, and 7 of 28, with Parkin at 21). The axis follows latitude, with a rank correlation of +0.77 as oriented, and latitude and longitude together account for 62 percent of its variance (`analyses/87_axis_geography.py`). It is largely a south-to-north gradient in composition, which is what the main text's argument would lead one to expect, and it is not a reliable proxy for time. The analyses therefore orient the axis in the direction the medians weakly favor, and every direction-dependent result is reported both ways. The basin chronology is stable under an alternative compilation: adding the net-new Mississippian dates from the lower-valley radiocarbon database compiled by Jeffrey Alvey (personal communication, 2022), twelve determinations concentrated at Hazel and Upper Nodena, shifts the summed-probability median by eight years, to AD 1436, and leaves the contact decline in place (26 percent of the probability after AD 1541 and 16 percent after AD 1600). Recent AMS dating of the Parkin platform-mound post, interpreted as the base of the cross the De Soto expedition raised at Casqui, places it at cal AD 1445 to 1650 [@mitchem_2017], consistent with the latest occupation inferred here. The individual determinations, with laboratory numbers, ages, calibrated medians, and sources, are listed in `radiocarbon_dates_used.csv`.

## The southeast-Missouri assemblages, and why we do not analyze them

The main text tests one region. This section reports an attempt to extend the test to a second and explains why it does not appear there. The southeast-Missouri material is not adequate for the test at present, not because its phases are sounder or weaker than the basin's but because its classes and sample sizes measure something other than the stylistic copying the test is built for. We report the attempt because a negative of this kind is useful to the next person who considers the same extension.

### What we tried

Williams [-@williams_1954] divided the southeast-Missouri lowlands into four named Mississippian phases, which makes it the obvious second case. We digitized his survey tables and georeferenced the sites through the Lower Mississippi Survey grid, which places his assemblages and the basin's in one frame (Figure S10). Across the named physiographic regions the cultural $F_{ST}$ is 0.11, most of it a Woodland-to-Mississippian change in composition rather than contemporaneous social structure. Restricting the analysis to assemblages with at least 30 sherds in non-Woodland classes, and to those classes, leaves 39 assemblages over 42 classes and lowers the between-cluster $F_{ST}$ to 0.034 across four spatial clusters. Several assemblages from one location remain separate points, which already limits their independence.

Run through the same calibrated comparison as the basin, the set behaves much as the basin does. Its assemblages do not separate into clusters on the ordination (Figure S11A and B). Ten parameter combinations match its diversity under pooled-profile innovation on the screen and eight on the fifty-run check, and at the matched combination most favorable to the model (120 learners, innovation 0.004, mixing 0.02, great-circle distance) the observed $F_{ST}$ lies inside the simulated range of 0.004 to 0.040 (median 0.012), reached by 4.6 percent of simulated datasets in seriation order, 3.2 percent in reverse, and 5.4 percent as contemporaneous. Uniform innovation fits at no combination and overpredicts the differentiation about two-and-a-half-fold at its nearest one (median 0.083). The bounded-groups model at a copying factor of 0.03 raises the median to 0.025 and reaches the observation in 30 percent of 30 runs (Figure S11C). Taken at face value this repeats the basin's result, and it matches the verdict Fox [-@fox_1998] reached for these phases by cluster analysis and ordination.

### Why we do not report it as a result

Taking it at face value would be a mistake, for three reasons that appear only once the classes are examined rather than counted (`analyses/66_cmv_class_composition.py`).

**The set is ninety percent plain ware.** Mississippi Plain alone is 81.5 percent of the 8,958 analyzed sherds, Bell Plain a further 7.5 percent, and the four plain classes together 90.1 percent. The basin analysis uses ten decorated types and no plain ware at all. Plain wares are utility pottery, and the paste difference between Mississippi Plain and Bell Plain is a matter of manufacture and function rather than style. It is also where most of the differentiation lies: the plain component alone gives a between-cluster $F_{ST}$ of 0.065, and the Mississippi-to-Bell ratio alone 0.076, both well above the 0.034 of the full set. A difference between groups in that variable is what Table S1 files under environmental patchiness, which the test cannot separate from bounded interaction on this evidence.

**The stylistic part is too small to test.** Only 883 sherds, 9.9 percent of the set, are not plain, a median of 16 per assemblage spread across 38 classes. Even a 30-sherd minimum for decorated material leaves 12 of the 39 assemblages. The basin set carries 14,101 decorated sherds, a median of 251 per assemblage across ten classes.

**Most of the decorated material is surface film rather than design, and it is chronologically loaded.** Varney Red is 38.7 percent of the non-plain sherds and Old Town Red Filmed a further 17.4 percent. Varney Red is a chronologically diagnostic red-filmed ware that the Woodland exclusion does not catch, so its spatial pattern carries the very gradient that exclusion was meant to remove. Dropping it moves the $F_{ST}$ of the named non-plain types from 0.351 to 0.180. Twenty-nine of the 38 non-plain classes occur in only one of the four spatial clusters while holding 302 sherds among them, so rarity and spatial restriction cannot be separated at this sample size.

The non-plain subset does differentiate strongly (0.325), and not as an artifact of small samples: resampling every assemblage from the regional pooled profile at its own count gives a median of 0.003. Neither is it a clean measurement of style, for the reasons just given. The honest statement is not that southeast Missouri is differentiated or undifferentiated. It is that this set cannot answer the question the basin set is asked.

### How this shows up in the modeling

The composition problem is visible in the model's behavior, which is what first drew our attention to it. In the basin, the innovation boundary raises differentiation with the diversity match intact: from a reordered share of 0 to 0.3 the median $F_{ST}$ rises from 0.0022 to 0.0122, passing the observed 0.0062 between 0.2 and 0.25, and all three diversity summaries stay within tolerance until the share reaches 0.4 (1,200 runs per setting, `output/findings/other_departures_c_highrep.md`). On the southeast-Missouri set the same change reaches the observed 0.034 at a share of 0.2 (median 0.033), but the diversity match is already lost at 0.15 and at every share above it, so no setting both reaches the observation and holds the match; the largest share that keeps the match, 0.1, leaves the median at 0.017, half the observed value. The cause is the plain ware. With the top two classes holding 89 percent of all sherds, reordering a group's innovation profile so that it catches Mississippi Plain moves the whole assemblage rather than its margins. The quantity being varied is the composition of utility pottery.

### A defect in the source table

Three classes in the digitized Williams tables are each carried twice, because their names differ only in capitalization: "Thin Ware" and "Thin ware" (5 and 49 sherds), "Fine Ware" and "Fine ware" (4 and 30), and "Brown Slip" and "Brown slip" (2 and 2). Together they are 92 sherds, and nothing above turns on them, but they inflate the class count and should be merged at the source before anyone builds on this table.

### What would make the extension possible

The extension needs a decorated-only analysis of these assemblages, on named types with red films treated separately from incised and punctated design, and with sample sizes raised by returning to the collections rather than to the published tables. Until then the southeast-Missouri phases stand on Fox's ordination as independent evidence that they do not hold as groups, a different and weaker claim than the one the main text makes for the St. Francis basin.
