## Statistical conventions

The conventions of the main text apply. Posterior quantities are medians with 90 percent equal-tailed intervals unless stated otherwise. Where an analysis states an interval on a decorated composition or a rim attribute, the posterior draws use a Dirichlet(1/2) prior for the composition and a Beta(1/2, 1/2) prior for the attribute. Statistics compared against neutral simulations are computed directly from the counts. Every Stan fit reports its sampler diagnostics, and signed quantities are stated with their direction in words. The sampler diagnostics are defined as follows. R-hat compares chains started from different points and is near 1 when they agree. The bulk and tail effective sample sizes give the number of independent draws the correlated chains provide at the center and extremes of the posterior. A divergent transition is a step at which the sampler's numerical integration failed, and each one indicates a region of the posterior the sampler cannot sample reliably. Tree depth saturation is how often the sampler reached its configured step limit. The energy Bayesian fraction of missing information detects samplers that move too slowly between high- and low-density regions. A fit passes when R-hat is at most 1.01, both effective sample sizes are at least 400, no transition diverged, and the energy fraction is at least 0.3. Draws from a sampler that has not explored the posterior do not represent it, so a failing fit is reported as failing and its posterior is not used as an estimate. Three sections carried over from the earlier version of this study cover the minimum sample size, the recovery of the phases, and the second case. They report 95 percent intervals and 95 percent simulation ranges and state this where appropriate.

## The minimum sample size, and why it is 75

This section explains why assemblages need at least 75 decorated sherds to enter the analysis. Cultural *F*~ST~ computed directly from counts is biased upward by sampling. Two assemblages drawn from the same population of pots differ because each is a finite sample, and the spurious variance between them scales roughly as $1/n$. To find the count at which that artifact becomes small relative to the differences we interpret, we drew pairs of multinomial samples of size $n$ from the basin's pooled class profile, 4,000 pairs at each size, and computed *F*~ST~ between them. The two share one profile by construction, so all of the *F*~ST~ between them is produced by sampling (`analyses/82_minimum_sample_size.py`). The reference is the observed between-cluster *F*~ST~ at five clusters, 0.0307 (Table S1).

At 75 sherds, the median artifact is about a seventh of the observed five-cluster differentiation, and its 95th percentile (0.0195) is below it. At 50 sherds, the 95th percentile (0.0305) is nearly equal to it. The five-cluster value is the reference because it is the finest division analyzed cluster by cluster (in the earlier version of this study), where clusters hold the fewest sherds and a sampling artifact would matter most. Against the two-cluster value of 0.0062, the artifact is proportionally larger at every sample size. For that reason, the copying model is always sampled at the real sherd counts, so that it is subject to the same artifact as the data. Above 75, the bias shrinks slowly, by about three percentage points between 75 and 100, while assemblages are lost quickly. A minimum of 100 would remove three more basin assemblages (Davis, Irby, and Grant). To leading order, the expected plug-in *F*~ST~ for two equal samples from one profile is $1/(2n)$. For population diversity $H$, the expected excess of pooled over within-sample diversity is $H/(2n)$, and expected pooled diversity is $H(1-1/(2n))$. That is a ratio of expectations. Table S1 reports medians, which lie below it. Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003] kept sites with 800 or more sherds in all. Decorated sherds are 13 percent of his table, so his rule corresponds to about 100 decorated sherds. Because the copying model is sampled at the observed sherd counts, the model comparison is not distorted by this bias. The minimum matters most for the values of single small clusters, where a value computed on a few dozen sherds would be mostly artifact.

Assemblage coordinates come from `data/raw/mainfort-pfg-cplXY.txt`, with published corrections applied on reading (`data/raw/coordinate_corrections.csv`): Belle Meade and Beck are moved to author-supplied coordinates inside the sections Phillips, Ford, and Griffin record, and Starkley and Davis (13-N-5) to the survey's own coordinates, which fall inside their recorded sections (for Davis, SE1/4 SE1/4 of Section 14, T3N R5E, confirmed against the Bureau of Land Management's cadastral survey layer). Cummins (11-O-4) is moved 22.7 km, from a file coordinate in quadrangle 10-N that belongs to a different site joined on the name (Arkansas record 3PO243, a Dalton site), to the coordinate the From LMV Project sheet of `data/raw/mainfort-spatial-data.xlsx` gives for it, which lies inside quadrangle 11-O as its designation requires and 0.76 km from the site numbered 4 that Lipo [-@lipo_2001, Figure 2.6] plots there. This correction is provisional until the section Phillips, Ford, and Griffin give for 11-O-4 is supplied. The survey's coordinates were recorded in the 1927 North American Datum. Reading them in the 1983 datum instead places each point about 209 m south of where the survey placed it, so we convert them. We convert only coordinates that can be derived from the survey: an assemblage coordinate is converted when it lies within 10 m of the survey coordinate for that site read in the 1983 datum. That applies to 19 assemblages, 15 of them in the basin set, each moved 209 m. Eleven further coordinates lie within 100 m of that position but outside the tolerance (14 to 54 m for the eight in the basin set), which means they were derived another way. Eight of them are Mainfort's own points, and all eleven are left as recorded. Settlement-table rows whose source is the survey (367 rows) are converted the same way. The conversion leaves the $k$-means divisions at two, three, five, and six clusters and their direct *F*~ST~ unchanged, and changes the division at four clusters (the four-cluster value moves from 0.0194 to 0.0186). Every result reported uses the converted coordinates (`scripts/measure_datum.py`, `output/findings/datum_conversion.md`).

## Radiocarbon chronology

This section gives the radiocarbon evidence for when the basin was occupied and shows why the seriation order cannot be interpreted as a chronology. We calibrated the Mainfort [-@mainfort_2001] radiocarbon compilation against IntCal20 [@reimer_etal_2020]. Forty determinations come from seven sites in and near the St. Francis basin, nineteen of them from Parkin. Their calibrated medians run from AD 1018 to AD 1687, with the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of the 40 medians at AD 1187, 1424 and 1582 (`radiocarbon_dates_used.csv`). Thirteen of the 40 medians fall after the De Soto entrada of AD 1541. The determinations are listed with their laboratory numbers, ages, calibrated medians, and sources in that file. We do not infer a basin-wide occupation history from them. In our judgment, the shape of a summed distribution of 40 dates, most from one site, would reflect the calibration curve and the sampling of sites as much as occupation. No analysis in this paper depends on the shape of the occupation history.

To test whether the seriation axis tracks calendar time, we pooled the dates of the four proveniences matching our assemblages (Kent Place, Parkin, Neeley's Ferry, and Clay Hill; 26 dates), summing the calibrated densities within each site to obtain that site's median, and correlated those median calibrated ages with position on the first correspondence-analysis axis. With pooled calibrated medians, the rank correlation is 0.40 in magnitude, with Clay Hill out of order, and it favors orienting the axis the way the analyses do. With one-sigma calibrated midpoints, it is zero. Four points under two reasonable conventions that disagree cannot establish which end of the axis is later.

House [-@house_1993, 23] reports the Kent and Clay Hill determinations with their contexts. At Kent, one date comes from a house floor (Feature 6, SMU-2102, 600 ± 115 BP) and the other from a post intrusive into it (SMU-2103, 310 ± 90 BP), and an archaeomagnetic date on another feature (Feature 1) falls at AD 1345 to 1380. Pooling the intrusive post with the floor makes the Kent Place median (AD 1466) later than the floor itself. Correcting it would not orient the axis either, because Kent Place, then among the earliest dated sites, and Clay Hill, the latest, lie at the same end of the axis. Hollywood, one of the analyzed assemblages but not in the radiocarbon file, has two calibrated dates reported by Haley [-@haley_2014] from Johnson and colleagues' excavations (Johnson et al. 2000:48-49, as cited there): central intercepts of AD 1310, 1355, and 1385 (one-sigma AD 1285 to 1420) for the earliest features, and AD 1405 (1300 to 1435) for the top of Mound A. The uncalibrated ages are not reported, so we have not recalibrated them against IntCal20 or pooled them with the other dated assemblages. Hollywood too lies at the same end of the axis as Kent Place and Clay Hill (ranks 6, 4, and 7 of 28, with Parkin at 21).

The axis follows latitude, with a rank correlation of +0.77 as oriented, and latitude and longitude together account for 63 percent of its variance (`analyses/87_axis_geography.py`). It is largely a south-to-north gradient in composition, which is what the main text's argument would lead one to expect, and it is not a reliable proxy for time. The analyses therefore orient the axis in the direction the medians weakly favor, and report every direction-dependent result both ways.

Recent AMS dating of the Parkin platform-mound post, interpreted as the base of the cross the De Soto expedition raised at Casqui, places it at cal AD 1445 to 1650 [@mitchem_2017], within the span of the latest determinations in the compilation.

## What recovers the phases, and the problem of zeros

The main text asks whether the site map or the assemblages recover the phase assignments. This section describes how assemblages were clustered on location and composition together, and one choice, the treatment of zero counts, that changes the answer.

Assemblages are clustered in a combined space: site coordinates in kilometers, centered and scaled so that the mean squared pairwise distance is one, joined to decorated-class composition scaled the same way and multiplied by the square root of a weight $w$. At $w = 0$, the representation is the site map alone. As $w$ grows, it becomes assemblage composition alone. We compare each clustering with the phase assignments, taken from Phillips's [-@phillips_1970] phase areas as mapped by Lipo [-@lipo_2001, Figure 2.6], using the adjusted Rand index. We ran three composition transforms, three clustering methods ($k$-means over 40 seeds, each the best of 500 restarts; Ward linkage; and average linkage), and nine weights from the map alone upward, plus composition alone. The full grid is in `output/findings/phase_recovery.csv`. Two of the 28 assemblages, Cramor Place (Kent) and Holden Lake (Parkin), lie inside no phase area, each 1.0 km outside its own. They are assigned to the nearest area, which is a geographic rule. Every value quoted in the main text is therefore computed on the 26 assemblages inside a phase area. The exception is the sensitivity case below, which scores all 28. The agreement reached by the site map alone depends on the clustering method. It is 0.480 by $k$-means and 0.740 by Ward linkage and by average linkage, whichever composition transform is used, since at $w = 0$ the transform does not enter (`output/findings/phase_recovery.md`). The $k$-means value is no better than the 0.485 that divisions with the phases' group sizes around random centers reach. With the chi-square transform, $k$-means reaches 0.740 from a quarter of the coordinates' weight up to equal weight, with the same two assemblages misplaced, and adding composition does not raise the Ward value. The difference lies in which assemblages each method places with another phase. Ward and average linkage misplace two, Big Eddy and Castile Landing, both in Phillips's Parkin area and both grouped with Kent. $K$-means misplaces those two and four more, Beck, Belle Meade, Commerce, and Hollywood, which it groups with Walls. With composition at half the coordinates' weight and the chi-square transform, all three methods misplace only Big Eddy and Castile Landing. Composition alone misplaces six of the 26 by $k$-means and by Ward linkage and thirteen by average linkage. These lists are from the chi-square transform and, for $k$-means, the first seed.

We also asked of each assemblage which phase its pottery fits best (`analyses/96_assemblage_phase_fit.py`, `output/findings/assemblage_phase_fit.md`). The measure is the chi-square distance from the assemblage's profile to the pooled profile of each phase, with the assemblage left out of its own phase's pool. Each profile is drawn 4,000 times from Dirichlet(counts + 1/2), and the posterior probability that an assemblage fits its own phase is the share of draws in which its own phase is the closest. That probability is above 0.75 for 22 of the 28 assemblages and below 0.25 for four: Big Eddy, Holden Lake, and Turnbow, which are closer to Kent, and Belle Meade, which is closer to Parkin. For Castile Landing, it is 0.60, and for Mound Place, 0.40. Castile Landing is nearly as close to Kent (distance 0.571) as to its own Parkin (0.555), and Mound Place is slightly closer to Kent and to Parkin (0.496 each) than to its own Walls (0.538). Two assemblages lie nearer a member of another phase than any other member of their own: Castile Landing (12 km from the nearest Kent assemblage, 16 km from the nearest Parkin assemblage) and Clay Hill.

We also asked whether assemblages made by copying with no groups would sort into the phases, using the copying model as the earlier version of this study calibrated it (`analyses/79_phases_under_drift.py`, `output/findings/phases_under_drift.md`). We ran the copying model on the real site map 300 times, sampled its type frequencies at the real sherd counts, and clustered each simulated record the same way as the real one. Under Ward linkage, with a quarter weight on assemblage composition, the simulated records match the phases at a median of 0.740, the real record's value, and 68 percent reach it. At half weight, 36 percent do. At equal weight, the simulated median falls to 0.487, and 6 percent reach the real 0.740. The decline is not steady: at twice the coordinates' weight, the real record's own agreement falls to 0.454, and 46 percent of simulated records reach it. Based on type frequencies alone, the real record's agreement of 0.358 lies near the top of the simulated range (median 0.098, 95 percent range -0.045 to 0.416), and 6 percent of simulated records reach it. Part of the real record's advantage on composition is expected by construction, because Phillips drew the phases partly from these type frequencies, so the real pottery was sorted to fit them and simulated pottery was not. $K$-means gives the same result at equal weight and on composition alone (6 and 5 percent). At a quarter weight, it shows a wider difference (13 percent), because in the real record the pottery changes the division $k$-means makes of the site map, and in most simulated records it does not.

As a sensitivity case, we counted Big Eddy and Castile Landing as Kent and reran the recovery. Ward linkage on the site map alone then reproduces the scheme exactly, as does $k$-means on the map and composition together, and $k$-means on the map alone reaches 0.666 against 0.509 (all 28 assemblages scored). The primary analysis keeps Phillips's assignments.

Class proportions are compositional data, and the centered log-ratio transform is the usual choice. It cannot be applied here without a choice that determines the result. Of the 280 cells in the basin matrix, 105 are zero, and log (0) is undefined. Zeros must be replaced before the transform, here with a fraction of the smallest positive proportion. That fraction determines how much weight an absent class receives, and agreement with the phases changes with it (Table S2).

|---|---|

We report the chi-square transform in the main text because it needs no such choice, and we use a pseudocount wherever the log-ratio transform appears. The result also applies to the phases themselves. A classification that is sensitive to which types are absent, applied to assemblages whose sherd counts differ more than twentyfold, will treat the absence of a rare type from a small sample as a cultural difference.

## Settlement and waterways

The main text reports that settlement clumps follow the larger rivers (Figure 4B). This section asks whether nearness to water accounts for the clumping, and gives the measurements (`analyses/81_settlement_and_rivers.py`, `output/findings/settlement_and_rivers.md`). The sites are the 81 with a late pre-contact component in the mapped area, and the comparison is with 6,000 points placed uniformly at random in the same area. Clumping is measured by the Clark-Evans ratio, the mean distance to the nearest neighbor divided by its expectation for randomly placed points, which is 1 for a random pattern and less than 1 for a clumped one. The sites' ratio is 0.67.

The main text also reports that the phase boundaries run through sparsely settled ground (`analyses/80_boundaries_in_settlement_gaps.py`, `output/findings/boundaries_in_settlement_gaps.md`; main-text Figure 4B). The comparison uses the 53 late-period sites that lie more than a kilometer from any analyzed assemblage, and the reconstructed boundaries described in the main text. Measured at a 5 km scale, 94 percent of same-size divisions around random centers and 100 percent of compact ones run through denser late-period settlement than the phase boundaries do, and the result is similar at the other two scales tested: 98 and 100 percent at 3 km and 89 and 86 percent at 8 km.

Any waterway mattered on a floodplain that the Mississippi regularly flooded, so we counted streams of every size. The stream network is HydroRIVERS [@lehner_grill_2013], with reaches added from the largest flow order to the smallest. For each level, Table S3 gives the distance from sites and from random points to the nearest stream, and the Clark-Evans ratio of random points placed at the sites' own distances from the streams. If nearness to water produced the clumping, those points would be as clumped as the sites.

Two limits apply. The network is modern, and channels have moved since these sites were occupied. HydroRIVERS is also derived from an elevation grid of about 500 m, so on flat alluvial ground its small reaches are computed flow paths rather than surveyed channels, and it holds no oxbow lakes, bayous, or sloughs. Many sites are named for such lakes, suggesting that settlement followed abandoned channels as much as active ones, but we have no layer to test that.

## The methods and results of the main text in full

The main text describes each analysis by the question it asks, what it is compared against, and how its result is read. This section gives the full statement of those methods and the results that the main text reports in summary, in the order of the main text. The sections after it give the supporting checks.

**Radiocarbon medians.** Radiocarbon determinations compiled by Mainfort [-@mainfort_2001] match just four of the 28 assemblages. Their calibrated medians, each computed by pooling that site's calibrated densities, are AD 1466 at Kent Place, 1483 at Parkin, 1513 at Neeleys Ferry, and 1596 at Clay Hill. Hollywood has two calibrated one-sigma ranges reaching back to AD 1285 [@haley_2014; @house_1993, 23]. On that evidence, the late pre-contact occupation spans roughly the fourteenth through the early seventeenth centuries, and the sequence includes communities affected by European contact and the demographic collapse that followed [@morse_morse_1996; @lipo_2001]. The deposits overlap temporally, and each accumulated over time. Every analysis below therefore treats the record as a time-averaged spatial sample, and the neutral simulations it is compared against are built to the same condition.

**Why Bayesian methods.** The analyses that fit a model or state an interval use Bayesian methods [@gelman_etal_2013; @otarola_castillo_torquato_2018]. A Bayesian analysis begins with a prior distribution, which states the plausible values of each unknown quantity before the data are considered. It combines the prior with the data to produce a posterior distribution, which states how plausible each value is given both. Three properties of the record and of our questions make this approach suitable. First, the record is small and uneven. The 28 assemblages range from 79 to 1,709 decorated sherds, and some classes are rare. The proportions calculated from the counts are therefore uncertain, and more uncertain at some sites than at others [@robertson_1999]. A posterior carries that uncertainty into every quantity it produces, whereas a calculation from the raw proportions treats a class's share of 79 sherds as well known as its share of 1,709. Second, our questions concern magnitudes: how much of the variation between sites follows location, and how different two sites a given distance apart are expected to be. A posterior answers them with a range of supported values and does not reduce them to accepting or rejecting a null hypothesis [@wasserstein_lazar_2016]. Third, 28 sites may not be enough to resolve some of these quantities. When they are not, the posterior remains wide or close to its prior, which directly shows the limit of the record. Because results can depend on the priors, we test how much they influence the posterior of each of the three fitted models. We refit each model under alternative priors and test whether it recovers known values from simulated records [@gelman_etal_2020].

**The spatial field model.** Our first analysis describes how pottery composition varies with geography without first dividing the sites into groups. To do this, we fit a Bayesian spatial field model to the counts of the ten decorated classes in each of the 28 assemblages. In the model, each assemblage is a multinomial sample, the distribution that describes sherds drawn at random from a site where the ten classes occur in fixed but unknown proportions. We do not estimate those proportions independently for each site. Each is built from two parts. The first is a baseline for the class, which is the same at every site and reflects how common the class is across the basin. The second is a site-specific adjustment. Addition occurs on the log-odds scale, which is the logarithm of a class's proportion divided by the reference class's proportion, and the softmax function converts the ten log-odds into positive proportions that sum to one. One class serves as the reference, with its log-odds fixed at zero, and the model estimates the other nine classes relative to it.

Geography enters the model through site-specific adjustments. For each of the nine non-reference classes, the adjustments across the 28 sites are modeled as a Gaussian process: a smooth surface is placed over the map, with nearby sites taking similar values and distant sites taking nearly independent values. An exponential covariance sets how quickly that similarity declines. The correlation between two sites falls as exp(−*d*/*ρ*), where *d* is the straight-line distance between them, and *ρ* is the range, a distance in kilometers that governs how far the resemblance extends. A second parameter, the field scale *σ*, sets how large the geographic adjustments can be. Each site also receives an adjustment with no spatial pattern, independent from site to site. Its scale, *τ*, sets how much a site can differ from its neighbors for reasons unrelated to location. All nine class fields share one *σ*, one *ρ*, and one *τ*. Comparing *σ* with *τ* is the purpose of the model. A record organized by location places most of its between-site variation in the surface, whose scale is *σ*. A record in which each site has its own character regardless of its neighbors places it in the site-level term, whose scale is *τ*.

We gave *σ* and *τ* the same half-Normal(0, 2) prior, so that neither source of variation is favored before the data are seen. A half-Normal(0, 2) distribution is a Normal distribution with mean zero and standard deviation 2, restricted to positive values. It places half its probability below 1.35 and 95 percent below 3.9 on the log-odds scale. For the range *ρ*, we used a prior proportional to 1/*ρ* over 0.5 to 300 km. This prior gives equal probability to each multiplicative distance interval within that window, so it expresses no preference between a range of a few kilometers and one longer than the basin. On the class baselines, we placed a Normal(0, 10) prior, which on the log-odds scale is wide enough to let a rare class be very rare. A narrower prior on the baselines can shift part of a rare class's rarity into the field's basin-wide level. We therefore refit the model under two alternatives, a Normal(0, 2) prior and a Normal prior centered on each class's log-odds in the pooled counts of the whole basin, and report all three. We wrote the model in Stan and fitted it by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo [@carpenter_etal_2017]. The posterior of a model of this kind has no formula that can be evaluated directly. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo is a sampler, an algorithm that instead generates a long sequence of parameter values, called draws, that are distributed according to the posterior as the sequence lengthens. Each independent run of the sampler, starting from its own random values, is a chain. By default, we ran four chains of 3,000 iterations each. The first 1,500 iterations of each chain are warmup, during which the sampler adjusts its settings, and they are discarded. That leaves 6,000 draws.

We focus on two quantities from the model. The first is the contrast-based spatial share. A class's field has a basin-wide average level that the data cannot separate from the class baseline. We remove that level and take the variance of what remains, the part of the field that makes sites differ from one another. The share is that remaining variance as a fraction of itself plus the site-level variance *τ*^2^, on the model's latent log-ratio scale. A share near one means nearly all the between-site variance the model finds on that scale lies in the smooth surface, and a share near zero means sites differ in ways location does not describe. The share partitions expected latent variance in log-ratios to the reference class, not variance explained in the observed compositions. The second quantity is the standard deviation of the contrast between two sites a stated distance apart, on the log-odds scale. It states how different, under the field alone, two sites that far apart are expected to be in a class's log-odds. We evaluate it at the median spacing between nearest neighbors, the median distance between site pairs, and the largest distance between any two sites in the sample.

*Sampler diagnostics of the three fits.* The spatial field model was fitted under the three priors on the class baselines. All three fits pass every diagnostic of the Statistical conventions section (largest R-hat 1.006, smallest bulk effective sample size 608, no divergent transitions, no iterations at maximum treedepth, smallest E-BFMI 0.67).

*Scale and range.* Two parameters describe the smooth surface. Its scale, *σ*, sets how large the differences across the map can be, and its range, *ρ*, is the distance over which sites resemble one another. The 28 sites cannot determine these two values separately, because different pairs of values fit the record about equally well. All 28 sites lie within 79 km of one another. At those distances, a field with a large scale and a long range produces nearly the same contrasts between sites as a field with a smaller scale and a shorter range. These data constrain how different two sites are expected to be, not *σ* and *ρ* separately, which is why we report the share and the contrast. The share also does not by itself distinguish a smooth surface from social units aligned with geography. If units existed and their members lived near one another, each unit's shared composition would appear at 28 sampled sites as one region of a smooth surface, and the surface would account for it. A high share explains why phases appear to be groups. It leaves open whether anything beyond geography structures the record, which the models for bounded units address.

**The neutral copying model.** To state what distance-dependent copying predicts, we use the finite-population network model of Lipo, DiNapoli, Madsen, and Hunt [-@lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021] on the real site map. Each of the 28 sites has a finite population of learners who copy decorated classes mostly within their site and, otherwise, from other sites with a probability that falls with straight-line distance. New variants appear at a constant rate in proportion to each class's share of the regional pool. Each simulated assemblage accumulates over a span with a random start and a random length, independent of location and phase. Span lengths are sized by the simulator's measured decorrelation length, and each simulated assemblage is subsampled to the real assemblage's sherd count. The model places no restriction on copying between any two sites.

The model has four parameters. The number of learners at each site sets how fast chance alone changes class frequencies, since a small population drifts faster than a large one. Innovation is the probability that a learner's class in a given step is drawn from the regional pool rather than copied from another learner. Mixing is the probability that a copy comes from another site rather than from the learner's own. Interaction length is the distance over which the probability of copying from a particular other site declines. The kernel is the form of that decline with distance.

We do not estimate these parameters from the record, and we do not tune the simulated assemblages to the between-site statistics the comparisons test, except as stated below. A model tuned to those statistics would reproduce the record by construction. Instead, we screen a grid of combinations of kernel shape, interaction length, population size, innovation rate, and mixing rate, and keep those whose simulated assemblages match three diversity summaries, each within 10 percent of the record's value. Two are local: the mean Gini-Simpson diversity, the probability that two sherds drawn from the same assemblage belong to different classes, and the mean number of classes present in an assemblage. The third is regional and consists of the Gini-Simpson diversity of the 28 pooled assemblages. Pooled diversity compared with local diversity carries information about dispersion between sites, so these summaries are not free of between-site information, although none of them is a statistic the later comparisons test.

An operating point is one combination of parameter values at which the model is run for the comparisons. From the matched combinations, we retain two operating points in two stages. The screen, at six seeds per combination, supplies the candidates. We retain matched combinations whose median two-cluster cultural *F*~ST~ (defined below) lies within 50 percent of the observed value. We then rerun each candidate with 50 fresh seeds and score it by the sum of squared relative errors over the three diversity summaries and the *F*~ST~ median. The primary point has the smallest such loss among candidates that still match at 50 seeds. The secondary has the smallest loss among those with a different kernel family and a different population size. The between-cluster *F*~ST~ therefore entered the choice of operating point, as a filter and as a term in the loss. The comparison of the record's two-cluster *F*~ST~ against the operating points in the Results is interpreted with that in mind. The same comparison across every diversity-matched combination of the screen, without that selection, is given below. The per-class, drift, marker, and rim statistics were not selection criteria, and their results remain conditional on the selected operating points.

Some predictions depend on a parameter the diversity match leaves open, for example, the window of time over which the assemblages' accumulation spans are spread or the amplitude of regional drift, both described in the next section. Such a parameter is not set to a single value. It is swept over a grid, and the record's position relative to the model is reported at every point on that grid, so readers can see whether a conclusion holds across the range or only at one setting.

For each statistic, we generate 50 simulated records at an operating point and compute the statistic on each. The 50 values show the range the statistic takes under neutral copying alone at that setting. We summarize them by their median and by the neutral band, the interval from their 10th to their 90th percentile, which contains the middle 80 percent of the simulated values. An observed value inside the band is one that neutral copying commonly produces at that operating point. About one simulated record in ten exceeds the band's upper limit, and about one in ten falls below its lower limit. Some statistics are therefore expected to fall outside the band under neutral copying alone. The marker analysis described below uses the neutral records' own count outside their band, near 10 of 50, as the baseline against which a detectable departure is measured. We classify the record as inside the band, outside it, or unavailable. It is unavailable when too few of the simulated records yield a finite value of the statistic for a band to be formed. Only an observed value above the band is interpreted as more variation than neutral copying produces. Restricted copying, if it occurred, would increase differences between sites, and a record that varies less than the model does not indicate it. Every such interpretation is conditional on the generator and its operating point. A different generator or setting could predict differently, which is why the swept parameters are reported at every point.

*Pooled composition of the phase areas.* Phillips's areas also differ in pooled composition. Between the Parkin and Kent areas, the log-odds of Parkin Punctated in the pooled counts are 0.47 higher in Parkin. The pooled log-odds of Barton, Kent, and Mound Place Incised, and of the painted types, are 0.12 and 0.90 lower in Parkin.

*The two operating points.* The archaeological record of the St. Francis basin does not allow us to determine the neutral model's four parameters. Many combinations of population size, innovation rate, mixing rate, and interaction length produce simulated records with the same diversity as the 28 time-averaged assemblages. Of the combinations we screened, 498 match all three diversity summaries, and the record gives no basis for choosing among them. We therefore carry two of them, the operating points, through every comparison. Each has a loss, the measure of mismatch with the record defined above, for which a smaller value is a closer match. The primary operating point has a loss of 0.108. Its kernel is Gaussian, a curve along which the probability of copying from another site declines slowly at short distances and steeply beyond them, with an interaction length of 6 km. Each site has 2,000 learners; the innovation rate is 0.002, and the mixing rate is 0.4. The secondary point has a loss of 0.148. Its kernel is exponential, declining most steeply at short distances and more gradually beyond them, with an interaction length of 6 km. It has 120 learners per site, an innovation rate of 0.024, and the same mixing rate of 0.4. The two points thus differ in kernel shape, in population size by a factor of about 17, and in innovation rate by a factor of 12, while matching the same diversity summaries. A result that holds at both does not depend on those three choices. It can still depend on the interaction length and the mixing rate, which are the same at both points.

**Statistics compared with the neutral model.** Cultural *F*~ST~ [@wright_1951; @bell_richerson_mcelreath_2009] measures the share of decorated diversity, by the Gini-Simpson index, that lies between groups rather than within them, in Nei's [-@nei_1973] form. We report it between the two spatial clusters the site layout supports, with a neutral band in each of 30 cells. The cells cross the two operating points with three sweeps. The first is occupation spread, where we vary the window over which span endpoints are drawn while keeping individual span lengths unchanged. The second is occupation spread with collection sizes assigned in rank order of span length. The third is regional drift. For each decorated class, we compute a site-weighted moment estimate of *F*: the between-site variance of the class's share, with binomial sampling variance removed, divided by its mean share times one minus that share. For the three common classes (Parkin Punctated, the pooled Barton, Kent, and Mound Place Incised, and the pooled painted types), the statistic is *F*. For the seven rare classes, it is the number of the 28 sites where the class is present, because *F* is poorly determined for a class present at few sites. In the model, neutral copying implies one population-level *F* shared by every class. Estimated *F* in finite samples still differs among classes, so we compare each class with its own simulated band.

Change over time is a second source of differences between sites that does not involve boundaries. Assemblages that accumulated at different times sampled a regional pool of designs that changed over the interval. Regional drift, the rise and fall of class frequencies across the whole region through copying and chance, is the documented form of that change in these data. As described so far, the simulator holds the regional pool from which innovations are drawn fixed. To let that pool drift, after each copying step we redraw it from a Dirichlet distribution centered on its current composition. A Dirichlet distribution is a probability distribution over sets of proportions that sum to one, so each draw is a completely new set of class shares that differs from the current set by a random amount. Each class's expected share is unchanged by the redraw, and the size of the random step matches the mean and variance of one generation of a Wright-Fisher population, the standard model of change by chance alone. The occupation window is the stretch of simulated time from which the assemblage spans are drawn. We set that window to *W* = 8*L*~R~, eight times the number of simulation steps over which the regional composition decorrelates. We quantify drift by its accumulated effect. The amplitude *F*~W~ is the regional *F* accumulated over the window. It measures how far the pool at the end of the window has drifted from the pool at its start, on the same scale as the between-site *F* reported for the classes. It is 0 when the pool is fixed, and we run it at 0, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, and 0.3, the last larger than any between-site *F* the record shows.

To measure what size of departure the comparison can detect, we add a marker to simulated records: a known change in one class in one of the two spatial clusters the site layout supports. The marker multiplies the class odds by 1.5, 2, or 3 at every site in the cluster before that site's sherds are drawn. A class with a share of 20 percent at a site thus becomes 27 percent at a multiplier of 1.5, 33 percent at 2, and 43 percent at 3. Everything else in a marker record matches the neutral record it was made from. For each marker and each drift amplitude, we count how many of the 50 marker records have the class's statistic outside the neutral band for that class and amplitude. We report that count beside the number of neutral records that fall outside their own band, which is near 10 of 50 by construction because the band spans the 10th to the 90th percentile. A marker the comparison can detect raises the count well above that baseline, and one it cannot detect leaves the count near it.

Two further sets of simulations extend the per-class comparison to conditions it does not include. We designed both after seeing its results, fixed the rules for reading each before running it, and ran each once. The first set varies drift and timing. It adds amplitudes of 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 to the five above. It varies how long each assemblage accumulated: span lengths are drawn up to the limit used above, up to a quarter of that limit, or set to a single step, which removes time-averaging. It varies the occupation window among 2, 8, and 32 times *L*~R~. It also varies how occupation dates relate to location, in seven arrangements. In one, dates are independent of location, as in the comparison above. In four, dates are ordered by latitude, with the later dates either in the north or in the south. The ordering is either strict or partial, and in the partial case a site's place in the sequence depends equally on its latitude rank and on a random draw. In the remaining two, the two spatial clusters occupy separate halves of the window, with either cluster the later one. We ordered dates by latitude because the common classes' shares change with latitude in the record (main text, Results). Crossing three span limits, three windows, seven arrangements, and eight amplitudes gives 504 combinations at each operating point, each with 50 simulated records.

For these simulations, we add two spatial-pattern summaries, computed the same way on the record and on every simulated record. The first is, for each class, the rank correlation between the class's share and latitude across the sites, which is positive when the share is higher to the north. We compare it with its band on its absolute value, because the direction in which a class changes under drift differs randomly from one simulated record to the next. The second is the rank correlation between the compositional difference between two sites and the distance between them, over all 378 site pairs. The compositional difference is half the sum of the absolute differences in class shares, and the correlation is positive when sites farther apart differ more.

The second set allows new types to appear during the occupation. The regional pool becomes a finite population of *N*~R~ copies. At each step, every copy is replaced, either by a copy of a randomly chosen member of the current pool or, with a small probability *μ*, by a type that has not existed before. This is the Wright-Fisher model with new variants entering at a constant rate. A type whose count falls to zero is lost and does not return. We set *N*~R~ so that the drift accumulated over the window equals the stated amplitude *F*~W~. Without new types, the model is therefore the counterpart of the drifting pool described above, and we compare the two directly at 20 shared settings (Table S8). We express the rate of new types as *θ* = 2*N*~R~*μ*, the parameter of the Ewens sampling formula [@ewens_1972], and run it at 0, 0.3, 1, 3, and 10. For reference, ten classes among the record's 14,101 decorated sherds correspond to a *θ* of 0.98 under that formula. We use that value only to place the grid, because the ten classes are analyst-defined units, some of which combine several types, and are therefore not a count of distinct variants. The pooled sample is also not drawn from one well-mixed population. New types arise only in the regional pool and reach sites through the innovation term. The pool is held fixed during burn-in, so new types appear only within the simulated occupation.

This set crosses the five rates with amplitudes of 0.1 to 0.9, windows of 8 and 32 times *L*~R~, the original span limit and the single step, and three date arrangements (independent of location, and strictly ordered by latitude in each direction). That gives 300 combinations at each operating point. We chose this narrower grid after seeing the first set. A simulated new type has no counterpart among the record's named classes, so here classes are compared by rank of abundance in the pooled record. The statistics are *F* for the three commonest classes, the number of sites at which each of the fourth to tenth commonest is present, the number of types, the share of sherds in types beyond the tenth (zero in the record), and the two spatial summaries. In both sets, combinations that differ only in span limit or date arrangement are readings of the same simulated histories, and every combination rests on one set of random draws. We therefore report the counts as descriptions and attach no probability to them. The settings are given in full below.

**Composition by collection.** Nine of the 28 rows combine the 1940 survey collection with Lipo's 1996 to 1997 collections at the same site. To ask whether merging collections produces the per-class pattern, we decompose each row into its parts and recompute the per-class statistics on the 1940 material alone, matched on the 27 sites whose 1940 part has at least 75 sherds. At each mixed site, we report the log odds ratio of each class between the two collections. Its 90 percent interval is computed under independent multinomial sampling of each part and a Dirichlet(1/2) prior, and the medians under Dirichlet(0.1) and Dirichlet(1) are reported as prior sensitivities.

The departure is not an artifact of merged collections. On the 1940 survey material alone, matched on the 27 sites with at least 75 sherds in the 1940 part, the common classes' *F* values are 0.151, 0.087, and 0.195. For the current counts on the same 27 sites, they are 0.145, 0.076, and 0.195 (main-text Table 1). Collection source matters at the level of a site's two collections and for the presence of rare classes. At the six sites with large additions, the log odds ratio of Parkin Punctated between Lipo's collection and the 1940 collection ranges from -1.03 at Cramor Place to +0.39 at Beck. The 90 percent interval excludes zero at four of the six sites for that class, at five of six for the pooled incised classes, and at one of six for the painted types. Wallace Incised is absent from every 1940 collection among the 28 and occurs only in Lipo's additions and at Holden Lake, so its presence pattern partly reflects which sites were revisited. A positive correlation between the pooled incised classes' share and collection size (Spearman +0.26 over the 28 rows) falls to zero in the 1940 material. Two correlations persist in the 1940 material. The share of Parkin Punctated rises with collection size (+0.42 over the 28 rows, +0.49 on the 1940 parts of the 27 matched sites) and the share of the painted types falls (-0.57 and -0.51). No neutral generator we ran reproduces them. Differences in site function may explain this, but these data cannot test that possibility. Further investigation would require examining variability in overall artifact classes and settlement structure. 

*The painted class by type.* The painted class pools Old Town Red, Nodena Red and White, and Avenue Polychrome. Only the 1940 collections can be separated by type, because Lipo's [-@lipo_2001] compilation records his additions as the pooled class (`analyses/per_type/painted_by_type.py`, `output/findings/painted_by_type.md`). Twenty-seven of the 28 assemblages have a 1940 part, and those parts hold 834 painted sherds: 751 Old Town Red (90.0 percent), 62 Avenue Polychrome (7.4 percent), and 21 Nodena Red and White (2.5 percent). Old Town Red occurs in all 27, Avenue Polychrome in 14, and Nodena Red and White in one, Hollywood. The 390 painted sherds in Lipo's additions are not separated. Old Town Red's share of the 1940 decorated sherds is lower to the north (rank correlation with latitude -0.71), and so is Avenue Polychrome's (-0.37). These are descriptive counts and correlations, and no interval is attached to them.

**Rim attributes.** Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003_rims, Table 1] published counts of six rim attributes on 7,195 rims from 55 late-period assemblages, tabulated separately from the decorated types and by one observer, including the interior beveling and exterior notching used here. Lip form is not in that paper. It comes from unpublished data that Mainfort shared with us, which gives it as percentages that we converted to counts using the published rim totals. Nineteen of our 28 assemblages meet this threshold, with at least 25 rims, for a total of 2,065 rims. One of the 19 falls below Mainfort's own minimum of 45. We use three attributes that we interpret as not tabulated as types in 1951: interior beveling, exterior notching, and lip form. Mainfort's unpublished data also record paste. Of the 2,065 rims, 641 are Bell and 874 Neeley's Ferry, and the remaining 550 (0 to 81 percent by site) are in neither named paste. We do not analyze paste, for the same reason that plain sherds are excluded: paste and temper can reflect firing conditions and vessel function (main text, Data). Bell Plain and Neeley's Ferry Plain are also type-level distinctions of the 1951 typology, so paste is not independent of how the phases were drawn. The rim simulations and the rim findings file (`output/findings/rim_r1.md`) cover the three attributes only.

For each attribute, we compute the same moment *F* on the 19 sites against the same neutral model, with all 28 populations transmitting and the 19 rim sites observed, plus the three common decorated classes on the same 19 sites. We report each attribute's rank correlation with latitude and longitude and its pairwise differences with distance as posterior summaries. Each site's frequency of an attribute is given a Beta(1/2, 1/2) prior. A Beta distribution describes a single proportion between zero and one, and this one has the same effect as adding half a rim with the attribute and half a rim without it to each site's count. It keeps a site where the attribute was never observed, or always observed, from being treated as exactly 0 or 100 percent. The summaries are computed over 4,000 draws from the resulting posteriors. We repeat them under a Beta(1, 1) prior, which adds one rim of each kind, as a sensitivity check. We also report the rank correlation between rim and decoration differences across the 171 site pairs after linear adjustment for distance. Phillips [-@phillips_1970] did not define the phases on decorated types alone, so we describe the rims as a second trait system recorded by a second observer and do not call them independent of the phase definitions.

**Models for bounded units.** The spatial field model describes composition as a smooth surface. Two further models ask whether the record is better described by that surface plus discrete units, each unit a set of sites that share a shift in composition beyond what their locations predict. Both keep everything in the spatial field model and add one feature, so that the field model is recovered exactly when the added feature is set to zero (main-text Figure 3).

The latent-unit model (main-text Figure 3B) assigns each site to one of three units, the number of phases in the basin, without being told which site belongs to which. Each unit shifts the log-odds of every class at its member sites. The three units' shifts sum to zero within each class, so the units move composition relative to one another without changing the basin-wide baseline. A single scale, *σ*~g~, governs the size of those shifts and has a half-Normal(0, 2) prior. Membership is unknown. A Dirichlet(1) prior on the frequencies of the three units is symmetric in their labels and uniform over those frequencies, which is not the same as uniform over divisions of the sites. The model sums over every possible assignment of sites to units rather than committing to one, so the posterior for *σ*~g~ reflects every way of dividing the sites into three units, weighted by that prior and the data.

The barrier model (main-text Figure 3C) fixes the division in advance by drawing an edge through the site map, and asks whether sites on the same side of the edge share a shift in composition beyond what the smooth surface predicts. Its barrier parameter *ω* is the standard deviation, in log-odds, of that shared shift, with a half-Normal(0, 2) prior, and *ω* = 0 is the spatial field model exactly. We placed the edge in two ways. The first is the two-cluster division of the sites by k-means on their coordinates, the same two spatial clusters used for the cultural *F*~ST~, which separates eleven of the twelve Parkin-area sites from the other seventeen. The second is a straight line across the basin, chosen from 30 candidate lines as the one that best agrees with Phillips's Parkin-versus-rest labels, so this placement uses the phase labels. The same line also best matches the k-means division.

Each model was tested on simulated records with known parameter values before it could be fitted to the record. We simulated records from each model's own generator at the real 28 site locations and fitted the model to each simulated record. We set the true value of *σ*~g~ to 0.25, 0.5, 1, and 2 for the latent-unit model and *ω* to 0, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 for the barrier model. For the barrier model, we repeated the fits with the field's range left free and with it pinned at 6 and 12 km, under both class-intercept priors. For the latent-unit model, the fourteen recovery fits were run under the Normal(0, 2) prior, and three of them were repeated under Normal(0, 10). A model that recovers the true value returns a posterior centered near it that moves as the true value moves. One whose posterior stays near the prior's median of 1.35, whatever the true value, has learned little about that parameter from the data. We report posterior medians against true values, sampler diagnostics for every fit, and the share of fits that pass those diagnostics. A model that does not recover a known value on records from its own generator cannot be interpreted on the record, where the true value is unknown, so we report no estimate from it.

**Reporting conventions.** Three kinds of numbers appear in the Results. Posterior quantities state what the record implies about a quantity once the uncertainty in its counts is carried through. Each is reported as its median with a 90 percent equal-tailed interval, the range from the 5th to the 95th percentile of the posterior draws, unless stated otherwise. Neutral comparisons place an observed statistic against the distribution of the same statistic over records simulated under neutral copying. We compute the cultural *F*~ST~ and the per-class *F* directly from the counts, in the same way for the record and for each simulated record. We report them as point values beside the neutral band defined above, not as a posterior interval. Descriptive statistics summarize the record or a set of divisions and carry no inferential claim.

Posterior intervals on proportions apply only where an analysis states one. For a decorated composition, each assemblage's proportions have a Dirichlet posterior, combining a multinomial likelihood for its counts with a Dirichlet(1/2) prior that adds half a sherd to every class. For a rim attribute, each site's frequency has a Beta posterior under a Beta(1/2, 1/2) prior. The statistic is recomputed on repeated draws from those posteriors, and its reported median and interval summarize that set of values.

We check every model fitted in Stan for whether the sampler explored the posterior before any of its output is used. The checks use five diagnostics [@vehtari_etal_2021]. R-hat compares variation between chains with variation within them. It equals 1 when the chains have converged to the same distribution, and it rises above 1 when they have not, in which case their draws cannot be combined as a single posterior sample. The effective sample size is the number of independent draws that would estimate a quantity as precisely as the chains do. It is smaller than the number of draws because successive draws in a chain resemble one another. The bulk effective sample size applies to the center of a posterior, and so to a median. The tail effective sample size applies to its extremes, and so to the limits of an interval. A divergent transition is an iteration at which the sampler's numerical approximation of its path failed. Divergent transitions indicate a region of the posterior that the sampler could not explore accurately, so the draws may underrepresent that region. The energy Bayesian fraction of missing information measures how freely the sampler moves between the dense center of the posterior and its sparse outer regions, and a low value means the outer regions were explored slowly. Tree depth is the length of the path the sampler follows in one iteration, which has a set maximum. Saturation is the share of iterations that reached that maximum, and it indicates inefficient sampling, not incorrect draws.

A fit passes when, for every parameter checked, R-hat is at most 1.01 and both the bulk and tail effective sample sizes are at least 400, no transition diverged, and the energy Bayesian fraction of missing information is at least 0.3. We report tree-depth saturation but do not fail a fit on it. A fit that fails at the default settings is rerun with longer chains and stricter sampler settings before it is judged. In the latent-unit model, the labels of the units are arbitrary and can differ from one chain to another, so parameters tied to a label are not checked. We require instead that the co-membership probabilities, the posterior probabilities that two sites belong to the same unit, agree across chains. A fit that fails is reported as failing, and its posterior is not used as an estimate. In the recovery tests, failed fits are shown with their draws marked as failed so that the denominator is visible. The sections below include prior and posterior predictive checks, prior sensitivity, and recovery of known values under each prior.

**The two-cluster comparison.** We compared the observed value with the neutral band in 30 cells. The neutral band is the interval containing the middle 80 percent of values simulated under neutral copying at one setting. A cell is one operating point at one setting of occupation spread or regional drift. The observed value falls within the band in 17 of the 30 cells and in 7 of the 11 cells at the primary point without regional drift. It lies above the band mainly at the widest occupation spreads and the smaller drift amplitudes (Table S6 and the cell-by-cell account below). At these two operating points, neutral copying therefore reaches the observed difference under narrower occupation spreads and larger drift amplitudes. These counts overstate how readily neutral copying produces the observed difference. We chose the two operating points in part because their *F*~ST~ is close to the observed value, and that choice favors combinations with unusually high *F*~ST~. Ranked by six-seed median *F*~ST~, the two points are 25th and 11th among the 498 diversity-matched combinations.

The 498 combinations, taken without that selection, give a different result. We ran each with six seeds. The observed value lies above the combination's six-seed 90th percentile in 436 of them (88 percent) and above all six simulated values in 400. It lies at or below the combination's median in only 8. All 8 have a Gaussian kernel, five with an interaction length of 6 km and three with 12 km. At the scale of the two clusters, the record is therefore consistent with neutral copying, in the sense that some neutral settings reproduce the observed difference. Most neutral settings produce a smaller difference than the record shows. Because both statements hold, this comparison neither shows nor excludes a boundary.

**The per-class comparison.** The three common decorated classes vary between sites far more than neutral copying alone predicts (main-text Table 1). Their site-weighted moment *F* values are 0.165 for Parkin Punctated, 0.117 for the pooled Barton, Kent, and Mound Place Incised, and 0.199 for the painted types, against neutral medians of 0.007 to 0.018 at every occupation-spread setting and both operating points. The seven rare classes, by contrast, are present at about as many sites as neutral copying predicts. At the primary operating point without drift, five of the seven lie inside the neutral band, Fortune Noded lies below it and Hull Engraved above it (main-text Table 1), and the exceptions change with the cell. The rare classes' site counts are a weak discriminator, and Table S6 reports them cell by cell. With a fixed set of classes, no tested setting of the simulator's unidentified parameters covers both the common classes' *F* and the presence of rare classes. The set of simulations in which new types enter the regional pool is the exception, and it is described below. The simulated common classes already estimate a higher *F* than the simulated rare classes, so the ordering of common above rare is not itself a departure. The departure is how far the common classes' *F* is above their simulated bands.

Regional drift over time does not remove the difference. Letting the regional innovation target drift over the occupation window raises the common classes' neutral *F*. At an amplitude of 0.3, the largest shown in main-text Figure 6, the medians reach only 0.029 to 0.035 at the primary operating point, with 10th to 90th percentile bands reaching 0.052 to 0.111, and 0.033 to 0.041 at the secondary. The record's 0.117 to 0.199 lies above every band (main-text Figure 6). Drift also removes the rare classes from the regional target. At an amplitude of 0.01, the loss already ranges from 1 to 44 of 50 records across the seven classes at the primary point, and at 0.1, Ranch Incised is lost in 39 of 50. All seven rare classes are covered by their bands together only at amplitudes of 0.01 and 0.03 at the primary point and at no tested amplitude at the secondary. No amplitude up to 0.3 places the record inside the bands for the common classes, and amplitudes that approach them remove the rare classes present in the record.

Three ways remain by which neutral copying could still produce variation in the common classes. Regional drift could have been larger than the amplitudes tested above. The sites could have been occupied at different times, in an order related to their location. New types could have entered the region during the occupation, which the model described so far does not allow. We examined all three in two further sets of simulations. We designed both after seeing the results above and ran each once. Each combination reported below is one simulator setting, run 50 times. We say that a combination covers a feature of the record when the record's value lies inside that combination's neutral band. For a rare class, the feature is the number of sites at which the class occurs.

The first set varied drift and timing. It crossed amplitudes up to 0.9 with three limits on how long an assemblage accumulated, three occupation-window lengths, and seven arrangements of occupation date by location, giving 504 combinations per operating point. Large drift does reach the common classes. At amplitudes of 0.7 and 0.9, all three common-class *F* values are covered in 5 of 504 combinations at the primary point, and in 45 of the 496 combinations at the secondary point for which bands could be computed. Those same combinations do not reproduce the rare classes. At most one of the seven rare classes is covered in any of them, because the simulated records hold the rare classes at fewer sites than the record does. The reverse also holds. In every combination that covers at least five of the seven rare classes, none of the three common-class *F* values is covered. Enlarging the drift therefore exchanges one mismatch with the record for another, with no change in accumulation limit or window length.

Timing matters for one feature: the latitude trend of the painted types. When occupation dates are independent of location, no combination covers that trend (0 of 72 at either point). When dates are ordered strictly by latitude, 91 of 144 combinations cover it at the primary point and 82 of 144 at the secondary. A partial ordering gives 13 and 1 of 144. Placing the two spatial clusters in separate halves of the window gives 16 and 5 of 144. Only four of the 28 assemblages are dated, so the record cannot show whether latitude ordered occupation.

The second set allowed new types. It replaced the regional pool with a finite population in which a copy is occasionally a new type, at five rates of origination from none to high, crossed with amplitudes of 0.1 to 0.9 (300 combinations per operating point). A simulated new type has no counterpart among the record's named classes, so we compared classes by abundance rank: the three most common, then the fourth through the tenth. Without new types, this model behaves as the first set did. The common-class *F* values are covered in 10 of 60 combinations at the primary point, and 11 of 60 at the secondary, and no rare class is covered in any of them. In a direct comparison at 20 shared settings, the two models also gave the record the same reading (inside, above, or below the band) in 307 of 320 cases (Table S8). With new types, the common classes and at least five of the seven rare classes are covered together in 36 of 300 combinations at the primary point and 35 of 300 at the secondary, all at amplitudes of 0.5 or more (Table S7).

Those combinations differ from the record in two other respects. First, they contain many more types. Their median number of types ranges from 18.5 to 333.5 at the primary point and from 26 to 302 at the secondary, compared with ten classes in the record. In every combination whose band for the number of types contains ten, at most four of the seven rare classes are covered. This comparison is weak evidence, because the record's classes are analyst-defined and some combine several types. Second, most of their common types are new. Six to eight of the ten commonest simulated types, including one or two of the three commonest, first appeared during the occupation (medians per combination). A dated sequence of type appearances could show whether the record's common types appeared that late, and the present record cannot.

No tested combination reproduces all of these features together: the variation of the common classes, the site counts of at least five rare classes, the number of types, and the spatial pattern (the increase of compositional difference with distance, and the latitude trends of the commonest and third-commonest classes). The combinations that cover both the common and the rare classes share three properties: a drift amplitude of 0.5 or more, first appearance of most of the commonest types within the occupation, and more types than the record holds. These are properties of the combinations we tested, not conditions shown to be necessary. Each count states only that one record summary lies within its own band. The combinations share simulated histories and so are not independent. They come from one set of simulations, and the grid does not exhaust the neutral settings. The record measures neither the amplitude of drift nor the dates at which types first appeared, so it cannot show whether those properties held.

For the question about phases, these simulations settle less than their number of combinations suggests. They ask whether copying with no boundary of any kind can produce the variation of the three common classes. At drift amplitudes up to 0.3 it does not, and at larger amplitudes it does so only in combinations that fail to reproduce other features of the record. The extra variation of the common classes therefore remains unexplained by the neutral settings we tested. It is not evidence for a boundary between phases. A departure from neutral copying shows that something other than distance-dependent copying acted on those classes, and it does not show what. The Discussion of the main text sets out the candidate causes.

The comparison can detect a moderate departure. Doubling the odds of one common class in one of the two spatial clusters placed that class outside the neutral band in 23 to 45 of 50 marker records at amplitudes up to 0.1. Tripling it did so in 37 to 50 of 50. The neutral cells' own count outside their band is 10 of 50. A 1.5-fold change placed 7 to 26 of 50 outside at amplitudes up to 0.1 and 5 to 21 at 0.3. It moved the median of the marker records outside the band only for the pooled incised classes in the larger cluster at the two smallest amplitudes. Walls Engraved, the one rare class given a marker, never moved its occupancy median outside the band at any multiplier (main-text Figure 6). These counts are conditional on the generator and the amplitude. They show what size of effect the neutral comparison detects in this sample, not what produced the record.

## Supporting material for the models of the main text

The analyses of the main text are those of the public repository's `output/findings/` directory, each described in the Materials and Methods of the main text. This section gives their supporting material: the defense of the spatial field model's priors, the recovery tests of the latent-unit and barrier models, sampler diagnostics, the prior-by-range comparison for the barrier model, the defense of the rim priors, the cell-by-cell outcomes behind the neutral comparisons, and the settings and counts of the two further sets of simulations for the per-class comparison.

**Intervals.** This section reports posterior medians with 90 percent equal-tailed intervals (the 5th to 95th percentiles), matching the main text and the findings files.

**The spatial field model's priors.** We examined each prior in four ways: the direction of its bias, the data it generates before the record is used (the prior predictive), the change in the result under a different prior (sensitivity), and whether the model recovers known values on simulated records (recovery). All were run on the basin fit under the Normal(0, 10) class-intercept prior of the main text (`a0_basin_refit.md`, `a0_predictive_summaries.md`). The run of 2026-10-16 under the earlier Normal(0, 2) prior is kept as `a0_basin_refit_alphaN0-2.md` and `a0_predictive_summaries_alphaN0-2.md`, and is quoted below where the two differ.

*Direction.* The half-Normal(0, 2) prior on the field scale *σ* and the site-level scale *τ* allows large variance of both kinds. The 1/*ρ* prior on the range puts half its mass below 12.2 km, the geometric mean of 0.5 and 300 km. The Normal(0, 10) prior on the class intercepts allows log-odds far beyond any class's rarity.

*Prior predictive.* We drew 200 records from the priors alone at the real geometry and sherd totals. They give a mean within-assemblage Gini-Simpson diversity of 0.13 (5th to 95th percentile 0.00 to 0.43) against the observed 0.56, a mean class richness of 2.2 (1.0 to 4.0) against 6.25, a pooled Gini-Simpson diversity of 0.27 (0.00 to 0.66) against 0.59, and a plug-in two-cluster *F*~ST~ of 0.014 (0.0001 to 0.211) against 0.0062. Of the 200 records, 13 hold a single class, for which *F*~ST~ is undefined. The priors alone therefore generate records far less diverse than the record, and they do not resemble it on any of the four summaries. Under Normal(0, 2), the prior-predictive medians were 0.41, 6.2, 0.75 and 0.028. The posterior predictive medians on the same summaries are 0.564, 6.4, 0.591 and 0.0063.

*Sensitivity.* These checks use the uncentered spatial share $\sigma^2/(\sigma^2+\tau^2)$, the share computed from the model's variance parameters. It is not the contrast-based share of the main text and is higher (0.98 against 0.93 under the same prior, `a0_intercept_prior.md`). Under half-Normal(0, 0.5) on *σ* and *τ*, which favors little variance of either kind, the uncentered share is 0.96 (0.86 to 1.00) against 0.98 (0.92 to 1.00) under the default. Under a uniform prior on *ρ* over 0.5 to 300 km, which favors long ranges, it is 0.98 (0.94 to 1.00). The between-site contrast standard deviations at 4.3, 36.6 and 79.1 km change by at most 0.70 logits under the narrow scale prior (3.13 to 2.43 at 79.1 km) and by at most 0.05 under the uniform range prior. The three class-intercept priors are compared in the main text (Figure 5).

*Recovery.* We simulated records from the model at the real geometry and sherd totals, with a spatial share of 0.2 and 0.9 crossed with a range of 5 and 60 km. The 90 percent interval covers the true uncentered share in all four cases (Table S4). The range and scale are recovered only at a share of 0.9, which is the identification limit the main text states. All eight fits of these checks pass the sampler diagnostics. The record with a share of 0.9 and a range of 5 km needed a third sampler setting (12,000 iterations per chain, adapt_delta 0.999), after the second setting ended with 11 divergent transitions and a tail effective sample size of 205 on the range.

**Recovery of the latent-unit and barrier models.** The main text summarizes these recovery tests and shows them in its Figure 8. The estimates behind that summary follow.

The latent-unit model could not tell a record with units from one without. On records simulated with a between-unit scale of 0.5, its posterior medians were 0.27 to 0.48, below the true value. At true values of 1 and 2, with two records each, the medians were 0.40 and 0.57, and 0.46 and 1.37, again below the true values. On a record simulated with no units, where the true value is zero, it returned 0.52 (0.05 to 1.39). The estimate from a record without units was therefore as large as most of the estimates from records that contained them. The model's sampler also failed its diagnostic checks in 12 of the 14 recovery fits and in the fit to the no-unit record (Figure 8A of the main text). We treat these fits as failed validation attempts and report no estimate from this model on the record. A different class-intercept prior left the result essentially unchanged (next entry).

The barrier model's sampler passed every diagnostic check, but its estimates tracked the true value only weakly. **The rim priors.** Each site frequency of a rim attribute has a Beta(1/2, 1/2) prior, and each composition has a Dirichlet(1/2) prior. We examined them in the same four ways.

*Direction.* The prior adds one pseudo-observation in total and shifts the posterior mean toward 1/2 with weight 1/(N + 1), which is 0.036 at 27 rims and 0.0036 at 277.

*Prior predictive.* Under Beta-Binomial(N, 1/2, 1/2) at each site's rim count, the 5th to 95th percentile interval of the predicted count spans 99 to 100 percent of N at every site. It contains all 57 observed counts for the three rim attributes, so the interval says little about the prior's shape. Beta(1/2, 1/2) favors frequencies near 0 and 1: at 27 rims, it puts probability 0.216 on the two endpoint counts against 0.071 under Beta(1, 1). Of the 57 observed counts, 51 lie between the endpoints and six are zeros, so the prior does not resemble the data.

*Sensitivity.* Medians under Beta(1, 1) and Dirichlet(1) are reported beside every posterior in `rim_r1.md`, sections B and C. They change the latitude and pair-association medians by at most a few hundredths.

*Recovery.* We ran 20 replicates at three true configurations on the 19 sites' rim totals, with 1,000 draws per fit and 90 percent intervals. The sign of the latitude correlation is recovered in 20 of 20, 20 of 20, and 18 of 20 replicates, the last at a true value near zero. The intervals cover the pair-distance correlation in 0.90, 0.95, and 0.80 of replicates. At 20 replicates, a coverage proportion has a binomial standard deviation of 0.067 at a true rate of 0.9. These runs can therefore detect a large failure of coverage but cannot confirm the nominal rate (`rim_r1.md`, section *F*).

**The two-cluster *F*~ST~ against the neutral model, by cell.** The observed cultural *F*~ST~ between the two spatial clusters the site layout supports is 0.0062. It is compared against the neutral band in 30 cells of three kinds. The ten C1 cells vary in occupation spread, the window over which span endpoints are drawn, with span lengths unchanged, at two operating points. The observed value is inside the band in 7 of them. The ten C2 cells are the C1 cells with collection sizes assigned in rank order of span length (inside in 6). The C3 cells vary in regional drift amplitude: five at the primary operating point (inside in 3) and five at the secondary (inside in 1). The total is 17 of 30. The observed value lies above the band, where the band's upper limit falls below it. This occurs at the widest occupation spreads (window settings 4 and 8 in C1 and C2, except primary C1 at 4), at amplitudes of 0 and 0.01 at the primary point, and at amplitudes of 0 to 0.1 at the secondary. The per-cell values are in `per_type_generators_C1.md`, `_C2.md`, `_C3_primary.md` and `_C3_secondary.md`.

The two operating points were selected partly on this statistic. The candidates were the diversity-matched combinations of the six-seed screen whose median *F*~ST~ lay within 50 percent of the observed value. Each was rerun with 50 new seeds, and a loss that included the two-cluster *F*~ST~ was computed from those runs. The primary point has the smallest loss among candidates that still matched at 50 seeds (0.108). The secondary has the smallest (0.148) among those with a different kernel family and population size (`resemblance_stage2.md`). The 30 cells therefore come from combinations selected for an *F*~ST~ that is relatively high and close to the observed value, although neither point has the highest.

Without that selection, the comparison uses the 498 combinations of the wider screen that match the three diversity summaries and have an accepted time-scale measurement, each run with six seeds. Across those combinations, the six-seed median *F*~ST~ has a median of 0.0010 (10th to 90th percentile 0.0003 to 0.0026). The observed value (0.0062, at full precision) exceeds the combination's six-seed 90th percentile in 436 of them (88 percent) and exceeds all six values in 400. In 8, the combination's median is at or above the observed value. By six-seed median, the primary point ranks 25th and the secondary 11th of the 498 (`screen_fst_unselected.md`). Six seeds describe a combination's spread only coarsely, so these are counts of combinations, not probabilities.

*Arrangements of occupation date.* The end times of the accumulation spans are drawn over the window as in the main comparison and are then assigned to sites. Each site receives a score $z = c q + (1 - c) u$. Here $q$ is the site's latitude rank scaled to lie between 0 and 1 (reversed for the arrangements with later dates in the south), $u$ is a uniform random draw, and $c$ is 1 for strict ordering and 0.5 for partial ordering. At $c = 0.5$ and 28 sites, the expected rank correlation between latitude and the score is 0.70 in magnitude, positive when later dates are in the north. In the two cluster arrangements, the sites of one spatial cluster draw their end times from the first half of the window and the sites of the other from the second half.

## Parkin in the settlement record

The main text concludes that decorated frequencies do not establish a boundary in ceramic learning at the edge of the Parkin phase. Two descriptions place Parkin in its regional context without bearing on that boundary.

The settlement record provides further context. Parkin's principal mound is a flat-topped platform 6.5 m (21.3 ft) high on the site's western edge, overlooking the St. Francis River, with six smaller mounds near it. The site covers 17 acres (6.9 ha), is ditched on three sides, and its deposits rise 3 m above the floodplain [@morse_1990; @morse_p_1981]. Its mound is the second tallest of the 55 basin sites with a recorded height, behind Mound Cemetery (13-N-3) at 23 ft, and the tallest exceeds the second by a ratio of only 1.08 (Figure S2). Parkin's height comes from excavation reports, whereas the others come from a regional compilation without per-site sources, so the series mixes measurement sources. Mound heights form a graded series with no gap at the top. Maximum mound height is a limited proxy for political rank. It records the final size of one construction, not the organization that built it, and a graded series is compatible with more than one form of ranking. It therefore describes the settlement record without testing for an administrative tier.

Regional evidence on the production and exchange of stone hoes adds context. Autonomous workshops made Mill Creek hoes at the source and distributed them largely through successive exchanges, with elites at consuming centers gaining at most privileged access [@cobb_2000, 75, 154, 198]. That evidence is not basin-specific and cannot test Parkin's position locally.

## Code, data, and the phasecheck tool

Code and data are at https://github.com/clipo/phases, the code under the MIT License and the data under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International license. A versioned archive will be deposited at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20796240 on publication. The repository holds the source tables (`data/raw/`), the processed matrix (`data/processed/`), the numbered analysis scripts (`analyses/`), the package they share (`src/mls_emergence/`, in which the copying model is `transmission/spatial.py`), the computed outputs (`output/`), the figures (`figures/`), and the tests (`tests/`). `MANIFEST.md` maps every output and figure to the script that writes it. `run_all.sh` rebuilds the figures of the main text from the committed data and findings tables, and with `--analyses` it also reruns the analyses. That rerun first regenerates the simulator time-scale files with `analyses/probe_latent_groups/time_scales.py`, since they are not in the repository. The per-class, regional-drift, and rim tables were produced under NumPy 2.5.4, and the simulation results carried from the earlier version of this study under NumPy 2.4.6. The two result sets have not been regenerated under one version. NumPy 2.5 changed the draws its multinomial generator returns, so seeded simulations do not reproduce exactly across that change, and three test-suite checks pinned to the earlier results fail under 2.5.4. The repository records this and pins NumPy below 2.5 for its test suite. The Stan fits record their own software versions beside each fit. The spatial field, latent-unit, and barrier models are Stan programs under `stan/spatial/`, and the per-class, regional-drift, collection, and rim analyses are under `analyses/per_type/` and `analyses/rims/`. Numbers in this supplement are given with the script or output file they come from.

The phase tests are also packaged as a tool, `phasecheck` (`src/phasecheck/`, guide `docs/PHASECHECK.md`), so that the questions asked here can be asked of another region or another phase scheme. It takes one table of assemblages, in a spreadsheet or a delimited file, with a name, a latitude and longitude, a phase label, and a count of sherds in each class per row, and it rejects tables it cannot use as counts (percentages, identifiers or totals treated as classes, phases with one member, study areas more than 1,000 km across). A plain run answers five questions in order: whether location alone recovers the phases (clustering on coordinates and composition at varying weights); whether the phase boundaries separate the assemblages better than same-size divisions around random centers and compact divisions (cultural *F*~ST~ and the boundary excess, over posterior draws of the counts); whether each phase is distinct from the rest; whether each assemblage fits its own phase; and how different the phases are in sherds. With `--model` it calibrates the copying model with no groups to the record's diversity, screening 297 parameter combinations and confirming the matches on fresh runs, and answers four more: whether the model reproduces the record's diversity; whether the record differs between phases by more than the model produces at the confirmed combinations; whether simulated records sort into the phases; and whether the comparison could detect a restriction at the phase boundaries, from records simulated with and without one. It writes a Markdown report with the tables as CSV and JSON. A distance matrix along rivers or trails can replace straight-line distance for the boundary excess, and a column of dates or seriation scores can order the assemblages in time.

The tool implements the earlier version's tests, not the spatial field, latent-unit and barrier models or the diversity-matched neutral comparison of the main text. It is checked against the record of this study as the earlier version analyzed it (`examples/phasecheck/`, `tests/phasecheck/`). With the river distances and that version's seed it reproduces that version's phase-boundary comparison to the digits printed (between-phase *F*~ST~ 0.0121; probabilities 0.57 and 0.72; boundary excess +7.3; probabilities 0.58 and 0.65), the recovery by location, each assemblage's fit, and the sherd differences. Its copying model is the paper's model and, with the paper's seeds, gives the calibration's 51 matched and 48 confirmed combinations and the model comparison's 7 of 300 runs reaching the observed between-phase *F*~ST~. It does not include local innovation as an alternative to a restriction, the map of compositional change, the settlement-gap test, or figures, and its calibration selects among the confirmed combinations by the difference between the phases rather than between the two spatial clusters. The probabilities it reports are the phase lines' rank among alternative lines, not significance levels, and on a record of this size the comparison discriminates only moderately, as its guide documents.
