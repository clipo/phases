## Statistical conventions

We use the conventions of the main text (Statistical approach), and a few terms recur throughout. A *credible interval* is the range within which a quantity lies with the stated probability, given the data and the prior. A *95 percent range* describes a simulation: it holds the middle 95 percent of the values a statistic takes when a model is run many times with fixed parameters. The *share of runs reaching the observed value* is the fraction of those runs in which the simulated statistic is at least as large as the one computed from the record, so a small share means the model rarely produces what the record shows. A *posterior predictive probability* makes the same comparison for a fitted model, using datasets generated from its posterior, and values near 0 or 1 indicate a poor fit. A *direct* value is computed from the counts as they stand, with no allowance for sampling uncertainty. No inference about the record rests on significance tests or p-values. The one test reported, a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test of rank uniformity in the section on tests of change along the sequence, checks the software's calibration on simulated data.

Three further terms follow the main text. *Closure* is the restriction of copying to within a group, the property that would make a phase a bounded group. The 16 versions of the bounded-groups model are *settings*, and the calibrated parameter sets of the copying model are *combinations*.

## How this supplement is organized

The sections follow the order of the main text, except that everything on the copying model is kept together. The first four define the measures and ask which of them can detect a bounded group on a record of this size. The next four support choices made in the Methods: the spatial model with no divisions, the minimum sample size, further checks of the measures, and a test of why only cultural $F_{ST}$ can be used. Seven sections then document the copying model: how it compares with the record, how it is built and calibrated, how sensitive the comparison is, what happens when its simplifying assumptions are relaxed, what happens when the phases are treated as groups, what record could separate the two explanations that remain, and how the record differs from the model at finer scales. A section on the map of compositional change is included, since it uses the same simulations. The remaining sections cover tests of change along the sequence, the clustering that recovers the phases, settlement and waterways, the second case, the coordinates, and the radiocarbon chronology.

## The four measures in full

We began with four measures, each a different trace that restricted copying should leave in assemblage frequencies. This section defines them. The main text draws its inferences from the third, cultural $F_{ST}$, and uses the fourth, the boundary excess, for description; the sections that follow show why the other two cannot be used on this record.

**Departure from neutral copying.** Following Neiman [-@neiman_1995], we estimate the transmission parameter $\theta$ two ways for each assemblage. The homozygosity estimator uses the unbiased sample homozygosity $F = \sum_i n_i(n_i-1)/[N(N-1)]$, where $n_i$ is the count of class $i$ and $N$ the assemblage size, and inverts the neutral relation $F = 1/(1+\theta)$ to give $\theta_F = (1-F)/F$. The richness estimator solves the Ewens [-@ewens_1972] expectation for the number of classes $k$,

$$\mathbb{E}[k \mid \theta, N] = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \frac{\theta}{\theta + i},$$

for the $\theta_E$ that reproduces the observed $k$. Under neutral copying, the two agree. Conformist copying concentrates sherds on common classes, raising $F$ and lowering $\theta_F$ relative to $\theta_E$, and a preference for novelty does the reverse. The measure is the ratio $\theta_F/\theta_E$, computed within spatial clusters and averaged across them, since pooling assemblages from divergent groups would cancel the effect.

**Seriation structure.** We use the iterative deterministic seriation method of Lipo, Madsen, and Dunnell [-@lipo_madsen_dunnell_2015; @lipo_madsen_dunnell_hunt_1997; @dunnell_1970; @graves_cachola-abad_1996; @marquardt_1978; @cowgill_1972; @obrien_lyman_1999; @lyman_obrien_2006]. A set of assemblages is co-seriable if some ordering makes each decorated class's frequency curve single-peaked and gapless. We enumerate the maximal co-seriable groups, the largest subsets for which a valid ordering exists, and report their number, their maximum size, and the number of groups each assemblage joins. A single group indicates one interacting community; many small, overlapping groups indicate a field too varied to fit one ordering; many small, separate groups indicate divided learning. Checking every ordering is intractable beyond about fourteen assemblages, so the method builds solutions upward from valid three-assemblage orderings, adding assemblages while every class stays single-peaked and continuous. An ordering breaks when a class is not single-peaked along it or when a class's relative frequency jumps between adjacent assemblages by more than a continuity threshold, which we set at 0.10. The overlapping structure reported in the main text holds across a range of thresholds. In the recovery experiment and the observed trajectory, the statistic is the number of maximal groups per assemblage within each period, computed the same way. The idealized validation (Figure S1) uses a simpler proxy, the number of fall-then-rise reversals in each class along the supplied order. Neither version includes the bootstrap significance step of the published method.

**Between- and within-group variance.** Cultural $F_{ST}$ is computed on Gini-Simpson diversity $H = 1 - \sum_i p_i^2$ as

$$F_{ST} = \frac{H_T - H_S}{H_T},$$

where $H_T$ is the diversity of the pooled assemblages and $H_S$ is the size-weighted mean diversity within clusters, which is Nei's [-@nei_1973] diversity form of the cultural $F_{ST}$ of Bell, Richerson, and McElreath [-@bell_richerson_mcelreath_2009]. It is zero when clusters share a repertoire and rises as they hold distinct ones.

**Boundary excess.** We measure Brainerd-Robinson similarity $S_{ij} = 200 - \sum_k |100\,p_{ik} - 100\,p_{jk}|$ between every pair of assemblages. A Mantel correlation cannot separate a sharp boundary from a smooth fade with distance, so within each band of pairwise distance we take the mean within-cluster similarity minus the mean between-cluster similarity and average over bands. Under pure isolation by distance, the excess is near zero; a sharp social boundary makes it large and positive. Spatial clusters come from $k$-means on site coordinates with multiple restarts, with the number of clusters chosen by the mean silhouette.

**Trends and subsampling.** Each measure's trend along the sequence is its rank correlation with position. All size-control draws take 50 sherds from each assemblage without replacement; an assemblage of exactly 50 is unchanged. We average the three measures the recovery experiment shows to be unusable over 400 draws. The $F_{ST}$ trend is averaged over 4,000 draws, and we quote the spread of single draws (2.5th to 97.5th percentiles, -0.80 to +0.40) rather than the Monte Carlo error of the mean, because a single draw has a standard deviation of 0.42 and the spread is what says whether the record resolves the trend. All of these condition on the observed assemblages, clusters, and order rather than on uncertainty about deposits, chronology, or model choice.

## The idealized validation model

Before applying the measures to the record, we checked that they behave as intended on artificial data in which the process is known. This section describes the simulation that produces those data.

The simulation copies decorated variants under neutral transmission [@neiman_1995; @bentley_hahn_shennan_2004] and has three controls: how far group class profiles diverge, how strongly potters conform within groups (a frequency-dependent copying bias after Henrich [-@henrich_2001]), and how group profiles are placed on the landscape. Four configurations produce the four processes of Table S1. Bounded groups raise divergence and conformity together across spatially bounded groups. Environmental patchiness holds a fixed difference between groups under neutral copying, keyed to an environmental layout. Conformity in one community raises conformity in a single undivided pool with no divergence. Smooth distance-limited copying produces a gradient under neutral copying. Each configuration produces a sequence of group-by-class samples along an order, and we compute the four measures on each without knowing its source, across 500 random seeds. On the bounded-group runs, we also compute the correlations among the four measures to confirm that the measures move together because of the process and not because they measure the same thing. These assemblages are large and clean (12 groups, several hundred sherds each), so the test shows what the measures can do in principle; the record-matched recovery (Figure S2) shows what survives on the real record.

## Can the record detect a boundary?

A process that genuinely divides a population into groups should leave its mark on every aspect of how decoration was copied, whereas a process that only resembles it leaves a mark on some aspects and not others. We therefore began with four measures, each a different consequence of restricted interaction (defined above), and asked which of them this record can use. Only a bounded interaction group drives all four together (Table S1). Environmental patchiness produces spatial structure without departing from neutral copying or breaking the seriation. Conformity within a single community departs from neutral copying without creating any boundaries. Smooth distance-limited copying produces only a fading of similarity with distance. Copying on a finite network is less tidy than that smooth ideal, because chance divergence among places raises $F_{ST}$ and the boundary excess with no social boundary present [@lipo_dinapoli_madsen_hunt_2021].

On idealized data, the four measures behave as designed (Figure S1). When interaction narrows toward closed groups, all four rise together, whereas the other three processes move only some of them. Under closure, the four are tightly correlated (mean absolute correlation 0.88), and under the other processes they are nearly independent (0.14 to 0.28), so their joint movement carries information.

Only a bounded interaction group drives all four together (starred). Across 500 random seeds, the bounded-group case was identified in 492 (95 percent credible interval 0.97 to 0.99, uniform prior), and none of the three other processes was identified in any of the 500 (upper limit of the 95 percent credible interval 0.007 for each, uniform prior; `analyses/04_validation_report.py`). Right panel: the mean absolute correlation among the four measures, about 0.88 under closure and 0.14 to 0.28 under the other processes, so their joint movement is informative rather than redundant.](../../figures/fig2_validation.png)

A real record is smaller, noisier, and time-averaged, so a measure that works in principle may fail in practice. We built one synthetic assemblage for each real one, matching its sample size, spatial cluster, and position in the sequence, and imposed closure of known strength *s*, from 0 (no groups) to 1 (sharply separated groups), increasing along the sequence. We time-averaged the synthetic assemblages by blending neighboring periods, subsampled them exactly as we did the real ones, and asked which measures recovered the imposed closure [@crema_edinborough_kerig_shennan_2014; @kandler_shennan_2015; @perreault_2019; @holdaway_wandsnider_2008]. Only cultural $F_{ST}$ recovers it (Figure S2). It rises steadily as imposed closure increases. The departure from neutral copying does not move in one direction as conformity increases, the boundary excess does not respond to the two spatial clusters the site layout supports, and the number of seriation groups does not rise as closure predicts. The section "Why only cultural $F_{ST}$ can be used" below shows that this reduction is a property of the record rather than of how closure was imposed.

The same simulations let us ask how much closure the basin could contain. For each closure strength, they show how often a trend like the observed one arises, and treating each strength as equally likely beforehand (a uniform prior over *s*) gives a posterior distribution for the strength. This posterior refers to closure that grows along the sequence; a boundary present from the start would not produce a trend, and the phase-line tests of the main text address that case. If the phases were becoming bounded groups, differences between them should grow as interaction narrows. Along the seriation order, they do not (Figure S3), although that order runs largely south to north rather than early to late, so this test speaks more to change across the basin than to change through time. The raw trend in cultural $F_{ST}$ along the sequence is strongly negative, a rank correlation of -0.80, but that trend is a sampling artifact: assemblage size also rises along the sequence (Spearman +0.61). Once every assemblage is subsampled to a common 50 sherds, the trend averages -0.048 over 4,000 subsamples, and a single subsample returns anything from -0.80 to +0.40. The record therefore shows no trend distinguishable from none. Two of the six periods hold assemblages from a single spatial cluster and carry no between-cluster variance, so the trend rests on four. Read against the recovery experiment, a trend this small corresponds to closure with a posterior median of 0.155 and a 95 percent credible interval of 0.00 to 0.37, with a probability of 0.001 on closure of 0.5 or more. With the record's time-averaging built into the simulations, the posterior widens to a median of 0.222 and an interval of 0.00 to 0.565, and 9 percent of it lies at 0.5 or above. Reversing the order barely changes this: the median becomes 0.17 (0.00 to 0.39), or 0.25 (0.00 to 0.60) with time-averaging. The record is consistent with no closure and with weak closure growing along the sequence, and shows no moderate or strong closure of that kind.

## Bayesian estimates of the $F_{ST}$ trend

The section above reports the $F_{ST}$ trend as a rank correlation on subsampled assemblages, which discards most of the sherds in the larger assemblages. Here we estimate the trend from all of the counts. The Balding-Nichols model is fitted to each period, giving a posterior for that period's $F_{ST}$ whose width reflects how many sherds it holds, and the trend is estimated as a slope across those posteriors. The prior on $F_{ST}$ is Beta(1,10). We chose it because a flat prior, although it looks neutral, predicts far more differentiation than this record could show: with these assemblages it expects a median $F_{ST}$ of 0.236 and places 44 percent of its predictive mass above 0.30, against an observed 0.0062, whereas Beta(1,10) expects a median of 0.026 with 2 percent above 0.30. Because the model treats sampling as part of the data rather than removing it by subsampling, its uncertainty is a credible interval rather than a subsampling spread, and its trend is a slope in $F_{ST}$ units per standardized period rather than a rank correlation. The per-period posteriors are in Table S2.

## The spatial model with no groups

Cultural $F_{ST}$ can be computed only after the basin has been divided into groups, and its value depends on the division. To describe the record without dividing it, we fitted a model in which composition varies smoothly over the map. This section gives the model, what it can and cannot show, and how well it reproduces the record.

The model treats each assemblage's decorated-class composition as a multinomial draw whose class probabilities, on the logit scale, sum a class mean, a spatially correlated surface over the assemblage coordinates, and an assemblage-specific term with no spatial structure. The surface has a Matérn-family covariance with amplitude $\sigma$ and length scale $\rho$, and the assemblage-specific term has standard deviation $\tau$. In the version we report, the assemblage-specific term enters the covariance directly rather than as a second latent surface. That halves the number of latent parameters and keeps the two terms from substituting for one another. We report the spatial share, $\sigma^2 / (\sigma^2 + \tau^2)$, the fraction of the latent, logit-scale variance among assemblages assigned to the spatial surface rather than to independent assemblage deviations. It is a ratio of variance components on the latent scale, not the fraction of observed compositional variation explained. Nor is it a measure of group absence. The surface can take any smooth shape the covariance allows, so the shared composition of a group whose members lie near one another enters through $\sigma$ at the sampled sites, and $\tau$ carries only deviations that are independent from one assemblage to the next [@rasmussen_williams_2006, chap. 2]. A high spatial share is therefore expected under geographically aligned groups and under continuous copying. We have not fitted the model to simulated bounded groups at these locations, nor compared it with a version with an explicit boundary term, so we use it only to describe the record, and its predictive checks below as evidence that a surface with no explicit divisions describes the record adequately. The length scale has an inverse-gamma prior that places its mass within the range the basin's geometry can resolve. The models are written in Stan and fitted by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (rstan 2.32); model files and scripts are in the repository (`stan/spatial/`, `analyses/02_spatial/`).

Fitted to the 28 assemblages and ten classes, the spatial share is 0.987 (95 percent credible interval 0.954 to 1.000) in the reported form and 0.987 (0.954 to 1.000) with the assemblage term as a second surface. The data do not determine the length scale. In the reported form, its posterior median is 199 km (95 percent credible interval 92 to 294 km), longer than the basin itself. The sampler reached its maximum tree depth on every iteration with no divergent transitions, indicating that many length scales fit the data equally well (a ridge in the posterior) rather than a sampling fault. Diagnostics for the reported form: R-hat 1.0016, bulk effective sample size 1,891, tail effective sample size 6,247, 0 divergent transitions of 12,000, all 12,000 iterations at the maximum tree depth of 10, E-BFMI 0.685; for the two-surface form, R-hat 1.0050, bulk effective sample size 618, 0 divergences, all iterations at the maximum tree depth, E-BFMI 0.807 (`output/findings/gp_basin_fit.md`). The spatial share does not depend on which length scale is used, and it is well sampled, whereas the length scale is not. We therefore report the length scale as unresolved, and read the fit as showing no detectable fade in composition across the St. Francis drainage beyond what the surface already carries. Recovery tests on simulated surfaces with known length scales show that this design resolves scales only well below the basin's extent (`analyses/02_spatial/01_recovery.R`, `analyses/48_length_scale_recovery.py`).

The model reproduces quantities it was not fitted to. Its predictive interval for the between-cluster $F_{ST}$ at three clusters is 0.0123 to 0.0192 (median 0.0155) against an observed 0.0156 (posterior predictive probability 0.468). For the fade of similarity with distance, measured here as a rank correlation with straight-line distance, it is -0.383 to -0.296 (median -0.338) against -0.360 (0.835), for mean within-assemblage diversity 0.553 to 0.577 against 0.563 (0.59), and for the spread of that diversity 0.097 to 0.122 against 0.112 (0.258). A surface with no explicit divisions therefore reproduces the observed differentiation at three clusters, which the copying model does not, as well as the fade with distance and the diversity structure. The copying model misses the shorter-range variation, but at the sampled sites it is smooth enough for such a surface to carry it, whether or not it arose at group edges. It describes the pattern rather than a mechanism, so it complements the copying model rather than competing with it.

### How cultural $F_{ST}$ depends on the division

The between-cluster $F_{ST}$ depends on how many clusters the basin is cut into. Fitted with the Balding-Nichols model, its posterior median moves from 0.0062 (95 percent credible interval 0.0051 to 0.0076) at two clusters, through 0.0155 (0.0133 to 0.0180) at three, to 0.0302 (0.0267 to 0.0342) at five and 0.0304 (0.0268 to 0.0343) at six, and the intervals at the two ends do not overlap. It also depends on where the cuts fall. The $k$-means division at two clusters is stable, since twenty seeds return it, and unremarkable, at the 43rd percentile of 150 alternative contiguous divisions into two (median 0.0067), but those alternatives range from 0.0018 to 0.0163 between their 5th and 95th percentiles. A quantity this sensitive to how the basin is divided cannot be interpreted by itself. This is why the main text also fits a model with no divisions, and why every between-cluster value is compared against the copying model at the same number of clusters.

## The minimum sample size, and why it is 75

This section explains why assemblages need at least 75 decorated sherds to enter the analysis. Cultural $F_{ST}$ computed directly from counts is biased upward by sampling. Two assemblages drawn from the same population of pots differ because each is a finite sample, and the spurious variance between them scales roughly as $1/n$. To find the count at which that artifact becomes small relative to the differences we interpret, we drew pairs of multinomial samples of size $n$ from the basin's pooled class profile, 4,000 pairs at each size, and computed $F_{ST}$ between them. The two share one profile by construction, so all of the $F_{ST}$ between them is sampling (`analyses/82_minimum_sample_size.py`). The reference is the observed between-cluster $F_{ST}$ at five clusters, 0.0307.

| decorated sherds per assemblage | spurious $F_{ST}$, median | 95th percentile | median as a share of the observed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | 0.0146 | 0.0630 | 48% |
| 50 | 0.0070 | 0.0305 | 23% |
| **75** | 0.0044 | 0.0195 | 14% |
| 100 | 0.0034 | 0.0156 | 11% |
| 150 | 0.0023 | 0.0105 | 7% |
| 200 | 0.0016 | 0.0074 | 5% |
| 300 | 0.0011 | 0.0052 | 4% |
| 500 | 0.0006 | 0.0030 | 2% |
| 1000 | 0.0003 | 0.0015 | 1% |

At 75 sherds, the median artifact is about a seventh of the observed five-cluster differentiation, and its 95th percentile (0.0195) lies below it, whereas at 50 sherds, the 95th percentile (0.0305) is nearly equal to it. The five-cluster value is the reference because it is the finest division analyzed cluster by cluster (analyses 72 and 85), where clusters hold the fewest sherds and a sampling artifact would matter most. Pooling into clusters shrinks the artifact further: redrawing every assemblage from the pooled profile at its own sherd count gives each five-cluster term at most 0.0003 (the section on the finer-scale differences below). Against the two-cluster value of 0.0062, the artifact is proportionally larger at every sample size, which is why the copying model is always sampled at the real sherd counts: it then carries the same artifact as the data. Above 75, the bias shrinks slowly, by about three percentage points between 75 and 100, while assemblages are lost quickly: a minimum of 100 would remove three more basin assemblages (Davis, Irby, and Grant). The analytic expectation for two equal samples from one profile is close to $1/n$. Mainfort [-@mainfort_2003] kept sites with 800 or more sherds in all; decorated sherds are 13 percent of his table, so his rule corresponds to about 100 decorated sherds. The copying model is sampled at the observed sherd counts and carries the same bias, so the model comparison is not distorted by it. The minimum protects readings of single small clusters, where a value computed on a few dozen sherds would be mostly artifact.

## Further checks of the measures

Several further checks are provided in the supplementary code. Neiman's [-@neiman_1995] sample-size diagnostic correlates the two neutrality estimates, and their difference, with assemblage size and with position in the sequence, to separate a transmission signal from a sample-size artifact. Neiman's squared Euclidean distance between assemblages is computed with its relation to diversity and its trend along the sequence. Measures of clustering tendency on the correspondence-analysis ordination favor a single group: the Hopkins statistic is 0.71, where 0.5 indicates no tendency, the gap statistic favors one cluster, and the best silhouette is 0.54 [@hopkins_skellam_1954; @rousseeuw_1987; @tibshirani_walther_hastie_2001]. The assemblages form a near-continuum rather than sharply bounded groups.

The repository also contains a sensitivity grid that we do not use (`output/sensitivity_grid.md`). It computes raw trends without subsampling, on sets of 38, 36, and 17 assemblages defined by latitude rather than by phase, so its trends carry the sample-size artifact that subsampling removes. Two entries show all three raw trends rising together, and we note this so a reader of the code does not mistake them for a result.

## Why only cultural $F_{ST}$ can be used

The recovery experiment imposes closure as divergence between clusters, which is what cultural $F_{ST}$ measures, so the experiment might favor cultural $F_{ST}$ only because the groups were built in the form that the measure detects. We tested this by imposing closure through two further routes, each matched to a different measure, and asking which measure recovers each (`analyses/22_generator_diagnostic.py`, 60 seeds, the real configuration of 28 assemblages, two clusters, six periods, subsampled to 50). One route raises conformity in a single undivided pool, which the neutral departure is designed to detect. The other groups assemblages into social groups scrambled relative to the spatial clusters, which seriation structure is designed to detect.

Neither route is recovered by the measure designed for it. Under conformity in one pool, the neutral departure falls rather than rises (its mean trend moves by -0.73 between no closure and strong closure) and no other measure responds. Under the scrambled social groups, the seriation measure barely moves (-0.21). $F_{ST}$, by contrast, responds in both cases. In every case, it was scored at the two $k$-means spatial clusters, never at the social groups themselves. Its mean trend rises from +0.01 to +0.96 under spatial divergence and from +0.07 to +0.77 under the scrambled social groups. The second rise happens because three social groups assigned at random over 28 assemblages are unevenly represented in the two spatial clusters, so their divergence leaks into between-cluster variance. Subsampling more deeply, to 100 or 150 sherds, does not rescue the other measures. The reduction to $F_{ST}$ is therefore a property of the record, 28 time-averaged assemblages over ten aggregated classes, rather than of the way closure was imposed. It also sets the limits stated in the main text. Closure carried purely by conformity within one pool moves none of the measures. Social groups that cut across the map can raise $F_{ST}$ at spatial divisions, but none of the four measures identifies them as groups: the only measure designed to register them, seriation structure, does not respond, and every division this study scores is spatial.

## The basin comparison in detail

This section compares the record with the copying model in more detail than the main text does. We simulated neutral copying and a bounded-groups variant on the real layout of the 28 assemblages, using the model and calibration described in the next section. The calibration is itself informative. Under uniform innovation, in which the ten classes are interchangeable, one of the 297 parameter combinations (120 learners, innovation 0.0002, mixing 0.2) falls within the 10 percent tolerance on both the six-run screen and the fifty-run check. None of the 48 reaches the observed between-cluster $F_{ST}$ at its median; the highest fifty-run median is 0.0028 against 0.0062 observed, and 18 of the 48 include the observation within their 95 percent ranges.

At the matched combination most favorable to the model (2,000 learners, innovation 0.001, mixing 0.02, interaction length 24 km along the rivers), the observed between-cluster $F_{ST}$ of 0.0062 at two clusters lies inside the simulated range of 0.0002 to 0.0112 (median 0.0021, 500 runs). ## The neutral copying model and its calibration

This section describes how the copying model works, how we chose its three parameters, how we measure distance, and how we sample simulated assemblages so they can be compared with real ones.

The comparison uses one finite-population network model of decorated-class transmission, run on the real geography of the 28 assemblages. Each assemblage location is a node holding a population of learners. Each generation, a learner copies the class of another learner, drawn from its own node with probability $1-m$ and from another node with probability $m$, the other node chosen with a weight that falls off exponentially with distance along the rivers at length scale $L$. With probability $\mu$, the learner instead adopts a class by innovation. The model tracks class frequencies rather than individual learners: next-generation frequencies at each node are a multinomial draw of $N$ learners from the copying-plus-innovation probabilities, which is equivalent to independent learner-by-learner copying. The repertoire is fixed at the ten measured classes. The bounded-groups variant multiplies every copying weight that crosses a specified division by a factor $\ell$ before normalizing; $\ell = 1$ is copying by distance alone, and 0.5, 0.1, and 0.03 impose progressively stronger boundaries on the basin's spatial clusters. Each run discards 1,200 burn-in generations and records 90 evenly spaced samples over the next 1,800 generations.

How new variants enter the model depends on what an archaeological class is. Under uniform innovation, the standard K-allele model, an innovating learner adopts any class with equal probability, treating the classes as interchangeable. Under pooled-profile innovation, an innovating learner adopts each class in proportion to its share of the region's pooled counts, reflecting that the culture-history types lump unequal numbers of underlying decorative variants. The pooled profile carries no information about any division, so it does not build the tested boundary into the model, and neither version introduces a copying bias.

Between-group predictions depend on population size, mixing, and innovation together, so the model is calibrated before any between-group comparison. The calibration uses three within-assemblage summaries that do not involve the tested division: mean within-assemblage Gini-Simpson diversity, mean number of classes present at the observed sherd counts, and Gini-Simpson diversity of the pooled counts. A grid of population size (120, 2,000, and 10,000 learners), innovation rate (0.0002 to 0.2), and mixing rate (0.001 to 0.4) is screened at six runs per combination, with the same sampling through time applied to the data, and a combination is matched when all three simulated summaries fall within 10 percent of the observed values. We then rerun each matched combination fifty times with fresh seeds and recheck the match; the baseline is the combination, among those still matched, with the highest fifty-run median between-cluster $F_{ST}$, so the comparison is as favorable to the model as the grid allows.

We confirm at fifty runs because a median over six runs is noisy, and choosing the highest of about fifty noisy medians selects for chance. The combination ranked first on six runs had a six-run median (0.0042) about twice its fifty-run median (0.0022), and an independent check with fresh seeds gave 0.0020 (`output/findings/calibration_stability.md`). The check reproduces the six-run ranking only in part: the independent fifty-run medians agree with it at a rank correlation of +0.64, and the two rankings share one of their five leading combinations. Nor is the leading combination sharply identified at fifty runs: the combination the independent check ranks first (120 learners, innovation 0.024, mixing 0.01) is not among the six that lead the calibration's own fifty-run ranking. We therefore repeat the key comparisons at the six most favorable confirmed combinations (Table S8).

We then run the baseline 500 times, the boundary factors 30 times each at interaction lengths of 12 and 24 km, and seven sensitivity cases (burn-in doubled, a single-class starting state with and without burn-in, and innovation and mixing halved and doubled) 50 times each. In the baseline, sensitivity, and boundary runs of this analysis (Table S4), the share of simulated datasets reaching the observed value is reported as $(e+1)/(R+1)$, where $e$ is the number of runs at or above the observed statistic and $R$ is the number of runs; the scale sweep, combination-sensitivity (Table S8), phases-as-groups, and power tables report the plain share $e/R$. We store every run with a record of the configuration, inputs, source code, and package versions that produced it, and rerun it when any of them changes.

We measure copying distance along rivers because interaction in this settlement system plausibly followed watercourses, and because that choice is more favorable to the model. We build a graph from the mapped hydrography (LMVHydrology centerlines clipped to the basin with a 25 km margin), join vertices within 250 m of one another, keep the largest connected piece, and attach each assemblage to its nearest node, adding the attachment distance at both ends. All 28 assemblages connect, and the mean distance from an assemblage to a mapped channel is 2.69 km. Paths along the rivers are longer than straight lines by a mean ratio of 3.8 and a median of 2.9 per pair; the mean is pulled up by pairs that are close in a straight line but must route around a meander. The pottery does not follow the rivers more closely than it follows straight lines. Decorated-class distance between assemblages correlates with straight-line distance at 0.38 and with river distance at 0.25, and once straight-line distance is held fixed, river distance adds nothing (-0.05). Between assemblages in different phases, straight-line distance correlates at 0.26 and river distance at -0.02. The network is modern hydrography, and the St. Francis, Tyronza, and Mississippi have all moved since these sites were occupied. Both measures are carried through the relaxations below. At every interaction length from 6 to 96 km the median under straight-line distance is at or below the median under river distance at the calibrated combination, so river distance is the choice more favorable to the model.

The assemblages are not contemporaneous, so the simulated field is sampled over time rather than at a single moment. Each simulated assemblage is drawn from the recorded sample matching its real counterpart's position on the first correspondence-analysis axis and accumulated over a trailing window of eight samples, so that, like a real deposit, it mixes production across a span. The earliest position maps to the end of the first complete window, so every assemblage gets the same window length. Each simulated assemblage is then subsampled to its real counterpart's sherd count and analyzed exactly as the real ones are, with the same $k$-means clusters, community detection, and $F_{ST}$. Because the dates only weakly indicate which end of the seriation axis is later, every run is also sampled in reverse order and as if contemporaneous, with every assemblage drawn from the final window.

The source of an assemblage's counts matters little. Within each of the three five-cluster groups holding at least two rows of each kind, we computed the posterior probability that rows carrying the survey's counts alone differ from rows carrying Lipo's compilation more than a random split of that group into subsets of the same sizes, with each assemblage's class proportions drawn from Dirichlet(counts + 1/2) (`analyses/78_source_effect.py`). It is 0.17, 0.12, and 0.17 in the three. The rows carrying the survey's counts alone are also the smallest, so source and sample size are confounded throughout.

Because community labels are arbitrary and switch between runs, we summarize the basin's simulated communities by co-membership (Figure S6). For each assemblage, we record the share of runs in which it falls in the same community as Parkin. Under the calibrated model, that share is 0.95 to 1.00 for every assemblage because the model returns a single community in most runs. Under pooled-profile innovation, the 29 time-sampled runs that return two or more communities give a between-community $F_{ST}$ with a median of 0.012 (95 percent range 0.009 to 0.019), and none reach the observed 0.039. Under uniform innovation, which does not robustly fit the assemblages' diversity, the observed value falls within a wide interval when assemblages are sampled through time or in reverse order, and above the interval when they are treated as contemporaneous.

## Relaxing four simplifications of the copying model

The calibrated model reproduces the difference between two clusters, although its median falls short. It does not reproduce the larger differences among three or more clusters, where the observed value lies at or beyond the upper edge of the model's range: outside it with the seeds of Table 1, Table S7, and Table S5 (main text Table 1; Table S7).

The model makes four simplifying assumptions: every site has the same population, copying falls off exponentially with distance along the rivers, every deposit accumulated over the same span, and new variants arise at the same rates everywhere. Any of these could account for the shortfall, so we relaxed each in turn. Each variant is still neutral copying. It is the same model with one assumption changed, run on the basin at the calibrated combination. Every variant, and the baseline, is judged on the same three criteria, kept separate because they answer different questions: whether the median reaches the observation (median discrepancy), how often runs reach it (predictive coverage), and whether all three diversity summaries stay within the 10 percent tolerance (joint fit to diversity). A variant that reaches the observed differentiation only by losing the fit to diversity has exchanged one misfit for another. The tables below give the two-cluster sweeps of analyses 64 and 65, with the median $F_{ST}$, its 95 percent range, the shortfall (observed divided by the model's median), and whether diversity stays matched; the full series is in `output/findings/other_departures.md`. The two-cluster value is already inside the baseline's predictive range, so Table S5 then scores the main settings at three and four clusters and at the phase lines, where the baseline falls short.

We relax each assumption separately, always at the baseline calibration. A variant that breaks the diversity match here might fit after recalibration, which we have not attempted, so a broken match means the variant is unsupported at this calibration, not that it cannot fit.

In this variant, each cluster draws its new variants from a partly reordered version of the regional class profile, with the strength setting the share of classes reordered, and the table reports 1,200 runs per setting. A reordering keeps the set of class frequencies exactly, so every cluster keeps the same richness and diversity in its source of new variants and differs only in which classes it favors. Using each cluster's observed profile instead would guarantee the result by construction. We rejected two other ways of building local profiles because each changed something besides which classes are favored: a random (Dirichlet) perturbation of the regional profile made profiles sparser as well as different, lowering mean richness, and mixing the regional profile with a reordered copy flattened it, raising diversity well above the observed value.

Individual runs are widely spread, so the agreement at strengths of 0.2 to 0.25 is between the model's median and the two-cluster observation rather than between narrow distributions, and at three and four clusters (Table S5) the medians still fall short while the ranges cover the observation. It identifies a mechanism the record is consistent with, not one it demonstrates, and the mechanism is still neutral copying, with new styles arising locally rather than at the same rates across the basin.

## The phases as groups: restricted copying and local innovation together

This section gives the phases both properties a bounded group would have and asks whether the record favors them. The bounded-groups model above draws new variants from one regional pool that no boundary interrupts, so it is at a disadvantage against local innovation, which most often covers the shortfall. Here the phases are the groups, and both properties of a bounded group are given to them together (`analyses/84_phases_as_groups.py`): copying across phase lines is multiplied by a factor of 1 (no copying boundary), 0.5, 0.1, or 0.03, and each phase draws new variants from a partly reordered version of the regional profile, with 0, 10, 20, or 30 percent of the classes reordered. Each of the 16 settings is run 300 times at the calibrated combination (2,000 learners, innovation 0.001, mixing 0.02), with interaction length 24 km along the rivers, and each run is scored on the between-phase $F_{ST}$, the $F_{ST}$ at the two spatial clusters, the boundary excess at the phase lines, and the three diversity summaries. The observed values are a between-phase $F_{ST}$ of 0.0123 and a boundary excess at the phase lines of +7.7.

We summarize the grid with a posterior over its 16 settings, estimated by approximate Bayesian computation. Every setting is treated as equally likely beforehand, the 5 percent of all 4,800 runs whose six standardized summaries lie closest to the record's are kept, and a setting's posterior probability is its share of the runs kept. The prior places three quarters of its mass on some copying boundary, which leans toward the groups hypothesis, and three quarters on some local innovation. The posterior probability of a copying boundary is 0.75, and of local innovation 0.68. Across acceptance rates of 1, 2, 5, and 10 percent, and with the summaries restricted to the between-phase $F_{ST}$ and the boundary excess, the first runs from 0.73 to 0.81 and the second from 0.60 to 0.79. ## What record would separate local innovation from restricted copying?

The grid of the previous section leaves two explanations of the additional variation standing: new styles arising locally with copying unrestricted, and copying restricted at the phase lines. This section asks what record could choose between them (`analyses/97_design_to_separate.py`, `output/findings/design_to_separate.md`). We simulated records under each explanation, at settings that reach the observed between-phase $F_{ST}$ at comparable rates (22 and 30 percent of runs): copying across the phase lines cut to 3 percent with one regional pool of new variants, and copying unrestricted with a fifth of the classes reordered by phase. Each simulated record was run through the procedure of the previous section (the same sixteen settings, six summaries, and 5 percent acceptance) with the record itself left out of the reference table, and we recorded the posterior probability of a restriction. A design separates the two explanations when that probability is high for records made with a restriction and low for records made without one. We summarize the separation as the share of pairs, one record of each kind, in which the record made with a restriction receives the higher probability. That share is 0.5 when the procedure cannot tell the two apart and 1 when it always can. It describes the simulated records and is not a test.

The designs change one thing at a time. Sherd counts are multiplied by four. Each of the ten decorated classes is split into two or four finer classes whose frequencies fall by halves, so that every class gains rarer members and the ten classes are recovered by adding them back up; local innovation still reorders the ten classes, so the process is the same one read at a finer grain. Assemblages are added at late-period sites of the settlement compilation that have none, with sherd counts drawn from the observed counts. Finally, the existing collections described below are added at their own locations and counts. The first design is the record as it is, and it reproduces the 4,800 runs of the previous section exactly.

On the record as it is, the two kinds of record receive overlapping probabilities, and the record's own value of 0.75 lies in the overlap. More sherds do not change that, and neither do more assemblages, whether at recorded settlement locations or from the existing collections; below 50 sherds the added collections lower the separation slightly. Finer classes raise it, and with each class split in four the two ranges no longer overlap. The reason lies in what each process changes. A restriction lets every class drift apart across a line, the rare ones included, so finer classes add independent evidence of it (the median between-phase $F_{ST}$ of records made with a restriction rises from 0.0062 to 0.0082). Local innovation changes only the classes it reorders, and its records differ no more at the finer grain (0.0056 and 0.0045).

Three limits apply. The copying model is not recalibrated for finer classes or for more assemblages, so the designs can be compared with one another but not with the record. Finer classes here are a division of the same frequencies, and local innovation leaves the finer divisions of each class in fixed proportions, so part of their advantage is built into the model; real attribute classes need not behave that way. The comparison is also between two settings of the grid, not among all sixteen.

The archaeological record will not supply new assemblages, but the Phillips, Ford, and Griffin table holds collections from the same ground that the analysis does not use (`analyses/98_small_collections.py`, `output/findings/small_collections.md`). In or nearest Phillips's Kent, Parkin, and Walls areas, 56 collections are not analyzed and hold at least one sherd of the ten classes; ten of them lie outside every area and take the nearest, as the analyzed assemblages do. Seven have 50 or more decorated sherds (five Kent, two Walls), 15 have 25 or more (eight Kent, two Parkin, five Walls), and 24 have 10 or more (twelve Kent, four Parkin, eight Walls). Two of them, Owens (141 sherds) and Moore (78), already meet the minimum of 75; they are absent because the analysis matrix was built from a list of named assemblages. None of the 24 lies within 10 km of an analyzed assemblage of another phase, which is part of why adding them does not sharpen the comparison at the phase lines. We count each site once, by its largest collection row, because some rows in the table overlap. Two of the 24, Murdock and Dogwood, lie within 1 km of an analyzed assemblage and may be further collections from the same place.

## The map of compositional change: smoothing scale and sensitivity

We then compare the median rate along the reconstructed phase boundaries with the median along the boundaries of 200 same-size divisions around random centers, each turned into territories the same way, by assigning every point to its nearest assemblage and merging by group (`analyses/75_groupness_surface.py`). Dropping any one cluster leaves the overall shortfall between 3.4- and 9.5-fold; the lowest value comes from dropping the first cluster, which carries about half of the observed five-cluster $F_{ST}$.

Three checks ask whether this is an accident of sampling (`analyses/85_excess_robustness.py`, 300 model runs, 2,000 draws). Redrawing each assemblage from the basin's pooled profile at its own sherd count gives each cluster a contribution near zero, at most 0.0003 at the 97.5th percentile, compared with observed contributions of 0.0028 to 0.0150. Drawing each assemblage's class proportions from Dirichlet(counts + 1/2) and dividing each cluster's contribution by the model's median gives posterior excesses whose 95 percent credible intervals all lie above 1 (the lowest lower bound is 1.9). Dropping each assemblage in turn from the data and from every model run leaves every multi-assemblage cluster between 1.8 and 34.9 times above the model. The shortfall is therefore not a sampling effect, and no single collection causes it. It is also not a matter of which source the counts came from: rows carrying the survey's counts alone and rows carrying Lipo's compilation each fall short of the model when taken separately, by 14- and 5.3-fold, although source and sample size cannot be separated (`analyses/78_source_effect.py`).

## Tests of change along the sequence

The four measures describe the record at points along the sequence, not the changes between them. Two further tests model change directly while allowing for the record's time-averaging, and three shorter checks follow. All of them depend on the seriation order, which follows geography at least as closely as time, so the main text draws on them only to say that the record shows no resolved change along the sequence.

**Copying bias inferred by simulation.** We simulate copying scenarios ranging from strongly conformist through neutral to novelty-seeking, keep those that reproduce the real assemblages, and infer the copying bias from the survivors, with time-averaging built into each simulation [@crema_kandler_shennan_2016; @carrignon_bentley_ruck_2019; @crema_edinborough_kerig_shennan_2014]. The model is frequency-dependent copying among the ten classes in a finite population: a class's adoption weight is proportional to its frequency raised to the power $1+b$, where $b>0$ is conformity, $b<0$ anti-conformity, and $b=0$ neutral copying, with an innovation rate that mixes in a uniform draw from the classes. An assemblage pools production over a window of generations. We placed broad priors on innovation, $b$, population size, and window length, and fitted the model by sequential Monte Carlo approximate Bayesian computation (800 particles, six rounds of shrinking tolerance, local-linear regression adjustment), matching each simulated six-period sequence to the observed diversity trajectory and rank-frequency profile [@toni_etal_2009; @beaumont_zhang_balding_2002]. The posterior of $b$ is concentrated near neutral, with a mean of +0.003 and a 95 percent credible interval of -0.035 to +0.039 that includes zero, and means of +0.001 to +0.004 across five independent runs (Figure S9A). Its standard deviation is 0.017 against a prior of 0.289, so the data strongly constrain it. P(b > 0) is 0.62 in the primary run and 0.53 to 0.63 across runs, so even the direction of any lean is unstable. Fitting the two halves of the order separately recovers no shift between them (-0.000 and +0.012). Simulation-based calibration over 200 datasets drawn from the prior gives ranks consistent with uniform (Kolmogorov-Smirnov D = 0.088, p = 0.086; Figure S9B), a check of the software rather than of the record [@talts_etal_2018], and an independent rejection-sampling fit agrees (mean $b$ = +0.019, 95 percent interval -0.062 to +0.054).

Two caveats apply. The calibration tests also report coverage of the nominal 95 percent interval at nine fixed values of $b$ (Table S9), and coverage falls to 0.65 at $b = +0.10$ and 0.75 at $b = -0.20$ (`output/abc_smc_validation.md`). They used 300 particles and five rounds rather than the empirical fit's 800 and six, and their synthetic data come from the same pooled-population simulator they fit, whereas the real observations are spatially distributed assemblages pooled into six periods. They therefore check the numerical inference under the assumed simulator, not whether that simulator describes how the record formed. We treat the interval as consistent with weak or absent basin-wide conformity, conditional on the pooled model, rather than as a demonstrated exclusion of strong conformity. Because the model pools the whole basin, it also says nothing about localized or group-specific conformity, which the spatial measures and the copying model address.

**Direction and stability of change.** The second test asks whether the assemblages' diversity wanders without direction, heads steadily one way, or is pulled back toward a stable value. The distinctions among an unbiased random walk, a directional walk, stasis, and mean reversion [@hunt_2006; @hunt_bell_travis_2008]. Rather than choose among the four, we fit the model that contains them and report the two parameters that distinguish them, since unbiased motion, stasis, and mean reversion occupy a single region of parameter space. Along the computed seriation order, decorated-class diversity runs from 0.666 at one end through 0.734 to 0.460 at the other, falling over the second half. Because the dates do not establish which end is later, the sign of any directional change is also unclear. The mean-reversion rate has a posterior median of 0.77 (95 percent credible interval 0.05 to 10.7), spanning the unbiased and stasis limits and so unresolved. The directional drift along the order as computed leans negative but is not resolved (median -0.061, interval -0.154 to +0.035, P(drift > 0) = 0.071). A decline of this kind is, in any case, what drift in a finite population produces, as the predictive check below shows. With six points the reversion rate and the drift remain partly confounded, since relaxation toward a lower optimum can imitate directional loss, which is why we report both parameters rather than a winning model (Figure S9C). Diagnostics for the fit: R-hat 1.008, minimum effective sample size 980, 18 divergent transitions of 8,000 (0.23 percent), E-BFMI 0.61. The companion fit meant to show that the design recovers a directional series did not converge (R-hat 1.18, minimum effective sample size 16, 1,079 divergent transitions of 8,000), so the design's ability to resolve directional change is not established, and we give the drift estimate no weight (`output/findings/tempo_mode_posterior.md`).

A third test looks for the early-warning signs of a critical transition, a rise in variance and in the similarity of successive values before an abrupt change. This record gives it little to work with: 28 ordered assemblages and a rolling window of 14. The rolling variance along the sequence shows no consistent trend (Kendall tau = +0.09, and -0.22 to +0.58 across the window and detrending choices we tried) and the lag-1 autocorrelation falls rather than rises (tau = -0.75; -0.77 to -0.57), so neither behaves as a critical transition would (`output/tempo_mode_ews.md`). Because the ordering places similar assemblages next to one another, we do not rely on this test [@scheffer_2009; @dakos_2012].

### Three further checks of neutrality along the sequence

The first follows each decorated class as it rises and falls through the sequence and asks whether those changes look like undirected fluctuation from unbiased copying or the directed change a marker would show. We fit the rescaled frequency increments of all ten classes in one hierarchical model with partial pooling, following the frequency increment construction [@feder_kryazhimskiy_plotkin_2014]. The population-level departure from unbiased copying has a posterior median of +0.008 with a 95 percent credible interval of -0.040 to +0.081, where unbiased copying implies zero, and P(departure > 0) = 0.64. Individual classes are not resolved: a class simulated with a substantial true departure is recovered at about a fifth of its size with an interval spanning zero, so the increments support the aggregate statement but not a per-class one.

The second asks whether a neutral model reproduces the observed diversity trajectory [@kandler_shennan_2013]. We draw population size and innovation rate from the posterior of the copying-bias inference and compare the observed trajectory with the resulting predictive distribution. The observed diversity falls inside the 95 percent predictive interval in all six periods, under the pooled posterior and under each of the five sampler seeds taken separately. The pooled posterior predictive probability is 0.885, ranging from 0.634 to 0.915 across seeds. A neutral model is adequate for this trajectory, which is concordance rather than proof that copying was neutral; the predictive interval is wide because population size is itself uncertain.

The third asks whether the assemblages grow more different from one another toward contact, as fragmenting groups would. Neiman's distance between assemblages shows no resolved trend along the sequence: the posterior median rank correlation between mean within-period distance and position is +0.09, with a 95 percent credible interval of -0.53 to +0.63 over six periods. Distances between the two spatial clusters are only modestly larger than distances within them, a ratio of 1.28, and within-assemblage diversity is weakly and negatively related to an assemblage's mean distance from the others (posterior median -0.14, 95 percent credible interval -0.44 to +0.20), the direction a single shared community produces (Figure S4) [@neiman_1995].

## What recovers the phases, and the problem of zeros

The main text asks whether the site map or the assemblages recover the phase assignments. This section describes how assemblages were clustered on location and composition together, and one choice, the treatment of zero counts, that changes the answer.

Assemblages are clustered in a combined space: site coordinates in kilometers, centered and scaled so that the mean squared pairwise distance is one, joined to decorated-class composition scaled the same way and multiplied by the square root of a weight $w$. At $w = 0$, the representation is the site map alone; as $w$ grows, it becomes assemblage composition alone. We compare each clustering with the phase assignments, taken from Phillips's [-@phillips_1970] phase areas as mapped by Lipo [-@lipo_2001, Figure 2.6], using the adjusted Rand index. We ran three composition transforms, three clustering methods ($k$-means over 40 seeds, each the best of 500 restarts; Ward linkage; and average linkage), and nine weights from the map alone upward, plus composition alone; the full grid is in `output/findings/phase_recovery.csv`. Two of the 28 assemblages lie inside no phase area and are assigned to the nearest area, a geographic rule, so every value quoted in the main text is computed on the 26 assemblages inside a phase area, apart from the sensitivity case below, which scores all 28. The agreement reached by the site map alone depends on the clustering method. It is 0.480 by $k$-means and 0.740 by Ward linkage and by average linkage, whichever composition transform is used, since at $w = 0$ the transform does not enter (`output/findings/phase_recovery.md`). The difference lies in which assemblages each method places with another phase. Ward and average linkage misplace two, Big Eddy and Castile Landing, both in Phillips's Parkin area and both grouped with Kent. $K$-means misplaces those two and four more, Beck, Belle Meade, Commerce, and Hollywood, which it groups with Walls. With composition at half the coordinates' weight and the chi-square transform, all three methods misplace only Big Eddy and Castile Landing. Composition alone misplaces six of the 26 by $k$-means and by Ward linkage and thirteen by average linkage. These lists are from the chi-square transform and, for $k$-means, the first seed.

We also asked of each assemblage which phase its pottery fits best (`analyses/96_assemblage_phase_fit.py`, `output/findings/assemblage_phase_fit.md`). The measure is the chi-square distance from the assemblage's profile to the pooled profile of each phase, with the assemblage left out of its own phase's pool; each profile is drawn 4,000 times from Dirichlet(counts + 1/2), and the posterior probability that an assemblage fits its own phase is the share of draws in which its own phase is the closest. That probability is above 0.75 for 22 of the 28 assemblages and below 0.25 for four: Big Eddy, Holden Lake, and Turnbow, which are closer to Kent, and Belle Meade, which is closer to Parkin. For Castile Landing it is 0.60 and for Mound Place 0.40: Castile Landing is nearly as close to Kent (distance 0.571) as to its own Parkin (0.555), and Mound Place is slightly closer to Kent and to Parkin (0.496 each) than to its own Walls (0.538). Two assemblages lie nearer a member of another phase than any other member of their own: Castile Landing (12 km from the nearest Kent assemblage, 16 km from the nearest Parkin assemblage) and Clay Hill.

As a sensitivity case, we counted Big Eddy and Castile Landing as Kent and reran the recovery and the phase-line comparison. Ward linkage on the site map alone then reproduces the scheme exactly, as does $k$-means on the map and composition together, and $k$-means on the map alone reaches 0.666 against 0.509 (all 28 assemblages scored). The between-phase $F_{ST}$ has a posterior median of 0.0142 (95 percent credible interval 0.0124 to 0.0162), against 0.0123 and 0.0121 for divisions around random centers and compact divisions, and the posterior probability that the phase lines separate the assemblages better is 0.61 and 0.57, against 0.57 and 0.72 with Phillips's assignments. The primary analysis keeps Phillips's assignments.

Class proportions are compositional data, and the centered log-ratio transform is the usual choice. It cannot be applied here without a choice that determines the result. Of the 280 cells in the basin matrix, 105 are zero, and log (0) is undefined. Replacing zeros with a fraction of the smallest positive proportion lets that fraction set how strongly absence counts, and agreement with the phases follows it:

| zero convention | adjusted Rand index |
|---|---|
| pseudocount of 0.5 added to counts | 0.522 |
| floor at 2.0 times the smallest positive proportion | 0.740 |
| floor at 0.5 times the smallest positive proportion | 0.740 |
| floor at 0.1 times the smallest positive proportion | 0.863 |

All four use the same data, geography, and weight ($w = 0.5$); only the zero convention differs, and it moves the answer by about a third of the index's range. We report the chi-square transform in the main text because it needs no such choice, and we use a pseudocount wherever the log-ratio transform appears. The result is also a caution about the phases themselves: a classification sensitive to which types are absent, applied to assemblages whose sherd counts differ more than twentyfold, will read sampling failure as cultural difference.

## Settlement and waterways

The main text reports that late-period sites lie nearer the larger rivers than random points do, and that this does not account for the clumping of settlement. This section gives the measurements (`analyses/81_settlement_and_rivers.py`, `output/findings/settlement_and_rivers.md`). The sites are the 81 with a late pre-contact component in the mapped area, and the comparison is with 6,000 points placed uniformly at random in the same area. The sites' ratio is 0.67.

If nearness to water produced the clumping, those points would be as clumped as the sites.

## Radiocarbon chronology

This section gives the radiocarbon evidence for when the basin was occupied and shows why the seriation order cannot be read as a chronology. We calibrated the Mainfort [-@mainfort_2001] radiocarbon compilation against IntCal20 [@reimer_etal_2020]. Forty determinations come from seven sites in and near the St. Francis basin, nineteen of them from Parkin. The summed probability of the basin dates has a median of AD 1428 (Parkin alone, AD 1483). It places 74 percent of the probability between AD 1241 and AD 1641, spread evenly over those four centuries (18 or 19 percent each), with 16 percent earlier and 10 percent in the century after (Figure S10; `output/chronology_14c.md`). It does not fall at the contact interval. The century after AD 1541 holds 19 percent, the same as the century before; 29 percent of the basin probability postdates AD 1541 and 18 percent postdates AD 1600. These shares are computed on calendar years AD 1000 to 1749, which assumes that no deposit postdates 1750. Calibrated without that bound, 8 percent of the probability falls after AD 1741, the shares after AD 1541 and AD 1600 become 31 and 21 percent, and the centuries before and after AD 1541 remain equal, at 17 percent each. This is consistent with occupation continuing through the De Soto entrada and declining afterward, although the shape of the calibration curve affects the shape of any summed distribution [@surovell_brantingham_2007], and the latest tail is partly a product of determinations near the post-1650 plateau.

To test whether the seriation axis tracks calendar time, we pooled the dates of the four proveniences matching our assemblages (Kent Place, Parkin, Neeley's Ferry, and Clay Hill; 26 dates) and correlated their median calibrated ages with position on the first correspondence-analysis axis. With pooled calibrated medians, the rank correlation is 0.40 in magnitude, with Clay Hill out of order, and it favors orienting the axis the way the analyses do; with one-sigma calibrated midpoints, it is zero. Four points under two reasonable conventions that disagree cannot establish which end of the axis is later.

House [-@house_1993, 23] reports the Kent and Clay Hill determinations with their contexts. At Kent, one date comes from a house floor (Feature 6, SMU-2102, 600 ± 115 BP) and the other from a post intrusive into it (SMU-2103, 310 ± 90 BP), and an archaeomagnetic date on another feature (Feature 1) falls at AD 1345 to 1380. Pooling the intrusive post with the floor makes the Kent Place median (AD 1466) later than the floor itself. Correcting it would not orient the axis either, because Kent Place, then among the earliest dated sites, and Clay Hill, the latest, sit at the same end of the axis. Hollywood, one of the analyzed assemblages but not in the radiocarbon file, has two calibrated dates reported by Haley [-@haley_2014] from Johnson and colleagues' excavations (Johnson et al. 2000:48-49, as cited there): central intercepts of AD 1310, 1355, and 1385 (one-sigma AD 1285 to 1420) for the earliest features, and AD 1405 (1300 to 1435) for the top of Mound A. The uncalibrated ages are not reported, so we have not recalibrated them against IntCal20 or pooled them with the other anchors. Hollywood too lies at the same end of the axis as Kent Place and Clay Hill (ranks 6, 4, and 7 of 28, with Parkin at 21).

The axis follows latitude, with a rank correlation of +0.77 as oriented, and latitude and longitude together account for 63 percent of its variance (`analyses/87_axis_geography.py`). It is largely a south-to-north gradient in composition, which is what the main text's argument would lead one to expect, and it is not a reliable proxy for time. The analyses therefore orient the axis in the direction the medians weakly favor, and report every direction-dependent result both ways.

The basin chronology is stable under an alternative compilation: adding the net-new Mississippian dates from the lower-valley radiocarbon database compiled by Jeffrey Alvey (personal communication, 2022), twelve determinations concentrated at Hazel and Upper Nodena, shifts the summed-probability median by eight years, to AD 1436, and leaves the contact decline in place (26 percent of the probability after AD 1541 and 16 percent after AD 1600). Recent AMS dating of the Parkin platform-mound post, interpreted as the base of the cross the De Soto expedition raised at Casqui, places it at cal AD 1445 to 1650 [@mitchem_2017], consistent with the latest occupation inferred here. The individual determinations, with laboratory numbers, ages, calibrated medians, and sources, are listed in `radiocarbon_dates_used.csv`.
